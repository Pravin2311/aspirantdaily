UPSC Mains Syllabus: GS I to IV, Essay, Optional and Qualifying Papers from the Official Notice

Nine papers • 1,750 written marks • 275-mark interview • GS I to IV topics • Essay • 48 optional choices • Qualifying language papers • Tie-breaking

UPSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

The Civil Services Examination has two stages: the objective Preliminary exam, which screens candidates, and the Main exam, a written test plus an interview that decides final rank. This page sets out the Main exam as printed in the UPSC notice for the Civil Services Examination, 2026. The Preliminary exam that year was held on 24 May 2026 for about 933 vacancies. Candidates had to be at least 21 and under 32 on 1 August 2026, and general candidates get six attempts.

Only a fraction of Prelims candidates reach Mains: the notice says about twelve to thirteen times the number of vacancies are admitted. About twice the number of vacancies are then called for the interview.

All nine papers are conventional essay-type papers, and each lasts three hours. Counting the qualifying papers, a candidate writes 2,350 marks' worth of papers over the Mains, but only 1,750 of those marks reach the merit list. The four GS papers alone are 1,000 marks, more than half the written total.

Except for Papers A and B, candidates may write their answers in English or in any one language of the Eighth Schedule. UPSC may set qualifying marks for any of the papers.

Paper A (an Indian language) and Paper B (English) are of Matriculation standard. Questions cover comprehension of passages, précis writing, usage and vocabulary, and short essays; the Indian language paper adds translation to and from English.

These papers are evaluated alongside the others, but your Essay, GS and optional papers are taken into account only if you score at least 25% in each: 75 of 300. Fail either and the rest of your Mains is not counted, however well you wrote it. Paper A is not compulsory for candidates from Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland and Sikkim, or for hearing-impaired candidates with a benchmark disability who were exempted from second or third language courses.

Candidates may be asked to write essays on several topics. The notice says candidates are expected to keep closely to the subject, arrange their ideas in an orderly fashion and write concisely, and that credit goes to effective and exact expression. There is no topic list: the essay draws on everything else you study.

  • Indian culture: art forms, literature and architecture from ancient to modern times.
  • Modern Indian history from about the middle of the eighteenth century: events, personalities and issues.
  • The freedom struggle, its stages and contributors from different parts of the country; post-independence consolidation and reorganisation.
  • World history from the 18th century: the industrial revolution, world wars, redrawing of boundaries, colonisation and decolonisation, and ideas such as communism, capitalism and socialism.
  • Indian society: diversity, women and women's organisations, population, poverty, urbanisation, globalisation, communalism, regionalism and secularism.
  • Geography: the world's physical geography, distribution of natural resources, location of industries, and phenomena such as earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanoes and cyclones.

Background reading: modern India and the freedom struggle, Indian architecture and sculpture and geomorphology and landforms.

  • The Constitution: historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure.
  • Union and states: federal structure, devolution of powers and finances to local levels, separation of powers, and comparison with other countries' constitutions.
  • Parliament and state legislatures; the executive and judiciary; the Representation of People's Act; constitutional, statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies.
  • Government policies, the development sector (NGOs, SHGs, donors), welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, health, education and human resources, poverty and hunger.
  • Governance: transparency, accountability, e-governance, citizens' charters and the role of civil services in a democracy.
  • International relations: India and its neighbours, bilateral, regional and global groupings, the Indian diaspora and international institutions.

Background reading: federalism and centre-state relations, Parliament of India and constitutional and statutory bodies.

  • The Indian economy: planning, mobilisation of resources, growth, employment, inclusive growth and government budgeting.
  • Agriculture: cropping patterns, irrigation, marketing, farm subsidies and MSP, the Public Distribution System, food security, food processing and land reforms.
  • Liberalisation and industrial policy, infrastructure (energy, ports, roads, airports, railways) and investment models.
  • Science and technology: applications in everyday life, achievements of Indians, IT, space, robotics, nanotechnology, biotechnology and intellectual property rights.
  • Conservation, pollution, environmental impact assessment, and disaster management.
  • Internal security: extremism, external state and non-state actors, cyber security, money laundering, border management and the mandates of security forces.

Background reading: budget and fiscal policy, planning, reforms and agriculture and disaster management in India.

This paper tests attitude and approach to integrity, probity in public life and problem solving in conflicts faced in dealing with society, and questions may use case studies. Its areas are:

  • Ethics and the human interface, and human values from the lives of leaders, reformers and administrators.
  • Attitude, its link with thought and behaviour, social influence and persuasion.
  • Aptitude and foundational values for the civil service: integrity, impartiality, objectivity, empathy and compassion for weaker sections.
  • Emotional intelligence and its use in administration.
  • Contributions of moral thinkers and philosophers from India and the world.
  • Public service values and ethics in public administration, including corporate governance and ethics in international relations.
  • Probity in governance: Right to Information, codes of ethics and conduct, citizens' charters, use of public funds and corruption.
  • Case studies on these issues.

Papers VI and VII are both on one optional subject that you choose. The list has 25 subjects (Agriculture; Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Science; Anthropology; Botany; Chemistry; Civil Engineering; Commerce and Accountancy; Economics; Electrical Engineering; Geography; Geology; History; Law; Management; Mathematics; Mechanical Engineering; Medical Science; Philosophy; Physics; Political Science and International Relations; Psychology; Public Administration; Sociology; Statistics; Zoology) plus the literature of any one of 23 languages, so there are 48 choices in all.

The optional papers are set broadly at honours degree level, above a bachelor's degree and below a master's; for engineering, medical science and law, the level is a bachelor's degree. Each subject's own syllabus is printed in the notice.

The interview board assesses personal suitability for a career in public service. The notice describes it as a purposeful conversation rather than a strict cross-examination, judging qualities such as mental alertness, clear and logical exposition, balance of judgement, leadership and integrity. It carries 275 marks with no minimum qualifying marks.

Final rank is the written total (Papers I to VII) plus the interview marks. If two candidates tie, the one with more marks in the common papers (Essay and GS I to IV) and the interview together ranks higher; if still tied, the one with more marks in the common written papers alone. Services are then allotted by rank and stated preferences. For the earlier stage, see the UPSC Prelims syllabus.

Do the English and Indian language papers count towards rank?

No. Papers A and B are qualifying only. You need 25% in each (75 of 300) for your Essay, GS and optional papers to be considered, but their marks are not added to your total.

How many marks does General Studies carry in Mains?

The four GS papers carry 250 marks each, 1,000 of the 1,750 written marks. With the 250-mark Essay, the common papers come to 1,250, and they also decide ties.
