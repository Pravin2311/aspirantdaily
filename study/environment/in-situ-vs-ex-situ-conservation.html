In-Situ vs Ex-Situ Conservation: Hotspots, Protected Areas and Sacred Groves vs Zoos, Seed Banks and Cryopreservation

In situ = on site: save the forest to save the tiger • Ex situ = off site: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation • 34 hotspots, three cover India

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

NCERT divides biodiversity conservation into two approaches:

In situ (on site)Ex situ (off site)
IdeaProtect the whole ecosystem, so biodiversity at every level is saved: "save the entire forest to save the tiger"Take threatened species out of their habitat into a special setting for protection and care
When usedThe default for conserving ecosystems and the species in themWhen a species is endangered and needs urgent measures to avoid extinction
ExamplesBiodiversity hotspots, biosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, sacred grovesZoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks, seed banks, cryopreservation, in vitro fertilisation, tissue culture
StrengthKeeps species, their interactions and their habitat togetherCan keep species alive even after they are extinct in the wild

Biodiversity hotspots. Many countries cannot afford to protect all their biological wealth, so conservationists picked out regions for maximum protection. These hotspots have very high species richness and high endemism, meaning species found nowhere else. They are also regions of fast habitat loss. Twenty-five were identified first and nine added later, for 34. Together they cover less than 2 per cent of the earth's land.

NCERT names three hotspots covering India's richest regions:

  • Western Ghats and Sri Lanka
  • Indo-Burma
  • Himalaya

Protected areas. India legally protects ecologically unique, biodiversity-rich regions as biosphere reserves, national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. NCERT's figures are 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks and 448 sanctuaries. These are older counts, and official current numbers are higher.

Sacred groves. Many Indian cultures set aside tracts of forest and venerated all the trees and wildlife in them. NCERT lists sacred groves in:

  • the Khasi and Jaintia Hills, Meghalaya, where they are the last refuge of many rare and threatened plants;
  • the Aravalli Hills, Rajasthan;
  • the Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra;
  • the Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar areas, which NCERT places in Madhya Pradesh. Sarguja and Bastar are now in Chhattisgarh, and Chanda (Chandrapur) is in Maharashtra.
  • Zoos, botanical gardens and safari parks: some animals now extinct in the wild survive only in zoological parks.
  • Cryopreservation: gametes of threatened species can be kept viable and fertile for long periods.
  • In vitro fertilisation: eggs can be fertilised outside the body.
  • Tissue culture: plants can be propagated from tissue.
  • Seed banks: seeds of different genetic strains of commercially important plants can be stored for long periods.

Ex situ methods have moved well beyond keeping animals in enclosures. They now conserve genetic material itself.

Three levels of biodiversity (a term popularised by Edward Wilson):

  • Genetic: variation within a species. India has over 50,000 rice strains and 1,000 mango varieties.
  • Species: the Western Ghats have more amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats.
  • Ecological: India's deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows give it more ecosystem diversity than Norway.

India's share: India has 2.4 per cent of the world's land but 8.1 per cent of its species diversity, which makes it one of the 12 mega diversity countries. About 45,000 plant species and twice as many animal species have been recorded.

The Evil Quartet, NCERT's four causes of loss:

  1. Habitat loss and fragmentation, the most important. Tropical rain forests once covered over 14 per cent of the land and now cover no more than 6 per cent.
  2. Over-exploitation, when "need turns to greed". Steller's sea cow and the passenger pigeon were lost this way.
  3. Alien species invasions. The Nile perch in Lake Victoria wiped out over 200 cichlid species. In India, carrot grass (Parthenium), Lantana, water hyacinth and the African catfish threaten native species.
  4. Co-extinctions. When a host species dies out, the species tied to it, such as its parasites, die with it.

Today's extinction rates are estimated at 100 to 1,000 times faster than before humans. NCERT groups the reasons to conserve as narrowly utilitarian (food, fibre, medicines), broadly utilitarian (ecosystem services such as oxygen and pollination) and ethical (every species has intrinsic value).

  • Described so far: IUCN (2004) put the number of described plant and animal species at slightly more than 1.5 million. Robert May's conservative estimate of total global species diversity is about 7 million.
  • Animals dominate: over 70 per cent of recorded species are animals, and insects make up over 70 per cent of those. There are more fungi species than fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals combined.
  • Latitudinal gradient: species diversity generally falls from the equator towards the poles. Colombia has nearly 1,400 bird species, New York 105 and Greenland 56. India, largely tropical, has more than 1,200.
  • Amazon: the Amazonian rain forest has the greatest biodiversity on earth, with more than 40,000 plant species.
  • Stability: David Tilman's experiments found that plots with more species varied less in total biomass from year to year. Paul Ehrlich's "rivet popper" analogy compares species to the rivets holding an aeroplane together.
  • Convention on Biological Diversity, "The Earth Summit", Rio de Janeiro, 1992: called on all nations to conserve biodiversity and use its benefits sustainably.
  • World Summit on Sustainable Development, Johannesburg, 2002: 190 countries pledged a significant cut in the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010.

Q1. Which of these is an example of in-situ conservation? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Sacred grove

💡 Explanation: Sacred groves protect species in their natural habitat, which is in situ. Seed banks, botanical gardens and cryopreservation keep them off site.

Q2. How many biodiversity hotspots does NCERT say cover India's high-biodiversity regions? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Three

💡 Explanation: NCERT names three: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, and Himalaya, out of 34 hotspots worldwide.

Q3. 'The Evil Quartet' refers to: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Four causes of biodiversity loss

💡 Explanation: It is NCERT's name for habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien species invasions and co-extinctions.

Q4. Which is the most important cause of extinction, according to NCERT? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Habitat loss and fragmentation

💡 Explanation: NCERT calls habitat loss and fragmentation the most important cause, with tropical rain forests shrinking from over 14 to about 6 per cent of land.

Q5. The Convention on Biological Diversity, called the Earth Summit, was held in: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Rio de Janeiro, 1992

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the Earth Summit was held in Rio de Janeiro in 1992. The Johannesburg summit of 2002 was its follow-up.

What is the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conservation?

In-situ conservation protects species in their natural habitat by protecting the whole ecosystem. Ex-situ conservation removes threatened species to zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks or gene banks for special care.

Which biodiversity hotspots are in India?

NCERT names three that cover India: the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, and the Himalaya. Some lists add Sundaland, which includes the Nicobar Islands, making four.

What are sacred groves?

Tracts of forest set aside by communities for religious and cultural reasons, where all trees and wildlife are protected. NCERT names groves in Meghalaya, Rajasthan, the Western Ghats and central India.
