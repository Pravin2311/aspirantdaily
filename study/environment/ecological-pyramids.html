Ecological Pyramids: Pyramids of Number, Biomass and Energy, When They Invert, and the 10 Per Cent Law

Base = producers, apex = top consumers • Biomass pyramid inverted in the sea • Energy pyramid always upright • 10 per cent law limits trophic levels • GPP − R = NPP

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Organisms in an ecosystem sit at trophic levels according to how they get food. NCERT puts producers at the first level, herbivores (primary consumers) at the second and carnivores (secondary consumers) at the third. Plot any quantity across these levels, and the shape is usually a pyramid: broad at the base, narrow at the top.

NCERT describes three ecological pyramids, each expressing the feeding relationship in a different unit:

NumberCount of organisms at each levelUpright (grassland: about 6 million plants support 3 top carnivores)Many insects feeding on one big tree, posed as a question
BiomassLiving mass (standing crop), best as dry weightUpright on landInverted in the sea
EnergyEnergy at each level per unit area over timeAlways uprightNone: it can never be inverted

In every pyramid the base is the producers and the apex is the tertiary or top consumer. Any count must include all organisms at a level, not a sample of a few individuals.

On land, producers outweigh herbivores, which outweigh carnivores. In the sea, NCERT says the pyramid of biomass is generally inverted because the biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton. Its Figure 12.4 (c) shows the same idea one level down: a small standing crop of phytoplankton supporting a large standing crop of zooplankton.

How can less food support more consumers? The pyramid of biomass shows the standing crop, the mass present at one moment, not the rate of production. Phytoplankton are small and short-lived but reproduce quickly, so a small standing stock can feed a larger mass of consumers over time. NCERT leaves this as a "paradox" for students to explain, which is why it is a favourite exam question.

The pyramid of energy is always upright. When energy passes from one trophic level to the next, some is always lost as heat. A higher level can never hold more energy than the level feeding it.

  • Sunlight to plants: less than 50 per cent of incoming solar radiation is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Plants capture only 2-10 per cent of the PAR, and NCERT's ideal energy pyramid shows producers turning only about 1 per cent of available sunlight into NPP.
  • 10 per cent law: only about 10 per cent of the energy at one level reaches the next. That is why a grazing food chain rarely goes beyond producer, herbivore, primary carnivore and secondary carnivore.
  • One-way flow: energy flows in one direction, from the sun to producers to consumers. Ecosystems need a constant supply of it, in line with the second law of thermodynamics.

Worked example: if producers fix 10,000 J, herbivores get about 1,000 J, primary carnivores about 100 J and secondary carnivores about 10 J.

  1. They do not count a species that occupies two or more trophic levels. A sparrow, for example, is a primary consumer when it eats seeds and a secondary consumer when it eats insects.
  2. They assume a simple food chain, which almost never exists in nature, and cannot show a food web.
  3. Saprophytes (decomposers) get no place, even though they play a vital role in the ecosystem.

An ecosystem's physical structure comes from the interaction of its biotic and abiotic parts. Two structural features matter most: species composition (which plants and animals are present) and stratification, the vertical layering of species. In a forest, trees form the top layer, shrubs the second, and herbs and grasses the bottom.

NCERT uses a pond as a simple, nearly self-sustaining example. Its abiotic part is the water, with dissolved substances and bottom soil. Its producers are phytoplankton, algae and floating, submerged and marginal plants. Its consumers are zooplankton and free-swimming and bottom-dwelling animals. Its decomposers are fungi, bacteria and flagellates, most abundant at the bottom.

Decomposition turns detritus (dead leaves, bark, flowers, animal remains and faeces) into carbon dioxide, water and nutrients. Its steps are fragmentation by detritivores such as earthworms, leaching of soluble nutrients into the soil, catabolism by bacterial and fungal enzymes, humification into dark, slow-decaying humus, and mineralisation, which releases inorganic nutrients. Decomposition needs oxygen. It is faster in warm, moist conditions and for nitrogen-rich detritus, and slower for detritus rich in lignin and chitin.

The two chains are linked. Some DFC organisms are prey for GFC animals, and omnivores such as cockroaches and crows feed on both, turning chains into food webs.

Productivity terms:

  • Gross primary productivity (GPP): the rate of production of organic matter in photosynthesis.
  • Net primary productivity (NPP): GPP minus respiration losses, GPP − R = NPP. It is the biomass available to herbivores and decomposers.
  • Secondary productivity: the rate at which consumers form new organic matter.
  • Global figures: the biosphere's annual NPP is about 170 billion tons of dry organic matter. The oceans cover about 70 per cent of the surface but produce only 55 billion tons of it.

Q1. Which ecological pyramid can never be inverted? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Pyramid of energy

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the energy pyramid is always upright because some energy is lost as heat at every step to the next trophic level.

Q2. The pyramid of biomass in the sea is generally: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Inverted

💡 Explanation: NCERT says it is generally inverted because the biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton.

Q3. According to the 10 per cent law, if producers fix 10,000 J, how much reaches the primary carnivores? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 100 J

💡 Explanation: About 10 per cent passes at each step: 10,000 J to producers, 1,000 J to herbivores and 100 J to primary carnivores.

Q4. Which is a limitation of ecological pyramids, as NCERT notes? medium

✅ Answer: (b) They give saprophytes no place

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists three limits: species at more than one trophic level are not counted, food webs are not accommodated, and saprophytes get no place.

Q5. Net primary productivity equals: easy

✅ Answer: (d) GPP minus respiration

💡 Explanation: NCERT defines NPP as gross primary productivity minus respiration losses, GPP − R = NPP. NPP is what is available to heterotrophs.

What are the three types of ecological pyramids?

The pyramid of numbers, the pyramid of biomass and the pyramid of energy. Each has producers at the base and the top consumer at the apex, and shows how numbers, living mass or energy change across trophic levels.

Why is the pyramid of energy always upright?

Because each transfer from one trophic level to the next loses some energy as heat. Higher levels can therefore never hold more energy than the level below them.

When is the pyramid of numbers inverted?

When one large producer supports many consumers, as with a big tree feeding many insects. NCERT poses this tree-and-insects case as a question for students to work out; it does not state the answer.
