Fact, Inference and Judgement: Telling Them Apart

Three definitions • Signal words and their limits • A worked passage • Answering by elimination • Traps

Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

You read a short passage and then classify each of several statements as a fact (F), an inference (I) or a judgement (J). Options often list combinations, for example "A–F, B–I, C–J", and only one matches. The question is not whether a statement is true but what kind of statement it is.

Each type has a clear definition. Work from the definitions, not from a feeling about how certain the statement sounds.

Fact. A statement of information that can be checked against evidence: figures, dates, events, what someone said, what a report found. A fact may come from the passage or be a matter of record. It contains no conclusion beyond what is given and no opinion. A restatement of given figures in another form, such as a percentage worked out exactly from two stated numbers, adds nothing uncertain and is treated as a fact.

Inference. A conclusion drawn from facts about something not directly stated. It goes beyond the information given, so it might turn out to be wrong, but it is reasoned from evidence. Statements about causes ("the lights helped reduce theft"), likely feelings, or likely future results are typical inferences.

Judgement. An opinion: approval or disapproval, a recommendation, or an evaluation of what is good, fair, wise or best. Evidence alone cannot settle a judgement, because two reasonable people can weigh the same facts differently.

A quick test: ask "how would I check this?" If you could check it now in a record, it is a fact. If you would need more evidence that may not exist yet, it is an inference. If no evidence could settle it, it is a judgement.

TypeCommon signalsExample
FactFigures, dates, "reported", "according to", past eventsThefts fell from 50 to 35.
Inference"probably", "likely", "so", "this suggests", "as a result"The lights probably reduced thefts.
Judgement"should", "must", "ought", "best", "unfair", "wasteful"The council should light every lane.

Signal words speed you up, but they are not rules. "The report says the council should add lights" contains "should", yet it is a fact about what the report says, which you can check. And an inference may contain no signal word at all, as in "Residents now feel safer." Always confirm with the definitions.

This passage is invented for practice; Saranpur is a fictional town.

Practice passage

In 2025, Saranpur's municipal council installed 120 new streetlights on the town's main roads, taking the total on those roads to 480. Reported night-time thefts on the main roads fell from 50 in 2024 to 35 in 2025. Residents' groups have praised the council's decision.

Statement A: 'The main roads had 360 streetlights before the new ones were installed.'

480 − 120 = 360. It follows exactly from the given figures with nothing assumed: fact.

Statement B: 'Reported night-time thefts on the main roads fell by 30%.'

The fall is 15 on a base of 50, so (50 − 35) ÷ 50 × 100 = 30%. An exact restatement of given figures: fact.

Statement C: 'The new streetlights reduced thefts on the main roads.'

Thefts fell after the lights went up, but the passage does not say the lights caused the fall. Police patrols or other changes could also explain it. A conclusion about a cause: inference.

Statement D: 'The council has made an excellent decision.'

"Excellent" is an evaluation. Even the residents' praise is evidence of opinion, not of excellence: judgement.

Statement E: 'The number of streetlights on the main roads rose by about a third.'

120 new lights on a base of 360 is 33.3%, about a third. Worked exactly from given figures: fact.

Notice that A, B and E are all calculations from the passage. Each one is certain: the 15 fewer thefts and the 360 earlier lights follow with no assumption, which is why they are facts rather than inferences.

With three statements and three labels each, there are 3³ = 27 possible F/I/J patterns, but the question gives only four options. You rarely need to classify every statement.

  1. Classify the easiest statement first, usually the clearest judgement (a "should" or "best" with no "the report says") or a bare figure.
  2. Strike out every option that disagrees on it.
  3. Classify the statement on which the remaining options differ.

Two well-chosen classifications usually leave one option. Keep the hardest call, often inference against fact, for last, and settle it with the "how would I check this?" test.

  • ✘ Calling a confident-sounding conclusion a fact. Certainty of tone is not evidence.
  • ✘ Marking every sentence with "should" as a judgement, even when it reports what someone else said.
  • ✘ Calling a statement about causes a fact because the timing fits.
  • ✘ Calling a judgement an inference because it mentions real figures. One evaluation word makes the whole statement a judgement.

Deciding what a passage supports is also the core of inferences from a passage, cause and effect and statement and conclusions.

Q1. Classify: 'The council should install streetlights in every lane of the town.' easy

✅ Answer: (c) Judgement

💡 Explanation: The statement recommends what ought to be done. It cannot be proved true or false, and it expresses the speaker's view, so it is a judgement.

Q2. Classify: 'Reported night-time thefts on the main roads fell from 50 in 2024 to 35 in 2025.' (Assume the passage states these figures.) easy

✅ Answer: (a) Fact

💡 Explanation: It reports figures that can be checked against police records. Nothing in it is a conclusion or an opinion, so it is a fact.

Q3. Classify: 'The new streetlights probably helped bring the theft numbers down.' easy

✅ Answer: (b) Inference

💡 Explanation: It draws a conclusion about a cause from two facts, more lights and fewer thefts. It is not proved and it is not an opinion about right or wrong, so it is an inference.

Q4. A passage says a school's enrolment rose from 200 to 240. Classify: 'Enrolment at the school rose by 20%.' medium

✅ Answer: (d) Fact

💡 Explanation: The rise is 40 on a base of 200, which is exactly 20%. It restates the given figures in another form with no assumption or opinion added, so it is treated as a fact.

Q5. Classify: 'Spending ₹2 crore on streetlights was the best use of the town's money.' medium

✅ Answer: (c) Judgement

💡 Explanation: 'Best use' is an evaluation that people can reasonably disagree about. Even though the amount may be a fact, the statement as a whole is a judgement.

Q6. Classify: 'Residents of Saranpur now feel safer walking at night.' (The passage reports that thefts fell and residents' groups praised the council.) hard

✅ Answer: (d) Inference

💡 Explanation: The passage does not report how residents feel. The statement reasons from fewer thefts and public praise to a likely feeling, so it is an inference, not a fact.

Is a statement with 'should' always a judgement?

Usually, but read the whole sentence. 'The report says the council should add streetlights' is a fact about what the report says, because you can check the report. Signal words are hints that speed you up; the definitions decide the answer.

How is an inference different from a judgement?

An inference is a reasoned guess about what is or was true, and it could in principle be checked later. A judgement is an opinion about what is good, bad or ought to be done, and evidence alone cannot settle it.
