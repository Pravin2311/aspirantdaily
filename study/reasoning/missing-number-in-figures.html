Missing Number in Figures: Row, Column, Centre and Opposite-Segment Patterns with a Checking Order

Rows first, then columns • Constant sums • Product and square rules • Centre of a triangle or square • Opposite segments of a circle • Always test every set

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Missing-number figures look varied, triangles, circles, grids and stars, but nearly all of them hide one rule applied to several sets of numbers. Finding the set is half the work. Test in this order:

  1. Rows of a grid, left to right.
  2. Columns, top to bottom.
  3. Corners and centre of a triangle or square: the centre is usually built from the corners.
  4. Opposite segments of a circle or star.
  5. Diagonals, which are rarer.

For each set, try operations from simple to complex: sum, difference, product, squares, then a mixture. A rule only counts if it works for every complete set in the figure.

The most common grid rules are "the third number comes from the first two" and "every row or column has the same total".

Worked example 1: third from first two

Rows: (3, 4, 11), (5, 2, 9), (6, 3, ?). Sums (7, 7) do not match the third numbers, but products minus one do: 3 × 4 − 1 = 11 and 5 × 2 − 1 = 9. So ? = 6 × 3 − 1 = 17.

Verify on every row

Before answering, confirm the rule on both complete rows. Both fit, so the rule is safe to apply.

Worked example 2: constant column total

A 3 × 3 grid has columns (9, 4, 7), (6, 8, 6) and (7, ?, 5). The first two columns add to 20, so the third must too: ? = 20 − 7 − 5 = 8.

If row totals differ but grow steadily, the rule may be an increasing total rather than a fixed one.

Worked example 3: totals that grow by 3

Rows: (4, 5, 6), (7, 2, 9) and (8, 6, ?). The first row adds to 15 and the second to 18. Neither products nor a fixed total fit, but the totals rise by 3, so the third row should total 21. Then ? = 21 − 8 − 6 = 7. When the totals change, always look at the gap between them: a steady step of 2, 3 or 5 is common, and so is doubling.

Grids sometimes combine both directions, with one rule for rows and a different one for columns. Use whichever rule involves the row or column that contains the missing number, and use the other direction only to double-check.

When numbers sit at the corners with one in the middle, the middle number is almost always made from the corners.

Worked example 4: twice the corner sum

Triangles: corners (2, 3, 4) with centre 18, corners (1, 5, 6) with centre 24, and corners (3, 3, 7) with centre ?. Sums are 9 and 12, and each centre is double: 18 and 24. So ? = 13 × 2 = 26.

Other common centre rules are the product of two corners minus the third, the sum of squares of the corners, and the product minus the sum.

Circles split into segments, and stars with numbers on their points, often pair each number with the one directly opposite.

Worked example 5: constant product

Opposite pairs: (2, 18), (4, 9) and (3, ?). The products are 36 and 36, so ? = 36 ÷ 3 = 12. Sums (20, 13) do not match, which is why the product is the rule.

When numbers come in pairs that map to a third number, squares and cubes are common.

Worked example 6: sum of squares

(2, 3) → 13, (1, 4) → 17, (5, 2) → ?. 4 + 9 = 13 and 1 + 16 = 17, so ? = 25 + 4 = 29.

  • Stopping at a rule that fits one set. Check every complete row, column or segment pair.
  • Ignoring the direction. Some rules read bottom to top or clockwise; if left to right fails, try the reverse.
  • Overcomplicating. Try plain sums and products before elaborate formulas.
  • Mixing rows and columns. Once you have found a working rule, use the same direction for the missing set.
  • Skipping squares. Numbers like 13, 25, 29 and 45 often hint at squares being added or subtracted.

For number patterns in a line rather than a figure, see number series, and for pairs of related numbers see number analogy.

Q1. Rows of a matrix read: (4, 5, 21), (6, 3, 19), (7, 2, ?). What is the missing number? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 15

💡 Explanation: In each row, third = first × second + 1: 4 × 5 + 1 = 21 and 6 × 3 + 1 = 19. So 7 × 2 + 1 = 15.

Q2. Every column of a grid adds up to 25. One column reads 6, 11, ?. What is the missing number? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 8

💡 Explanation: 6 + 11 + ? = 25, so the missing number is 8.

Q3. Three triangles have corners and centres: (2, 3, 4) → 15, (1, 5, 6) → 18, (3, 2, 5) → ?. What goes in the last centre? hard

✅ Answer: (d) 20

💡 Explanation: Centre = product of corners − sum of corners: 24 − 9 = 15 and 30 − 12 = 18. So 30 − 10 = 20.

Q4. A circle is cut into six segments, and opposite segments add up to the same total. The pairs are 7 and 8, 9 and 6, and ? and 4. What is the missing number? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 11

💡 Explanation: Opposite pairs sum to 15 (7 + 8, 9 + 6), so ? = 15 − 4 = 11.

Q5. Pairs map to numbers: (5, 3) → 16, (7, 2) → 45, (6, 4) → ?. What is the missing number? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 20

💡 Explanation: The rule is a² − b²: 25 − 9 = 16 and 49 − 4 = 45. So 36 − 16 = 20.

How do I know whether the pattern runs along rows or columns?

Try rows first, because most setters use them. If no simple rule fits all complete rows, try columns, then diagonals. The right rule must work for every complete row or column, not just one.

What if two different rules fit?

Test each against every complete set in the figure. Usually only one survives. If both survive and give different answers, check which answer appears among the options.

Which operations should I try first?

Sum, then difference, then product, then squares and cubes, then a mix such as a × b + c. Simple rules are far more common than elaborate ones.
