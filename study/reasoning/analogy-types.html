Analogy Reasoning Tricks: Word, Number and Letter Analogies

Name the relationship first • Word analogy types • Number rules: squares, cubes, n² ± 1 • Letter shifts and opposite pairs • Breaking ties with the options

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-23

An analogy question gives one pair with a hidden relationship and asks you to complete a second pair with the same relationship. The fastest method is to put the first relationship into a short sentence before looking at the options: "a pen is the tool a writer uses", or "the second number is the cube of the first". Then test that sentence on the second pair. If it fits only one option, you are done. If it fits several, make the sentence more precise.

The questions come in three families: word analogies, number analogies and letter analogies. Each has a small set of relationships that appears again and again, so learning them turns most questions into recognition rather than search.

These are the relationships seen most often in SSC and Railway papers. Keep the direction in mind: tool to worker is not the same as worker to tool.

When an option seems loosely related, check whether it fits the exact relationship. For "Pen : Writer :: Scalpel : ?", a hospital is related to a scalpel, but the relationship is tool to worker, and only "surgeon" is the worker who uses a scalpel.

Number pairs are almost always linked by a power or a small change to a power. Check these in order: square, cube, square plus or minus one, cube plus or minus one, then simple multiplication.

Worked example 1: a cube

3 : 27 :: 5 : ? Since 27 = 3³, the answer is 5³ = 125.

Worked example 2: square plus one

4 : 17 :: 6 : ? Since 17 = 4² + 1, the answer is 6² + 1 = 37.

Worked example 3: square minus one

6 : 35 :: 8 : ? Since 35 = 6² − 1, the answer is 8² − 1 = 63.

Sometimes two rules fit the first pair, and the options decide which one the question setter meant.

Worked example 4: two rules that both fit

16 : 64 :: 25 : ? One reading is "multiply by 4", which gives 100. Another is "16 = 4² and 64 = 4³, so square becomes cube", which gives 5³ = 125. If only one of 100 and 125 is among the options, that is the answer. If both are, the square-to-cube rule is the more typical design, but read the other pairs in the question for a clue.

The multiply-by-4 reading gives 25 × 4 = 100, which is why both numbers often appear as options together.

Letter analogies use the same tools as coding-decoding with letter shifts: letter positions (A = 1 to Z = 26), forward or backward shifts, opposite letters and words spelt backwards. Write the positions above the letters before looking for a pattern.

Worked example 5: a fixed shift

ACE : BDF :: GIK : ? Each letter moves one place forward, so GIK becomes HJL.

Opposite letters are pairs whose positions add to 27: A and Z, B and Y, C and X, and so on. The quick memory aid is that M and N sit in the middle as a pair.

Worked example 6: opposite-letter pairs

AZ : BY :: CX : ? Each pair is a letter and its opposite, and the first letter moves forward by one. After C comes D, whose opposite is W, so the answer is DW.

Worked example 7: a whole word of opposites

BAT : YZG :: DOG : ? Each letter of BAT is replaced by its opposite: B by Y, A by Z, T by G. Doing the same to DOG gives D → W, O → L and G → T, so the answer is WLT.

  1. Say the relationship of the first pair in one short sentence, including its direction.
  2. For numbers, test squares and cubes first, then plus or minus one.
  3. For letters, write the positions and check shifts and opposite pairs.
  4. Apply the rule to the second pair and look for the result among the options.
  5. If more than one option fits, sharpen the sentence until only one does.
  • ✘ Choosing a word that is related but in a different way, such as a place instead of a worker.
  • ✘ Reversing the direction of the relationship on the second side.
  • ✘ Stopping at the cube when the rule is the cube plus one.
  • ✘ Counting letter positions from memory instead of writing them down.

Q1. Pen : Writer :: Scalpel : ? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Surgeon

💡 Explanation: A pen is the tool a writer works with. In the same way, a scalpel is the tool a surgeon works with, so the relationship is tool and worker.

Q2. Thermometer : Temperature :: Barometer : ? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Pressure

💡 Explanation: A thermometer measures temperature, and a barometer measures atmospheric pressure. The relationship is instrument and the quantity it measures.

Q3. 5 : 126 :: 7 : ? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 344

💡 Explanation: 5³ = 125 and 125 + 1 = 126, so the rule is n³ + 1. For 7 this gives 343 + 1 = 344. The option 343 is the trap for stopping at the cube.

Q4. BDF : CEG :: MOQ : ? easy

✅ Answer: (c) NPR

💡 Explanation: Each letter of BDF moves one place forward to give CEG. Moving each letter of MOQ one place forward gives NPR.

Q5. FU : GT :: KP : ? medium

✅ Answer: (b) LO

💡 Explanation: F and U are opposite letters, since their positions 6 and 21 add to 27. G and T are the next opposite pair. After K and P, the next pair is L and O, whose positions 12 and 15 also add to 27.

Q6. 121 : 12 :: 169 : ? hard

✅ Answer: (d) 14

💡 Explanation: The square root of 121 is 11, and 11 + 1 = 12. Applying the same rule, the square root of 169 is 13, and 13 + 1 = 14.

What if two different rules fit the given pair?

Apply both rules to the second term and compare the results with the options. Usually only one result appears among the options. If both appear, prefer the simpler, more common rule, such as a square or cube, over an unusual combination of operations.

Does the order of the words in a word analogy matter?

Yes. Pen : Writer is tool to worker, so the answer must also be tool to worker, not worker to tool. Reversing the order on one side is a common way options are made to look correct when they are not.
