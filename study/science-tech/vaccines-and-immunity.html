Immunity and Vaccines: Innate vs Acquired, Active vs Passive, Antibodies, Allergy and the Lymphoid Organs

Innate: physical, physiological, cellular, cytokine barriers • Acquired: B cells (humoral) and T cells (CMI) • Antibody H2L2 • Colostrum IgA • Allergy IgE • Bone marrow and thymus

SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

We meet huge numbers of microbes every day, but few of them make us ill. NCERT defines immunity as the body's overall ability, given by the immune system, to fight disease-causing organisms. It comes in two types.

The four innate barriers:

  1. Physical: skin, the main barrier, and the mucus lining of the respiratory, digestive and urogenital tracts.
  2. Physiological: stomach acid, saliva and tears.
  3. Cellular: white blood cells that engulf and destroy microbes. These are neutrophils (PMNL), monocytes and natural killer cells in the blood, and macrophages in tissues.
  4. Cytokine: virus-infected cells release interferons, which protect nearby cells from infection.

On first meeting a pathogen, the body mounts a weak primary response. On a later encounter it mounts a much stronger secondary, or anamnestic, response, because it "remembers" the first one. Two kinds of lymphocyte do this work:

  • B-lymphocytes release proteins called antibodies into the blood. Because the antibodies are in the blood, this is the humoral (antibody-mediated) response.
  • T-lymphocytes do not secrete antibodies, but they help B cells make them. They also carry out cell-mediated immunity (CMI).

Antibody structure: each antibody has 4 peptide chains, two light (L) and two heavy (H), so it is written H2L2. The types include IgA, IgM, IgE and IgG.

Why transplants need matching: the body can tell "self" from "non-self", and CMI rejects grafts from a non-matching source. Tissue matching and blood group matching are needed first, and the patient takes immunosuppressants for life.

This explains why a mother's first milk matters. Colostrum, the yellowish fluid secreted in the first days, is rich in IgA antibodies that protect the newborn.

Vaccination uses the immune system's memory. A vaccine introduces the pathogen's antigenic proteins, or the pathogen itself in a killed or weakened form. The body makes antibodies against these, and it also forms memory B and T cells. If the real pathogen arrives later, these cells recognise it quickly and produce antibodies on a massive scale.

When a quick response is needed, as with tetanus or snakebite, there is no time to wait for the body's own response. Doctors then inject preformed antibodies or antitoxin, which is passive immunisation.

Recombinant vaccines: recombinant DNA technology lets a pathogen's antigen be produced in bacteria or yeast on a large scale. The hepatitis B vaccine made in yeast is NCERT's example.

Allergy is an exaggerated immune response to harmless substances called allergens, such as dust mites, pollen and animal dander. The antibodies involved are IgE. Mast cells release histamine and serotonin, causing sneezing, watery eyes, a runny nose and difficulty breathing. Anti-histamines, adrenalin and steroids quickly reduce the symptoms. NCERT notes that more children in Indian metro cities now have allergies and asthma, possibly because of a too-protected environment early in life.

Autoimmunity: sometimes, for genetic or unknown reasons, the body attacks its own cells. Rheumatoid arthritis is NCERT's example of an autoimmune disease.

  • Humoral vs cell-mediated: humoral immunity uses antibodies from B cells in the blood. Cell-mediated immunity is carried out by T cells and is behind graft rejection.
  • IgA vs IgE: IgA is the antibody in colostrum that protects the newborn. IgE is the antibody of allergy.
  • Vaccine vs antitoxin: a vaccine makes your body produce its own antibodies and memory cells, which is active immunity. An antitoxin supplies ready-made antibodies for an emergency such as tetanus or snakebite, which is passive immunity.
  • Primary vs secondary response: the first exposure gives a weak response. A repeat exposure gives a strong, fast, anamnestic response, which is why vaccines and boosters work.
  • Primary vs secondary lymphoid organs: lymphocytes mature in bone marrow and thymus. They meet antigens in the spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches and appendix.
  • Innate cells: natural killer cells are a type of lymphocyte, but they belong to innate immunity's cellular barrier, not to acquired immunity.

Q1. The antibodies in colostrum that protect a newborn are mainly: easy

✅ Answer: (c) IgA

💡 Explanation: NCERT says colostrum has abundant IgA antibodies. This is an example of passive immunity.

Q2. Interferons belong to which barrier of innate immunity? medium

✅ Answer: (a) Cytokine barrier

💡 Explanation: Virus-infected cells secrete interferons, which protect non-infected cells. NCERT lists them as the cytokine barrier.

Q3. An anti-tetanus injection of preformed antibodies gives: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Passive immunity

💡 Explanation: Ready-made antibodies or antitoxin given directly are passive immunisation. The body does not make them itself.

Q4. Graft rejection after an organ transplant is due to: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Cell-mediated immunity

💡 Explanation: NCERT states that the cell-mediated immune response, carried out by T-lymphocytes, is responsible for graft rejection.

Q5. Which is a primary lymphoid organ? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Thymus

💡 Explanation: Bone marrow and thymus are the primary lymphoid organs. Spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches and appendix are secondary.

What is the difference between active and passive immunity?

In active immunity your own body makes antibodies after infection or vaccination; it is slow to start but lasting. In passive immunity ready-made antibodies are given, as in colostrum or antitoxin, so protection is quick.

Why does a booster dose work better than the first dose?

Acquired immunity has memory. The first exposure gives a weak primary response, while a later exposure triggers a much stronger secondary, or anamnestic, response from memory cells.

Which antibody is linked to allergy?

IgE. Allergens such as dust mites and pollen trigger IgE, and mast cells then release histamine and serotonin, which cause sneezing, watery eyes and breathing trouble.
