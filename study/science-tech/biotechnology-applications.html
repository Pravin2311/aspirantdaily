Applications of Biotechnology: Bt Cotton, RNAi, Insulin, Gene Therapy, Transgenic Animals and Biopiracy

Agriculture: micropropagation, Bt crops, RNAi • Medicine: insulin from E. coli (1983), first gene therapy (1990), PCR and ELISA • Transgenic animals • GEAC, Basmati and biopiracy

UPSC SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

NCERT describes biotechnology as the industrial-scale production of biopharmaceuticals and biologicals using genetically modified microbes, fungi, plants and animals. Its applications include therapeutics, diagnostics, genetically modified crops, processed food, bioremediation, waste treatment and energy production. The Class XII chapter focuses on two areas that matter most to human life: food production and health.

NCERT names three critical research areas: finding the best catalyst (an improved organism, usually a microbe, or a pure enzyme), engineering the best conditions for that catalyst to work, and downstream processing to purify the protein or organic compound. For the tools behind all this (restriction enzymes, vectors, cloning), see the previous chapter; for India's current GM policy, CRISPR and other emerging technology, see biotechnology, nuclear and emerging technology.

NCERT frames three options for raising food output: agro-chemical farming, organic farming and genetically engineered crops. The Green Revolution tripled the food supply, but agrochemicals are often too costly for farmers in the developing world, and conventional breeding could not keep pace with demand. Tissue culture was developed to fill that gap.

  • Totipotency: scientists learnt in the 1950s that a whole plant can be regenerated from an explant (any part of a plant grown in a test tube under sterile conditions). The nutrient medium must supply a carbon source such as sucrose, inorganic salts, vitamins, amino acids and growth regulators like auxins and cytokinins.
  • Micro-propagation: producing thousands of plants this way in a short time. The plants are genetically identical to the parent, so they are called somaclones. Tomato, banana and apple have been produced commercially by this method.
  • Virus-free plants: even in an infected plant, the apical and axillary meristem is free of virus. Culturing it in vitro gives healthy plants; this has been done for banana, sugarcane and potato.
  • Somatic hybridisation: cell walls are digested to get naked protoplasts, and protoplasts from two varieties are fused into a hybrid. Tomato and potato gave the pomato, which did not have the right mix of traits to be commercially useful.

Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are plants, bacteria, fungi or animals whose genes have been altered. NCERT credits GM crops with five gains: tolerance of abiotic stress (cold, drought, salt, heat), less reliance on chemical pesticides, lower post-harvest losses, more efficient mineral use (which slows soil exhaustion) and better nutrition, as in golden rice, which is enriched with vitamin A. GM plants can also supply industry with starches, fuels and pharmaceuticals.

Bt crops. The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis makes protein crystals that kill certain insects: lepidopterans (tobacco budworm, armyworm), coleopterans (beetles) and dipterans (flies, mosquitoes). The toxin does not harm the bacterium because it is stored as an inactive protoxin. In an insect's alkaline gut the crystals dissolve and the toxin becomes active. It binds to midgut cells and punches pores in them, so they swell and burst and the insect dies. Toxin genes are called cry genes and are chosen by pest: cryIAc and cryIIAb proteins control cotton bollworms, and cryIAb controls corn borer. Besides Bt cotton, NCERT lists Bt corn, rice, tomato, potato and soyabean.

RNA interference. The nematode Meloidogyne incognita attacks tobacco roots and cuts yields sharply. RNAi, a natural defence in all eukaryotes, silences a specific mRNA when a complementary double-stranded RNA binds it. Scientists used Agrobacterium vectors to put nematode-specific genes into tobacco so that the plant made both sense and antisense RNA. The two strands paired into dsRNA, silenced the nematode's mRNA, and the parasite could not survive in the transgenic plant.

Recombinant DNA technology allows mass production of safer, more effective drugs that do not provoke the immune reactions seen with products taken from non-human sources. NCERT gives about 30 recombinant therapeutics approved worldwide, 12 of them marketed in India.

ApplicationWhat NCERT says
Human insulinInsulin was once extracted from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, which caused allergies in some patients. Insulin has two chains, A and B, joined by disulphide bridges; it is made as pro-insulin with an extra C peptide that is removed. In 1983 Eli Lilly put DNA for chains A and B into plasmids of E. coli, made the chains separately and joined them.
Gene therapyInserting a normal gene into cells to make up for a faulty one. The first clinical case (1990) was a 4-year-old girl with ADA deficiency. Her lymphocytes were grown outside the body, given a functional ADA cDNA through a retroviral vector and returned. Because these cells are not immortal, periodic infusions are needed; introducing the gene at an early embryonic stage could be a permanent cure.
Molecular diagnosisPCR amplifies a pathogen's nucleic acid, so very low levels can be detected before symptoms appear. It is routinely used to detect HIV and to find gene mutations in suspected cancer patients. Radioactive probes with autoradiography reveal mutated genes. ELISA works on antigen-antibody interaction.

Transgenic animals carry and express a foreign gene. Rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish have been made, but over 95 per cent are mice. NCERT lists five uses:

  1. Normal physiology and development: for example, studying insulin-like growth factor.
  2. Disease models: for cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's.
  3. Biological products: human α-1-antitrypsin to treat emphysema. In 1997 the first transgenic cow, Rosie, gave milk with human alpha-lactalbumin (2.4 g per litre), better balanced for human babies than ordinary cow milk.
  4. Vaccine safety: transgenic mice are used to test polio vaccine and could replace monkeys.
  5. Chemical safety testing: animals made more sensitive to toxins give faster toxicity results.

Because GM organisms released into ecosystems can behave unpredictably, India set up the Genetic Engineering Approval Committee (GEAC) to decide on the validity of GM research and the safety of releasing GM organisms for public use.

Biopiracy is the use of bio-resources by multinational companies and others without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned and without paying them. NCERT's main example is Basmati: India has an estimated 200,000 rice varieties and 27 documented Basmati varieties, yet in 1997 an American company won US patent rights on a "new" Basmati that had been bred from Indian farmers' varieties crossed with semi-dwarf lines. Patents have also been sought on uses of turmeric and neem. Industrialised nations tend to be rich in money but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge, while developing countries are the reverse. India responded with the second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill.

Q1. Bt toxin crystals do not kill the Bacillus thuringiensis bacterium itself because the toxin is: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Inactive in the bacterium

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the toxin exists as an inactive protoxin and becomes active only in the alkaline gut of the insect. This adapts NCERT exercise 4.

Q2. Which part of a plant is used to raise virus-free plants? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Meristem

💡 Explanation: The apical and axillary meristems stay free of virus even in an infected plant, so they are cultured in vitro. This adapts NCERT exercise 1.

Q3. The first clinical gene therapy (1990) treated a deficiency of which enzyme? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Adenosine deaminase

💡 Explanation: A 4-year-old girl with adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency received it; her lymphocytes were given a functional ADA cDNA through a retroviral vector.

Q4. Which technique detects a pathogen by antigen-antibody interaction? easy

✅ Answer: (d) ELISA

💡 Explanation: ELISA detects antigens of a pathogen or antibodies made against it. PCR instead amplifies the pathogen's nucleic acid.

Q5. Silencing a nematode's mRNA in tobacco roots used: medium

✅ Answer: (b) RNA interference

💡 Explanation: Nematode-specific genes introduced through Agrobacterium made sense and antisense RNA, which formed dsRNA and silenced the parasite's mRNA (RNAi).

Why is insulin made in bacteria better than animal insulin?

Insulin from slaughtered cattle and pigs caused allergy or other reactions in some patients. Recombinant human insulin is identical to the natural molecule, so it does not trigger unwanted immune responses, and bacteria can be grown to make it in bulk.

What is biopiracy?

NCERT defines it as the use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned and without compensatory payment. The 1997 US patent on Basmati and patent attempts on turmeric and neem are its examples.

Why are most transgenic animals mice?

NCERT gives the figure (over 95 per cent are mice) but not the reason. Mice are used for studying gene function and disease models, testing vaccine safety (for example, polio vaccine) and chemical toxicity testing.
