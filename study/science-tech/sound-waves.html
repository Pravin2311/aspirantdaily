Sound Waves: Speed of Sound in Different Media, Frequency, Echo and Ultrasound

Longitudinal mechanical wave • Compressions and rarefactions • v = λν • Steel 5000, water 1500, air 340 m/s • 20 Hz to 20 kHz • Echo and 17 m • Reverberation • Ultrasound and SONAR

SSC Railways 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave. It needs a medium, and it travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. NCERT's Class 9 science textbook "Exploration" (chapter 10, "Sound Waves: Characteristics and Applications") gives these approximate values at 15 °C:

StateMediumApproximate speedCompared with air
SolidSteel5000 m/sabout 14.7 times
LiquidWater1500 m/sabout 4.4 times
GasAir340 m/s1

The last column is our own division of the table values. NCERT's general statement is that sound is about 4 to 5 times faster in water and typically 15 to 20 times faster in solids than in air. Sound cannot travel through vacuum at all.

Sound is produced by vibration, the periodic to and fro motion of an object. A plucked string, a struck tuning fork, the air column in a bansuri and human vocal cords in the larynx all work this way. The object that produces the sound is its source.

The vacuum bell jar experiment shows that a medium is needed. As air is pumped out of a jar holding a ringing electric bell, the sound fades. Near vacuum, almost nothing is heard even though the bell is seen ringing. This is why astronauts on a spacewalk cannot hear each other directly and talk through devices in their spacesuits.

Inside the medium, a vibrating source pushes particles together and then lets them spread out:

  • Compression (C): a region where the density of the medium is higher than average.
  • Rarefaction (R): a region where the density is lower than average.

A sound wave is a series of alternating compressions and rarefactions moving through the medium. The particles do not travel with the wave; each one only vibrates about its mean position, and it is energy that is passed on. Because the particles vibrate parallel to the direction the wave moves, sound is a longitudinal wave. Because it needs matter to travel, it is a mechanical wave. Light, a transverse wave, needs no medium, which is how sunlight reaches the Earth through space.

On a graph of density against distance, the highest point (maximum density, inside a compression) is the crest and the lowest point (minimum density, inside a rarefaction) is the trough.

QuantityMeaningSymbol and SI unit
WavelengthDistance between two consecutive crests or two consecutive troughsλ, metre
FrequencyNumber of density oscillations at a fixed point per unit timeν, hertz (Hz)
Time periodTime taken for one complete density oscillation at a fixed pointT, second
AmplitudeMaximum change in density in a compression or rarefaction, compared with the average densityNo unit given at this level
IntensitySound energy passing per unit time through a unit area perpendicular to the direction of travelMeasured quantity

Two formulas link these quantities:

  • ν = 1/T. A shorter time period means a higher frequency. Ten oscillations in 2 seconds give a frequency of 5 Hz and a time period of 0.2 s.
  • v = λ × ν. Speed equals wavelength times frequency.

A wave with a larger amplitude carries more energy. As sound spreads out from its source, the same energy covers a larger area, so its intensity falls with distance.

  • The medium: solid, then liquid, then gas, from fastest to slowest.
  • Temperature and humidity of air: speed rises with both. In dry air it is about 331 m/s at 0 °C and nearly 344 m/s at 22 °C.
  • Not frequency: in most media, such as air, the speed depends only on the medium. If the source's frequency changes, the wavelength changes and the speed stays the same.

When an aircraft flies faster than sound (a supersonic aircraft), it produces a loud sound called a sonic boom.

Wavelengths at the edges of hearing. With v = 344 m/s: at 20 Hz, λ = 344 ÷ 20 = 17.2 m. At 20,000 Hz, λ = 344 ÷ 20,000 = 0.0172 m, or 1.72 cm.

Steel fence versus air. Two people stand 340 m apart along a steel fence, and one knocks on it. Through air, the sound takes 340 ÷ 340 = 1 s. Through steel, it takes 340 ÷ 5000 = 0.068 s. The gap is 0.932 s, well above the 0.1 s needed to hear two separate sounds, so the listener hears the knock twice.

Lightning distance. Thunder heard 5 s after the flash, at 340 m/s, means the strike was about 340 × 5 = 1700 m away. Light arrives almost instantly.

Temperature effect. Over 1720 m, sound takes 1720 ÷ 344 = 5.0 s at 22 °C but 1720 ÷ 331 ≈ 5.2 s at 0 °C, about 0.2 s longer.

  • Pitch is how we perceive frequency. A whistle or siren has a high pitch; thunder has a low pitch.
  • Loudness is how we perceive amplitude. Intensity is measurable, but loudness also depends on the listener's hearing.
  • Decibels: rustling leaves are a few dB, normal conversation is about 60 dB, and firecrackers can exceed 100 dB.
  • Timbre is the quality that makes a flute and a tabla sound different on the same note. It comes from the pattern of overtones above the fundamental frequency.

Sound follows the same laws of reflection as light. An echo is reflected sound heard separately from the original. The brain needs a gap of at least 0.1 s to separate two sounds. At 340 m/s, sound covers 34 m in 0.1 s, going to the reflecting surface and back, so the minimum distance for an echo is 17 m.

Hard, smooth surfaces give strong echoes. Curtains absorb sound and rough surfaces scatter it. Clapping in a corridor and hearing the echo after 0.5 s puts the wall 340 × 0.25 = 85 m away.

Reverberation is sound persisting through many reflections, which happens when reflections arrive less than 0.05 s apart. Concert halls use soft, porous panels, upholstered chairs and curtains to control it. NCERT cites the Whispering Gallery of the Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur, Karnataka, where a faint whisper is heard several times across the dome.

WaveUses listed by NCERT
UltrasonicUltrasonography of internal organs; breaking kidney stones; ultrasonic welding and cleaning delicate machine parts; finding defects inside metal blocks; locating objects by reflection
InfrasonicDetecting earthquakes, volcanic eruptions and severe storms

Echolocation: bats send out short bursts of ultrasound and use the echoes to find obstacles and prey. Dolphins, whales and some birds do the same.

SONAR (sound navigation and ranging) applies the same idea under water. A ship sends ultrasound into the sea and analyses the reflection to find the distance, direction and speed of submarines or wrecks. If a signal returns after 0.90 s in seawater at 1530 m/s, the object is 1530 × 0.45 = 688.5 m away. Halve the time, because the sound goes there and back.

  • Sound is a longitudinal, mechanical wave; it cannot pass through vacuum.
  • Speed order: solids > liquids > gases (steel 5000, water 1500, air 340 m/s at 15 °C).
  • v = λν and ν = 1/T; frequency is measured in hertz.
  • Human hearing: 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Below is infrasonic, above is ultrasonic.
  • Echo needs at least 0.1 s, so the reflector must be at least 17 m away.
  • Pitch depends on frequency; loudness on amplitude.

Q1. In which medium does sound travel fastest? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Steel

💡 Explanation: Sound is fastest in solids; NCERT's table gives about 5000 m/s in steel at 15 °C.

Q2. Sound cannot travel through: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Vacuum

💡 Explanation: Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a medium; the bell jar experiment shows this.

Q3. Sound waves in air are: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Longitudinal

💡 Explanation: Air particles vibrate parallel to the direction of propagation, so sound is longitudinal.

Q4. The audible range for humans is: easy

✅ Answer: (d) 20 Hz to 20 kHz

💡 Explanation: NCERT gives 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, a range that varies by person and narrows with age.

Q5. The minimum distance of a reflector to hear a distinct echo in air (340 m/s) is: medium

✅ Answer: (b) 17 m

💡 Explanation: Sound needs 0.1 s: 340 × 0.1 = 34 m there and back, so the reflector is 17 m away.

Q6. Pitch of a sound depends mainly on its: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Frequency

💡 Explanation: Pitch is how humans perceive frequency; loudness relates to amplitude.

Q7. Sound waves above 20 kHz are called: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Ultrasonic

💡 Explanation: NCERT: frequencies above 20 kHz are ultrasonic; below 20 Hz are infrasonic.

Q8. If wavelength is 17.2 m and speed 344 m/s, the frequency is: medium

✅ Answer: (d) 20 Hz

💡 Explanation: Frequency equals speed divided by wavelength: 344 ÷ 17.2 gives 20 Hz.

What is the speed of sound in air?

About 340 m/s at 15 °C in NCERT's table. It rises with temperature and humidity: about 331 m/s at 0 °C and nearly 344 m/s at 22 °C.

Why is lightning seen before thunder is heard?

Light travels at about 300,000 km/s and arrives almost instantly, while sound travels at about 340 m/s, so a 5 s gap means about 1.7 km.
