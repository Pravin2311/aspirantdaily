Mitosis vs Meiosis: Cell Cycle, Stages, Prophase I Sub-stages and Key Differences

Interphase G1, S, G2 and G0 • Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase • Leptotene to diakinesis • Recombinase and chiasmata • 2 diploid vs 4 haploid cells

SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Both are ways a eukaryotic cell divides. Mitosis makes copies for growth and repair. Meiosis makes haploid cells for sexual reproduction. The table below is the comparison most often tested.

PointMitosisMeiosis
Also calledEquational divisionReduction division
Occurs inSomatic (body) cells, usually diploidDiploid cells that will form gametes
DivisionsOneTwo (meiosis I and II)
DNA replicationOnceOnce only, before meiosis I
Pairing of homologuesNoYes, in zygotene (synapsis)

Human cells in culture divide about once every 24 hours, while yeast can finish a cycle in about 90 minutes. Division itself (the M phase) takes only about an hour of a human cell's 24 hours. Interphase takes more than 95% of the time, or about 22.8 hours. Interphase is not a resting stage: it is when the cell prepares to divide.

PhaseWhat happensDNA content
G1 (Gap 1)Cell grows and is metabolically active; no DNA replication2C

G2 (Gap 2)Proteins are made for mitosis; growth continues4C
G0 (quiescent)Cells that stop dividing, such as heart cells, leave G1 and wait here; they stay metabolically active—

A common trap: the S phase doubles the amount of DNA but not the number of chromosomes.

  1. Prophase: chromosomes condense and the duplicated centrosomes move to opposite poles, sending out asters that form the spindle. By the end, the Golgi complex, ER, nucleolus and nuclear envelope are no longer visible.
  2. Metaphase: the nuclear envelope has fully broken down, and the chromosomes line up on the metaphase plate at the spindle equator. This is the best stage for studying chromosome shape.
  3. Anaphase: each chromosome splits at the same moment, and the two chromatids, now daughter chromosomes, move to opposite poles with the centromere leading.
  4. Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and decondense. The nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi complex and ER re-form.

Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm. In animal cells a furrow in the plasma membrane deepens until it cuts the cell in two. Plant cells have a rigid wall, so a cell plate grows from the centre outward; it becomes the middle lamella. If nuclear division is not followed by cytokinesis, the result is a multinucleate cell.

In animals, mitosis usually happens only in diploid somatic cells. Haploid male honey bees are an exception, and in some lower plants haploid cells also divide by mitosis.

Meiosis I separates the homologous chromosomes. Its long prophase I has five sub-stages, which are a favourite exam topic:

Sub-stageKey event
LeptoteneChromosomes become visible and keep condensing
ZygoteneHomologues pair (synapsis); the synaptonemal complex forms; a pair is called a bivalent or tetrad
PachyteneRecombination nodules appear; crossing over between non-sister chromatids, using the enzyme recombinase
DiploteneThe synaptonemal complex dissolves, and the homologues stay joined at X-shaped chiasmata. In some vertebrate oocytes this lasts months or years
DiakinesisChiasmata terminalise, the spindle forms, and the nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear
  • Metaphase I: bivalents line up at the equator.
  • Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes separate, while sister chromatids stay joined at their centromeres.
  • Telophase I: the nuclei re-form and cytokinesis gives two cells, called a dyad.

Interkinesis is a short gap with no DNA replication.

Meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis. Chromosomes line up again in metaphase II. In anaphase II the centromeres split and the sister chromatids finally separate. Telophase II and cytokinesis give a tetrad of four haploid cells.

Worked example: an onion root tip cell has 16 chromosomes. After mitosis each daughter cell still has 16. After meiosis in the same plant, each of the four cells has 8.

  • DNA vs chromosome count: after the S phase, DNA content doubles from 2C to 4C, but the chromosome number stays 2n.
  • Order of prophase I events: pairing (synapsis) happens in zygotene and crossing over in pachytene. Chiasmata become visible in diplotene and terminalise in diakinesis. Questions often swap zygotene and pachytene.
  • What separates when: sister chromatids separate in mitotic anaphase and in anaphase II. Homologous chromosomes separate in anaphase I.
  • Interkinesis is not interphase: there is no DNA replication between meiosis I and meiosis II.
  • Plant vs animal cytokinesis: plant cells form a cell plate; animal cells form a furrow.

Mitosis gives diploid cells with the same genetic material as the parent. It is behind the growth of multicellular organisms, and it restores the balance between nucleus and cytoplasm as a cell grows. It also repairs the body: the upper epidermis, the gut lining and blood cells are constantly replaced. In plants, mitosis in the apical and lateral meristems keeps them growing throughout their life.

Meiosis halves the chromosome number, so that fertilisation restores it. This keeps each species' chromosome number constant across generations. Crossing over also increases genetic variation from one generation to the next, which NCERT calls very important for evolution.

Q1. Crossing over takes place during which sub-stage of prophase I? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Pachytene

💡 Explanation: Crossing over between non-sister chromatids happens in pachytene, at recombination nodules, with the enzyme recombinase. Synapsis begins earlier, in zygotene.

Q2. The synaptonemal complex is formed during: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Zygotene

💡 Explanation: Synapsis of homologous chromosomes in zygotene is accompanied by the synaptonemal complex. It dissolves at the start of diplotene.

Q3. Homologous chromosomes separate during: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Anaphase I

💡 Explanation: In anaphase I the homologous chromosomes move apart while sister chromatids stay joined. Sister chromatids separate later, in anaphase II.

Q4. Cells such as heart cells that stop dividing enter which stage? easy

✅ Answer: (b) G0 (quiescent stage)

💡 Explanation: Cells that do not divide further leave G1 and enter the quiescent G0 stage, where they remain metabolically active.

Q5. If an onion root tip cell has 16 chromosomes, a gamete-forming cell of the same plant after meiosis would have: easy

✅ Answer: (c) 8

💡 Explanation: Meiosis halves the chromosome number, so 16 becomes 8 in each of the four haploid cells. Mitosis would keep it at 16.

Why is meiosis called reduction division?

Because it halves the chromosome number. A diploid (2n) cell gives four haploid (n) cells, and fertilisation later restores the diploid number.

Why is mitosis called equational division?

Because the parent and both daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes, and the daughters carry an identical genetic complement.

Does DNA replicate before meiosis II?

No. DNA replicates once, in the S phase before meiosis I. NCERT states there is no DNA replication in interkinesis, the short gap between the two divisions.
