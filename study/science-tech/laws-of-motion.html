Newton's Laws of Motion With Everyday Examples: Inertia, F = ma, Impulse, Action and Reaction, and Conservation of Momentum

Aristotle's fallacy and Galileo • First law: inertia • Second law: rate of change of momentum, F = ma, 1 N = 1 kg m/s² • Impulse • Third law: forces in pairs • Conservation of momentum and recoil

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

Common experience suggests that a body needs a push to keep moving: a toy car stops when the child lets go of the string. The Greek thinker Aristotle turned this into a rule, which NCERT phrases as: an external force is required to keep a body in motion.

NCERT explains the flaw. The toy car stops because friction from the floor opposes it. When the car moves uniformly, the child's pull and friction cancel, so there is no net force. Galileo, studying balls on inclined planes, saw that objects speed up going down and slow down going up, and concluded that on a frictionless horizontal plane a body would move at constant velocity forever. Rest and uniform straight-line motion are equivalent states. Newton built his three laws on this idea.

Statement: every body continues to be in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by some external force to act otherwise.

The property described is inertia, which NCERT glosses as "resistance to change". A simpler form: if the net external force on a body is zero, its acceleration is zero.

  • Bus starting suddenly: our feet move with the floor through friction, but the upper body stays where it was, so we are thrown backward.
  • Bus braking suddenly: the body tends to keep moving, so passengers are thrown forward.
  • Book on a table: it is at rest, so the net force is zero; the table's upward normal force must equal the book's weight. NCERT points out that the correct reasoning runs from the observed rest to zero net force, not the other way round.
  • Spaceship in deep space with its rockets off: no net force, so it keeps moving with uniform velocity.

Momentum is mass × velocity, a vector. Everyday cases show why it matters: a loaded truck needs a far bigger push than a car to reach the same speed in the same time, and a fast bullet does far more damage than the same bullet at low speed.

Statement: the rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction in which the force acts. For constant mass this gives F = ma.

The SI unit of force is the newton: the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s², so 1 N = 1 kg m/s².

The second law is a vector law: a force changes only the component of velocity along its own direction. In projectile motion, gravity acts downward, so the horizontal velocity stays unchanged.

When a large force acts for a very short time, as when a ball bounces off a wall or a bat, the force and time are hard to measure separately, but their product is clear. This product, impulse = force × time = change in momentum, is measured in N s. NCERT calls such a short, large force an impulsive force.

A seasoned cricketer draws in the hands while catching. The ball's change in momentum is the same either way, but spreading it over a longer time needs a smaller force, so it does not hurt. A novice who stops the ball almost instantly feels a much greater force.

NCERT's example: a batsman hits a 0.15 kg ball straight back at its original speed of 12 m/s. Its velocity changes by 12 + 12 = 24 m/s, so the impulse is 0.15 × 24 = 3.6 N s, directed from the batsman to the bowler.

Statement: to every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction. NCERT gives a clearer form: forces always occur in pairs; the force on body A by B is equal and opposite to the force on B by A.

  • "Action" and "reaction" both just mean force.
  • There is no cause and effect: the two forces act at the same instant, and either can be called the action.
  • They act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other. Forces between parts of one body do add up to zero.

Everyday examples: a compressed spring pushes back on your hand; the earth pulls a stone down and the stone pulls the earth up by the same amount, though the earth's huge mass means its acceleration is too small to notice; a swimmer pushes water back to move forward; a rocket pushes exhaust gases out and is pushed the other way.

The second and third laws together give the law of conservation of momentum: the total momentum of an isolated system of interacting particles is conserved. Mutual forces change each particle's momentum, but the changes cancel in pairs.

Gun recoil: before firing, gun and bullet are at rest, so total momentum is zero. After firing, the bullet's momentum forward equals the gun's momentum backward. Take NCERT's exercise of a 0.020 kg shell leaving a 100 kg gun at 80 m/s. The shell's momentum is 0.020 × 80 = 1.6 kg m/s, so the gun recoils at 1.6 / 100 = 0.016 m/s. The same law governs collisions between two bodies.

Q1. Passengers in a bus that starts suddenly are thrown backward. Which law explains this? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Newton's first law (inertia)

💡 Explanation: Friction moves the feet with the bus, but the upper body tends to stay at rest because of inertia, so it falls back relative to the bus.

Q2. A net force of 6 N acts on a 2 kg mass. What is its acceleration? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 3 m/s²

💡 Explanation: From F = ma, a = F / m = 6 / 2 = 3 m/s².

Q3. Why does a fielder pull the hands back while catching a fast ball? medium

✅ Answer: (c) To increase the time of impact and so reduce the force

💡 Explanation: The change in momentum is fixed; spreading it over a longer time lowers the rate of change of momentum, which is the force.

Q4. Why do action and reaction forces not cancel each other? medium

✅ Answer: (d) They act on different bodies

💡 Explanation: NCERT stresses that action and reaction act on different bodies, so they cannot cancel each other's effect on either body.

Q5. A 0.020 kg shell leaves a 100 kg gun at 80 m/s. What is the recoil speed of the gun? hard

✅ Answer: (a) 0.016 m/s

💡 Explanation: Total momentum stays zero: 100 × v = 0.020 × 80 = 1.6, so v = 1.6 / 100 = 0.016 m/s, opposite to the shell.

What are Newton's three laws of motion in one line each?

First: a body stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless a net external force acts. Second: force equals the rate of change of momentum, F = ma for constant mass. Third: forces occur in pairs, equal and opposite, acting on different bodies.

Is a force needed to keep a body moving at constant velocity?

No net force is needed. In daily life we push to cancel friction, so the forces add up to zero. Without friction a body would keep moving uniformly, as Galileo showed.

What is the SI unit of force?

The newton. One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s², so 1 N = 1 kg m/s².
