Five Kingdom Classification: Whittaker's Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, plus Viruses, Viroids, Prions and Lichens

Aristotle → Linnaeus (two kingdoms) → Whittaker 1969 (five kingdoms) • Criteria: cell structure, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, phylogeny • Ivanowsky, Beijerinck, Stanley, Diener

SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Classification began with practical needs such as food, shelter and clothing. Aristotle made the earliest scientific attempt. He sorted plants into trees, shrubs and herbs, and animals into those with red blood and those without.

Linnaeus's two-kingdom system, Plantae and Animalia, lasted a long time but had gaps. It did not separate prokaryotes from eukaryotes, unicellular from multicellular organisms, or photosynthetic green algae from non-photosynthetic fungi. Bacteria, blue-green algae and fungi were all called "plants" only because they had cell walls.

In 1969, R.H. Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. He used five criteria:

  1. Cell structure
  2. Body organisation
  3. Mode of nutrition
  4. Reproduction
  5. Phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships

Why the change mattered: once cell structure, the nature of the cell wall, the mode of nutrition and the body plan were counted, the old "plant" group broke apart. Fungi went to their own kingdom because their walls are not made of cellulose. All prokaryotes went to Monera, and all single-celled eukaryotes to Protista.

NCERT also mentions a later three-domain system. It splits Monera into two domains and puts all eukaryotes in a third, which gives six kingdoms.

Monera: bacteria are its only members and the most abundant micro-organisms. They live even in hot springs, deserts, snow and deep oceans, and they show the widest metabolic diversity of any group.

  • Shapes: coccus (spherical), bacillus (rod), vibrium (comma) and spirillum (spiral).
  • Archaebacteria live in harsh places: halophiles in salty areas, thermoacidophiles in hot springs, and methanogens in marshes. Methanogens in the gut of cows and buffaloes produce methane, the biogas in dung. A different cell wall structure helps archaebacteria survive these conditions.
  • Eubacteria have a rigid cell wall. Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) contain chlorophyll a. Some, such as Nostoc and Anabaena, fix nitrogen in special cells called heterocysts.
  • Heterotrophic bacteria are the most abundant. Most are decomposers, and they help make curd and antibiotics and fix nitrogen in legumes.

Protista holds all single-celled eukaryotes, which are mostly aquatic. It brought together organisms that earlier systems split: Chlamydomonas and Chlorella, once algae, and Amoeba and Paramoecium, once animals.

  • Chrysophytes (diatoms and desmids): walls contain silica. Their deposits form diatomaceous earth, used for polishing and filtering. Diatoms are the chief producers in the oceans.
  • Dinoflagellates: mostly marine, with cellulose plates and two flagella. Gonyaulax causes red tides.
  • Euglenoids: Euglena has a flexible pellicle instead of a wall, and feeds like a heterotroph without sunlight.
  • Slime moulds: saprophytic. They form a spreading plasmodium, and their spores survive for years.
  • Protozoans: all heterotrophs. They include amoeboid forms (Amoeba, Entamoeba) and flagellated forms (Trypanosoma, which causes sleeping sickness). Ciliated forms such as Paramoecium and sporozoans such as Plasmodium, which causes malaria, are the other two groups.

Fungi are heterotrophs that like warm, humid places. Yeast makes bread and beer, Puccinia causes wheat rust, and Penicillium is a source of antibiotics. Except for yeasts, fungi are made of thread-like hyphae that form a mycelium, and their walls are made of chitin and polysaccharides. Most are saprophytes; some are parasites; some are symbionts, as in lichens (with algae) and mycorrhiza (with plant roots). The four classes are Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes and Deuteromycetes. Deuteromycetes are the "imperfect fungi", because only their asexual stages are known.

Plantae are eukaryotes with chlorophyll and cellulose walls, from algae to angiosperms. A few are partly heterotrophic: bladderwort and Venus fly trap are insectivorous, and Cuscuta is a parasite. Their life cycle alternates between a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte (alternation of generations).

Animalia are multicellular heterotrophs without cell walls. They digest food in an internal cavity, store it as glycogen or fat, and feed by ingestion (holozoic nutrition). See animal kingdom phyla for the groups.

Whittaker's system leaves out lichens and acellular agents such as viruses, viroids and prions.

Q1. Who proposed dividing living organisms into five kingdoms? easy

✅ Answer: (b) R.H. Whittaker

💡 Explanation: R.H. Whittaker proposed it in 1969. Linnaeus used two kingdoms, and Aristotle made the earliest attempt based on simple morphology.

Q2. Which kingdom contains all prokaryotes? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Monera

💡 Explanation: All prokaryotic organisms were grouped under Monera, and bacteria are its sole members.

Q3. Cell walls of fungi are made of: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Chitin and polysaccharides

💡 Explanation: NCERT says fungal walls are made of chitin and polysaccharides. Green plants have cellulose walls, and diatom walls contain silica.

Q4. Potato spindle tuber disease is caused by a: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Viroid

💡 Explanation: T.O. Diener (1971) found that it is caused by a viroid, a free RNA without a protein coat.

Q5. In a lichen, the algal partner is called the: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Phycobiont

💡 Explanation: The algal partner is the phycobiont, which is autotrophic. The fungal partner is the mycobiont, which is heterotrophic.

Why was the two kingdom system replaced?

It put prokaryotic bacteria with eukaryotic plants, single-celled organisms with multicellular ones, and non-photosynthetic fungi with green plants. Many organisms fitted neither plants nor animals.

Are viruses part of the five kingdoms?

No. Whittaker's system does not mention viruses, viroids, prions or lichens. Viruses are non-cellular, inert outside a host cell, and are not considered truly living in the cell-based sense.

Why is Euglena hard to classify?

It photosynthesises in sunlight but feeds like a heterotroph in the dark, and it has a flexible pellicle instead of a cell wall. It is placed in Protista as a euglenoid.
