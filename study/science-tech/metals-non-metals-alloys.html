Metals, Non-Metals and Alloys: Properties, Exceptions, Activity Series, Extraction, Corrosion and Alloy Composition

Brass = Cu + Zn • Bronze = Cu + Sn • Solder = Pb + Sn • Stainless steel = Fe + Ni + Cr • Amalgam has mercury • 22 carat = 22 parts gold + 2 parts Cu or Ag

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal. It is made by melting the main metal and dissolving the others in it in fixed proportions. NCERT names these alloys and their constituents:

AlloyMade ofKey property or use (NCERT)
BrassCopper + zinc (Cu, Zn)Poor conductor of electricity compared with copper
BronzeCopper + tin (Cu, Sn)Poor conductor of electricity compared with copper
SolderLead + tin (Pb, Sn)Low melting point; used to join electrical wires
SteelIron + small amount of carbon (about 0.05%)Hard and strong, unlike soft pure iron
Stainless steelIron + nickel + chromiumHard and does not rust
AmalgamAny alloy in which one metal is mercuryNamed for the mercury
22 carat gold22 parts gold + 2 parts copper or silverHard enough for ornaments; 24 carat gold is too soft
NichromeNickel, chromium, manganese, ironHeating coils (from NCERT's electricity chapter)

Why alloy? Alloying changes a metal's properties to suit a use. An alloy's electrical conductivity and melting point are lower than those of the pure metal, which is why copper, not brass, is used for circuits. Alloying is also one of the ways to prevent rusting.

Gold and silver are the most malleable metals, and gold is the most ductile: about 2 km of wire can be drawn from one gram. Most non-metals form acidic oxides; most metals form basic oxides, and sodium and potassium oxides dissolve to give alkalis.

NCERT's activity series, from most to least reactive:

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au

  • With water: potassium and sodium react violently with cold water and the hydrogen catches fire, so they are stored in kerosene. Calcium reacts less violently and floats on hydrogen bubbles. Magnesium needs hot water. Aluminium, iron and zinc react only with steam. Lead, copper, silver and gold do not react at all.
  • With acids: metals above hydrogen release hydrogen from dilute acids (Mg > Al > Zn > Fe); copper does not react with dilute HCl. Nitric acid gives no hydrogen because it is a strong oxidising agent.
  • With oxygen: silver and gold do not react even at high temperature.
  • Displacement: a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.
  • Aqua regia: concentrated HCl and HNO₃ in a 3:1 ratio dissolves gold and platinum.

Metals lose electrons and non-metals gain them to form ionic compounds such as NaCl. These are hard, brittle solids with high melting points, soluble in water but not in kerosene or petrol, and conduct electricity when dissolved or molten but not as solids.

ReactivityExamplesHow the metal is obtained
HighK, Na, Ca, Mg, AlElectrolysis of molten chlorides or oxide; carbon cannot reduce them
MediumZn, Fe, Pb, CuRoasting (sulphides, excess air) or calcination (carbonates, limited air), then reduction with carbon
LowHg, Cu; Au, Ag, Pt found freeHeating alone, e.g. cinnabar (HgS) to mercury
  • Terms: minerals occur naturally; an ore is a mineral rich enough to extract from profitably; gangue is the soil and sand impurity. Extraction and refining together are metallurgy.
  • Thermit reaction: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al gives molten iron; used to join railway tracks and cracked machine parts.
  • Electrolytic refining: impure metal is the anode, a pure strip the cathode; insoluble impurities settle as anode mud.
  • Corrosion: silver blackens (silver sulphide), copper turns green (basic copper carbonate) and iron rusts in moist air.
  • Prevention: painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (zinc coating, which protects even when broken), chrome plating, anodising (a thicker oxide layer on aluminium) and alloying.

Ancient Indian metallurgy: the iron pillar near the Qutub Minar in Delhi, more than 1600 years old, 8 m high and 6 tonnes, has resisted rust. For acids and salts, see acids, bases and the pH scale.

Q1. Bronze is an alloy of: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Copper and tin

💡 Explanation: NCERT gives bronze as copper and tin (Cu and Sn). Brass is copper and zinc, and solder is lead and tin.

Q2. Which alloy is used to join electrical wires because of its low melting point? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Solder

💡 Explanation: Solder, an alloy of lead and tin, has a low melting point and is used for welding electrical wires together, NCERT says.

Q3. Which metal is a liquid at room temperature? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Mercury

💡 Explanation: All metals except mercury are solids at room temperature. Gallium and caesium have melting points low enough to melt on the palm but are solid at room temperature.

Q4. Which reaction is used to join broken railway tracks? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Thermit reaction

💡 Explanation: In the thermit reaction iron(III) oxide reacts with aluminium, giving so much heat that molten iron forms and can weld rail ends or cracked machine parts.

Q5. 22 carat gold contains how many parts of pure gold in 24? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 22

💡 Explanation: NCERT says 22 carat gold is 22 parts of pure gold alloyed with 2 parts of copper or silver to make it hard enough for ornaments. Pure gold is 24 carat.

What is an alloy?

A homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or of a metal and a non-metal. It is made by melting the main metal and dissolving the others in it in definite proportions. Alloys usually conduct electricity less well and melt at lower temperatures than pure metals.

Why is pure iron not used?

Pure iron is very soft and stretches easily when hot. Adding about 0.05 per cent carbon makes it hard and strong, and adding nickel and chromium gives stainless steel, which does not rust.

Why are sodium and potassium stored in kerosene?

They react so vigorously with oxygen that they catch fire if left in the open. Keeping them under kerosene keeps air and moisture away and prevents accidental fires.
