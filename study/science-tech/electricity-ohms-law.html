Electricity: Ohm's Law, Resistivity, Series and Parallel Resistors, Joule Heating and Electric Power

Two measuring rules come up in almost every paper: the ammeter goes in series with the part whose current you want, and the voltmeter goes in parallel across the two points whose potential difference you want. By convention, current flows opposite to the direction of electron flow.

Worked example 1: charge from current and time

A bulb filament draws 0.5 A for 10 minutes. Q = It = 0.5 A × 600 s = 300 C.

Ohm (1827) found that for a metallic wire, V is directly proportional to I as long as the temperature stays the same. The ratio V/I is the wire's resistance R, so the V–I graph is a straight line through the origin. From I = V/R, the current is inversely proportional to resistance: double R and the current halves.

Resistance depends on three things: length (R ∝ l), area of cross-section (R ∝ 1/A) and the material. Together these give R = ρl/A, where ρ (rho) is the resistivity, a property of the material alone. Changing the wire's length or thickness changes R but not ρ.

  • Metals and alloys: ρ of 10−8 to 10−6 Ω m, so they are good conductors. NCERT lists silver (1.60 × 10−8) and copper (1.62 × 10−8) as the lowest.
  • Insulators: rubber and glass are around 1012 to 1017 Ω m.
  • Alloys such as nichrome (nickel, chromium, manganese, iron) have higher resistivity than their metals and do not oxidise easily when hot, so they make heating coils in irons and toasters.
  • Tungsten is used almost only for bulb filaments; copper and aluminium carry power on transmission lines.

Ratio trick: NCERT's Example 11.6 halves the length and doubles the area of a 4 Ω wire. R = ρl/A changes by (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4, so the new wire is 1 Ω. Any "same material, new size" question works this way.

Method for a mixed circuit: (1) find groups wired in parallel and replace each with its equivalent; (2) add the groups that are now in series; (3) use I = V/R for the total current; (4) work back to each resistor with V = IR.

Shortcuts: two resistors in parallel give R1R2/(R1 + R2); n equal resistors R give nR in series and R/n in parallel.

Worked example 2: lamp and resistor in series (NCERT 11.7)

A 20 Ω lamp and a 4 Ω conductor in series across 6 V. Rs = 24 Ω, so I = 6/24 = 0.25 A. The lamp takes 20 × 0.25 = 5 V and the conductor 4 × 0.25 = 1 V, adding back to 6 V.

Worked example 3: three resistors in parallel (NCERT 11.8)

5 Ω, 10 Ω and 30 Ω across 12 V. Branch currents are 2.4 A, 1.2 A and 0.4 A, total 4 A. 1/Rp = 1/5 + 1/10 + 1/30 = 1/3, so Rp = 3 Ω, and 12/3 = 4 A agrees.

Worked example 4: mixed circuit (NCERT 11.9)

10 Ω and 40 Ω in parallel give 8 Ω. 30 Ω, 20 Ω and 60 Ω in parallel give 10 Ω. The two groups are in series: 8 + 10 = 18 Ω. On 12 V the current is 12/18 ≈ 0.67 A.

The energy a source supplies is VIt, and in a resistor it all becomes heat. Substituting V = IR gives Joule's law of heating: H = I²Rt. Heat rises with the square of current, and directly with resistance and time.

  • Bulbs: the filament must get very hot without melting, so it is tungsten (melting point 3380°C), and the bulb is filled with inactive nitrogen and argon to make the filament last longer.
  • Fuse: a wire of suitable melting point placed in series with the device. Too much current heats and melts it, breaking the circuit. Domestic fuses are rated 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A and so on.

Power is the rate of using energy: P = VI = I²R = V²/R, in watts. 1 W is 1 A at 1 V. Bills use the kilowatt hour, the "unit": 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 106 J. You pay for energy, not for electrons; electrons are not used up.

Worked example 5: bulb on half voltage

A 220 V, 100 W bulb has R = 220²/100 = 484 Ω. On 110 V, P = 110²/484 = 25 W. Since P = V²/R, half the voltage gives a quarter of the power.

Worked example 6: electricity bill (NCERT 11.13)

A 400 W fridge runs 8 hours a day for 30 days: 400 × 8 × 30 = 96,000 Wh = 96 kWh. At ₹3 per unit the cost is ₹288.

Worked example 7: Joule heating from a known heat rate

100 J of heat is produced each second in 4 Ω (NCERT 11.11). I² = H/(Rt) = 100/4 = 25, so I = 5 A and V = IR = 20 V. Check: I² = 5² = 25.

  1. Doubling the current through the same resistor quadruples the heat (H ∝ I²), not doubles it.
  2. Same wires, series vs parallel: two equal wires across the same voltage have 2R in series and R/2 in parallel. Since H = V²t/R, parallel produces 4 times the heat, so series : parallel = 1 : 4.
  3. Resistivity does not change when you stretch or cut a wire; only resistance does.
  4. Ohm's law needs constant temperature. A bulb filament's V/I ratio is not fixed as it heats up.
  5. Units: convert minutes to seconds for joules, and W h to kWh by dividing by 1000 for bills.

Q1. A wire of resistance R is cut into five equal parts, which are then joined in parallel. If the new resistance is R′, what is R/R′? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 25

💡 Explanation: Each piece has resistance R/5. Five equal resistors of R/5 in parallel give (R/5)/5 = R/25, so R/R′ = 25. This is NCERT exercise 1.

Q2. A bulb rated 220 V, 100 W is run on 110 V. What power does it consume? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 25 W

💡 Explanation: R = V²/P = 220²/100 = 484 Ω. At 110 V, P = 110²/484 = 25 W. Halving the voltage quarters the power.

Q3. Which of these does NOT represent electric power? easy

✅ Answer: (c) IR²

💡 Explanation: NCERT gives P = VI = I²R = V²/R. IR² has the wrong units, since I times R is a voltage and one more R does not give watts.

Q4. Why are heating elements of toasters and irons made of an alloy rather than a pure metal? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Alloys have high resistivity and do not oxidise readily when hot

💡 Explanation: NCERT says alloys have higher resistivity than their constituent metals and do not burn (oxidise) readily at high temperatures, which suits heating devices.

Q5. How is a voltmeter connected to measure the potential difference across a resistor? easy

✅ Answer: (b) In parallel across it

💡 Explanation: NCERT: a voltmeter is always connected in parallel across the points between which the potential difference is measured; the ammeter goes in series.

What is the formula for resistors in series and in parallel?

In series, add the resistances: Rs = R1 + R2 + R3, so the total is larger than any one resistor. In parallel, add the reciprocals: 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3, so the total is smaller than the smallest resistor.

Why are household appliances connected in parallel?

Each appliance then gets the full mains voltage and draws the current it needs, and one appliance failing does not cut off the others. In series the same current would flow through a bulb and a heater, which need very different currents.

How many joules are in one unit of electricity?

One unit is one kilowatt hour: 1000 W for 3600 s, which is 3.6 × 10^6 joules.
