Structure of the Atom: Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr Models, Subatomic Particles and Isotopes

Ideas about the atom changed as each new experiment showed the old picture was incomplete. NCERT's Class 9 science textbook "Exploration" (chapter 8, "Journey Inside the Atom") traces the sequence:

Acharya Kanada's Vaisesika Sutras describe parmanus, particles too small to perceive, which combine into dyads and triads to form all matter. The Greek word atomos means indivisible. Dalton's 1808 theory was the first scientific description, based on experiments of the time.

Two findings broke the idea that atoms cannot be divided:

  • Radioactivity: some elements emit radiation and particles, so atoms must contain smaller parts.
  • Cathode rays: in 1897 Thomson passed current through gas at very low pressure. The rays from the cathode were streams of negatively charged particles, much lighter than atoms. These were later called electrons. The rays were the same whatever the cathode material or gas, so electrons are present in every atom.

Thomson won the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work on the conduction of electricity in gases. The electron's charge, −1.602 × 10−19 C, is taken as −1 by convention.

Atoms are neutral, so the negative electrons need balancing positive charge. Thomson proposed the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons distributed through it, like plums in a pudding. NCERT's Indian picture is a watermelon: the red pulp is the positive matter and the seeds are the electrons.

In 1911, Geiger and Marsden, working under Rutherford, fired a narrow beam of alpha particles at a very thin gold foil. Alpha particles are positively charged; each is a helium nucleus with two protons and two neutrons. If Thomson was right, they should pass straight through or bend only a little.

ObservationRutherford's conclusion
Most alpha particles passed through undeflectedMost of the atom is empty space
Some were sharply deflected and a few bounced backThe positive charge and most of the mass sit in a tiny, dense nucleus

Electrons revolve around the nucleus like planets around the Sun, so this is called the planetary model. The atom's diameter is about 10−10 m and the nucleus's about 10−15 m, a ratio of 105 (one lakh). NCERT's comparison: if the atom were a cricket ground about 100 m across, the nucleus would be a grain of black pepper a few millimetres wide.

The flaw: an electron moving in a circle is accelerating, and an accelerating charge should lose energy, spiral inward and fall into the nucleus. Atoms would collapse, but real atoms are stable. Rutherford's model could not explain this.

Rutherford also showed that the nucleus's positive charge comes from protons, which are much heavier than electrons and carry an equal and opposite charge. He won the 1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Niels Bohr proposed his model in 1913 to explain stability:

  • Electrons move only in fixed circular paths called stationary states, orbits or shells, and not in between them.
  • Each shell has a definite energy, so shells are also called energy levels. They are labelled K, L, M, N or n = 1, 2, 3, 4.
  • K (n = 1), nearest the nucleus, has the least energy; energy rises outward.
  • An electron in a fixed shell does not lose energy. It moves between shells by absorbing or releasing exactly the energy difference.

The letters start at K because Charles Barkla named the first X-ray line he observed K, leaving room for an earlier series that was never found. Bohr received the Nobel Prize in 1922.

A helium atom has two protons but about four times the mass of hydrogen, which has one. In 1932 James Chadwick, a student of Rutherford, solved this by discovering the neutron. It has nearly the mass of a proton and no charge. He won the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Light atoms often have equal protons and neutrons, such as carbon with six of each. Heavy ones have more neutrons: iron has 26 protons and 30 neutrons, and uranium has 92 protons and 146 neutrons.

  • Atomic number (Z): the number of protons. It identifies the element. In a neutral atom it also equals the number of electrons.
  • Mass number (A): protons plus neutrons, together called nucleons. Lithium, with 3 protons and 4 neutrons, has A = 7.

The Bohr–Bury rules for filling shells:

  • A shell can hold at most 2n2 electrons: K holds 2, L holds 8 and M holds 18.
  • The outermost shell holds at most 8.
  • Shells fill in order, K first, then L, then M.

Valency is the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete an octet. Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one, so its valency is 1. Oxygen (2, 6) gains two: valency 2. Carbon (2, 4) shares four: valency 4. Atoms with a full octet, or two electrons in helium's case, are largely unreactive.

Isotopes have the same electronic configuration, so their chemical properties are similar, but physical properties such as boiling and melting points differ. Uses named by NCERT:

  • Uranium-235: fuel in nuclear reactors that generate electricity.
  • Cobalt-60: radiation treatment for cancer.
  • Iodine-131: treatment of goitre and thyroid cancer.
  • Carbon-14: finding the age of fossils and artefacts.

Average atomic mass of chlorine. Chlorine has isotopes of 35 u and 37 u in the ratio 3:1 (about 75% and 25%). The simple average would be 36 u, but the weighted average is 35 × 0.75 + 37 × 0.25 = 35.5 u. No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 u; that is the mass of an average atom in a natural sample.

  • Electron: Thomson, 1897. Nucleus: Rutherford, 1911. Shells: Bohr, 1913. Neutron: Chadwick, 1932.
  • Thomson's model is the plum pudding model; Rutherford's is the planetary model.
  • Rutherford's model failed to explain the stability of the atom; Bohr's stationary states fixed that.
  • Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n2; at most 8 in the outermost shell.
  • Isotopes share Z; isobars share A.

Why did scientists keep changing atomic models?

Each new experiment exposed a gap. Gold foil scattering ruled out Thomson's model, and the stability problem ruled out Rutherford's, which Bohr's stationary states addressed.

What is the difference between isotopes and isobars?

Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers, like protium and deuterium. Isobars have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
