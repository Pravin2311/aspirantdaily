Methods of Separation of Mixtures: Crystallization, Distillation, Chromatography, Centrifugation and Colloids

Each separation method uses one property in which the components of a mixture differ: solubility, boiling point, density, particle size or the ability to sublime. NCERT's Class 9 science textbook "Exploration" (chapter 5, "Exploring Mixtures and their Separation") covers these methods:

A mixture with the same composition throughout is homogeneous, such as salt dissolved in water. One with visibly different parts is heterogeneous, such as sand stirred into water. Most gas mixtures are homogeneous; NCERT's example is the hydrogen and oxygen used as rocket fuel. Smoke, fog and dust in air are heterogeneous.

By particle size, mixtures are solutions, colloids or suspensions:

TypeParticle diameterSettles?Scatters light?Examples
SolutionLess than 1 nmNoNoSalt water, glucose solution
Colloid1 to 1000 nmNoYesMilk, blood, tomato sauce, ice cream
SuspensionMore than 1000 nmYes, over timeYesSand, sawdust or tea leaves in water

Alloys are homogeneous mixtures of metals, or of a metal and a non-metal. Physical methods cannot separate them. Brass is about 80% copper and 20% zinc; bronze is about 80% copper and 20% tin.

MeasureMeaningWhere usedNCERT example
Mass by mass, % m/m (or % w/w)Grams of solute in 100 g of solutionPackaged food labels, milk powder10 g salt in 90 g water = 10%
Mass by volume, % m/vGrams of solute in 100 mL of solutionMedicines and laboratories5 g glucose in 100 mL = 5%; saline drips are usually 0.9%
Volume by volume, % v/vMillilitres of solute in 100 mL of solutionPerfumes, cosmetics, vinegar1 mL pesticide in 100 mL spray = 1%

Note that the m/m example divides by the mass of the solution (10 + 90 = 100 g), not the mass of water alone.

Solubility is the most solute that dissolves in a fixed amount of solvent (100 g or 100 mL) at a given temperature. A solution that can dissolve no more is saturated. For solids in liquids, solubility generally rises with temperature.

Crystallization forms crystals from a saturated solution. It works because solubility changes with temperature. If a solid is dissolved hot and the solution is cooled, the excess comes out as pure crystals while impurities stay dissolved. In NCERT's solubility-curve example, a solution holding 287 g of compound B per 100 g of water at 60 °C is cooled to 40 °C, where only 241 g can stay dissolved. The other 46 g crystallizes out.

Coastal communities in India made salt this way. NCERT names two kinds: panga salt, from boiling concentrated sea brine, and karkatch salt, from evaporating sea water.

In distillation, the mixture is heated until the liquid with the lower boiling point vaporises. Its vapour is cooled in a condenser and collected as a pure liquid. The method needs a boiling-point gap of at least about 25 °C, and it can also recover a liquid from dissolved solids.

Fractional distillation handles boiling points less than 25 °C apart. Refineries use it to split crude petroleum into fractions. The gas fraction is filled into steel cylinders under high pressure, where it liquefies into LPG for cooking.

NCERT's Indian example is Kannauj in Uttar Pradesh, known as India's perfume capital. There the Deg-Bhapka method distils the smell of rain on earth into Mitti ka Ittar.

A spot of ink near the bottom of a paper strip dipped in solvent separates as the liquid climbs. Each component moves at its own speed, depending on how it interacts with the solvent and the paper. The word comes from the Greek chroma (colour) and graphein (to write), "writing with colour", because the method was first used on dyes and inks.

  • Separating funnel: immiscible liquids such as mustard oil and water form separate layers by density, so each layer can be run off on its own.
  • Sublimation: heated camphor turns directly from solid to vapour without melting, and the vapour turns back to solid on cooling, called deposition. Sand does not sublime, so it stays behind. Naphthalene and dry ice also sublime.
  • Centrifugation: spinning tubes at high speed sends the heavier particles outward to the bottom, while the lighter liquid stays on top. Blood banks use it to separate plasma, platelets and white and red blood cells. A hand-powered paperfuge does the same without electricity, which helps test for malaria and anaemia in remote areas.
  • Coagulation: alum makes fine mud particles clump together, so they settle (sedimentation) and can be removed by decantation or filtration. Paneer is also made by coagulation, using lemon juice or vinegar on milk proteins.

The Tyndall effect, named after John Tyndall, is the scattering of light by particles. It is the visible beam of a stadium floodlight or a sunbeam through a dusty room. Light scatters in colloids and suspensions but not in a true solution, so the effect tells a colloid from a solution.

A colloid has a dispersed phase spread through a dispersion medium. When both are liquids, the colloid is an emulsion:

  • Oil-in-water: milk, vanishing cream.
  • Water-in-oil: butter, body lotion, cold cream.

Emulsifying agents, such as the proteins in milk and butter, keep emulsions stable.

  • Distillation needs a boiling-point gap of at least about 25 °C; fractional distillation handles smaller gaps.
  • Particle sizes: solution under 1 nm, colloid 1 to 1000 nm, suspension over 1000 nm.
  • The Tyndall effect appears in colloids and suspensions, not in solutions.
  • Camphor, naphthalene and dry ice sublime.
  • Blood, milk and ice cream are colloids.
  • Alum acts as a coagulant in water purification.

Q1. Camphor can be separated from sand by: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Sublimation

💡 Explanation: Camphor turns directly into vapour on heating while sand does not, so sublimation works.

Q2. Two miscible liquids with boiling points 56 °C and 100 °C are best separated by: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Distillation

💡 Explanation: Their boiling points differ by more than 25 °C, so simple distillation separates them.

Q3. The Tyndall effect is shown by: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Milk

💡 Explanation: Milk is a colloid, which scatters light; true solutions do not.

Q4. Particle size in a colloid is: medium

✅ Answer: (d) 1 to 1000 nm

💡 Explanation: NCERT gives colloid particles a diameter between 1 and 1000 nm.

Q5. Alum is used in water purification as a: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Coagulant

💡 Explanation: Alum makes fine suspended particles clump together so they settle and can be removed.

Q6. Crude oil is separated into petrol, kerosene and diesel by: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Fractional distillation

💡 Explanation: The fractions have boiling points less than 25 °C apart, which needs fractional distillation.

Q7. Butter is an example of: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Water-in-oil emulsion

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists butter, body lotion and cold cream as water-in-oil emulsions.

Q8. Plasma and red blood cells are separated by: easy

✅ Answer: (d) Centrifugation

💡 Explanation: Spinning at high speed sends the heavier red cells to the bottom of the tube.

How do you tell a colloid from a solution?

Shine a beam of light through it. A colloid such as milk scatters the light and the beam becomes visible (the Tyndall effect), while a true solution does not.

Why can't brass be separated into copper and zinc by filtering or distilling?

Brass is an alloy, a homogeneous mixture of metals formed by melting them together, and NCERT notes that physical methods cannot separate an alloy's components.
