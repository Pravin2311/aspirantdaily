Human Nervous System: CNS and PNS, Neurons, Nerve Impulse, Synapse, and the Parts of the Brain with Their Functions

CNS: brain + spinal cord • Neuron: dendrites in, axon out • Na+ influx = action potential • Forebrain: cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus • Hindbrain: pons, cerebellum, medulla

SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

The body's organs must work together. NCERT explains that the neural system gives quick, point-to-point coordination, while the endocrine system gives slower chemical integration through hormones. Neural systems grow more complex through the animal kingdom: Hydra has a simple network of neurons, insects have a brain with ganglia, and vertebrates have the most developed system.

A neuron has three parts:

  • Cell body: cytoplasm, organelles and Nissl's granules.
  • Dendrites: short branching fibres, also with Nissl's granules, that carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: a long fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body. Its branches end in synaptic knobs holding vesicles of neurotransmitters.

Myelinated fibres are wrapped by Schwann cells that form a myelin sheath. The gaps between sheaths are the nodes of Ranvier. These fibres are found in spinal and cranial nerves. In unmyelinated fibres, the Schwann cell does not form a sheath; they are common in the autonomous and somatic neural systems.

  1. Resting state: the membrane is more permeable to K+ than to Na+. The sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in. The outside becomes positive and the inside negative. This is the resting potential, and the membrane is polarised.
  2. Stimulus: the membrane at that spot becomes freely permeable to Na+. Na+ rushes in and the polarity reverses, so the membrane is depolarised. This potential is the action potential, or nerve impulse.
  3. Conduction: current flows to the next site, reverses its polarity in turn, and the impulse moves along the axon.
  4. Recovery: within a fraction of a second K+ diffuses out, the resting potential returns, and the fibre can respond again.

Synapses pass impulses between neurons. At an electrical synapse, current flows directly; this is always faster but rare in humans. At a chemical synapse, a fluid-filled synaptic cleft separates the neurons. Vesicles release neurotransmitters, which bind receptors on the next neuron and open ion channels. The new potential may be excitatory or inhibitory.

What the brain controls: NCERT lists voluntary movements, the balance of the body, vital involuntary organs such as the lungs, heart and kidneys, thermoregulation, hunger and thirst, the circadian (24-hour) rhythms of the body, several endocrine glands and human behaviour. It is also where vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions and thoughts are processed.

The brain is the body's "command and control system", protected by the skull and three meninges: the outer dura mater, the thin middle arachnoid and the inner pia mater, which touches the brain tissue.

Brain stem = midbrain + pons + medulla oblongata. It connects the brain to the spinal cord.

  • Cerebrum vs cerebellum: the cerebrum is the forebrain's largest part, for higher functions. The cerebellum is in the hindbrain and integrates input from the semicircular canals.
  • Thalamus vs hypothalamus: the thalamus relays sensory and motor signals. The hypothalamus, at its base, controls temperature, hunger and thirst, and links to the endocrine system.
  • Afferent vs efferent: afferent fibres go to the CNS; efferent fibres go from it.
  • Dendrite vs axon: dendrites bring impulses towards the cell body; axons carry them away.
  • Grey vs white matter: grey matter is the outer cortex of cell bodies. White matter is the inner myelinated fibres.
  • Na+ vs K+: Na+ influx creates the action potential. K+ outflow restores the resting state.

Q1. Which part of the brain controls respiration and cardiovascular reflexes? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Medulla oblongata

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the medulla contains centres that control respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions.

Q2. The two cerebral hemispheres are connected by: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Corpus callosum

💡 Explanation: A tract of nerve fibres called the corpus callosum connects the left and right hemispheres.

Q3. An action potential is generated mainly by a rapid: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Inflow of Na+

💡 Explanation: A stimulus makes the membrane freely permeable to Na+, and the rapid Na+ influx reverses polarity. K+ outflow then restores the resting potential.

Q4. Bipolar neurons are found in the: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Retina of the eye

💡 Explanation: NCERT says bipolar neurons, with one axon and one dendrite, are found in the retina. Multipolar neurons are in the cerebral cortex.

Q5. Hunger, thirst and body temperature are controlled by the: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Hypothalamus

💡 Explanation: The hypothalamus, at the base of the thalamus, has centres that control body temperature and the urge to eat and drink.

What are the main parts of brain and functions in short?

Forebrain: cerebrum for thinking, memory and senses, thalamus for relaying signals, hypothalamus for temperature, hunger and thirst. Midbrain: visual and auditory inputs. Hindbrain: pons, cerebellum and medulla for breathing and heart reflexes.

Which is the largest part of the human brain?

The cerebrum. NCERT says it forms the major part of the human brain and is split into left and right hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum.

What is the difference between grey matter and white matter?

Grey matter is the cerebral cortex, grey because neuron cell bodies are concentrated there. White matter lies inside and is made of nerve fibre tracts covered with myelin, which looks opaque white.
