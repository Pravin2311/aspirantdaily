Light: Reflection, Refraction, Concave and Convex Mirrors and Lenses, with Image Tables and Formulas

R = 2f • Mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f • Lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f • n = c/v • P = 1/f (dioptre) • Concave mirror: torches, shaving, dentists • Convex mirror: rear-view

A spherical mirror is part of a sphere. If the reflecting surface curves inwards, towards the centre of the sphere, it is concave. If it bulges outwards, it is convex. The inside of a spoon behaves like a concave mirror and the back like a convex one. Exam questions on mirror uses almost always come back to what kind of image each mirror gives.

A concave mirror held towards the Sun focuses sunlight into a bright spot that can burn paper. That spot is the Sun's image at the principal focus, and its distance from the mirror is roughly the focal length.

Terms: the pole (P) is the centre of the mirror's surface. The centre of curvature (C) is the centre of the sphere: in front of a concave mirror and behind a convex one. The radius of curvature (R) is PC. The principal axis passes through P and C. The principal focus (F) is where parallel rays meet (concave) or appear to come from (convex), and the focal length (f) is PF. For small apertures, R = 2f, so F lies midway between P and C.

A convex mirror has just two cases. With the object at infinity, the image is at F behind the mirror, point-sized. For any other position, the image is between P and F behind the mirror and diminished. Both images are virtual and erect.

Ray rules for drawing any image: a ray parallel to the axis reflects through F (or appears to come from F); a ray through F reflects parallel to the axis; a ray through C returns along its own path; a ray striking P reflects at an equal angle on the other side of the axis.

NCERT uses the New Cartesian Sign Convention. The pole is the origin and the principal axis is the x-axis. The object is always on the left. Distances to the right of the origin are positive and to the left negative. Heights above the axis are positive and below negative. So for a concave mirror f is negative, and for a convex mirror f is positive.

  • Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, valid for all spherical mirrors and object positions.
  • Magnification: m = h′/h = −v/u. A negative m means a real (inverted) image; a positive m means virtual (erect). |m| above 1 means enlarged.

Worked example 1: rear-view mirror (NCERT 9.1)

R = +3.00 m, so f = +1.50 m; a bus is at u = −5.00 m. 1/v = 1/1.5 − 1/(−5) gives v ≈ +1.15 m, behind the mirror. m = −v/u ≈ +0.23: virtual, erect, smaller.

Worked example 2: screen distance for a concave mirror (NCERT 9.2)

f = −15 cm, u = −25 cm. 1/v = 1/(−15) − 1/(−25) gives v = −37.5 cm: put the screen 37.5 cm in front. A 4 cm object gives h′ = −6 cm, so the image is real, inverted and enlarged.

A pencil looks bent in water, a pond looks shallower, and letters under a glass slab look raised. These are effects of refraction: light changes direction when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another, because its speed changes.

  • Laws of refraction: the incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in one plane; and sin i / sin r is constant for a given colour and pair of media (Snell's law). That constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first.
  • Glass slab: the ray bends towards the normal entering glass and away leaving it, so it emerges parallel to the incident ray, shifted sideways.
  • Refractive index: n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in air and v the speed in the medium. Light is fastest in vacuum, at 3 × 108 m/s.
  • Rarer to denser: light slows and bends towards the normal. Denser to rarer: it speeds up and bends away.

Worked example 3: speed of light in glass

For glass of n = 1.50, v = c/n = 3 × 108 / 1.5 = 2 × 108 m/s.

A convex lens is thicker in the middle and converges light. A concave lens is thicker at the edges and diverges it. A ray through the optical centre (O) passes undeviated. A lens has two foci, F1 and F2.

A concave lens, like a convex mirror, always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image on the object's side, between F1 and O (point-sized at F1 for an object at infinity).

  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, measured from O. Convex f is positive; concave f is negative.
  • Magnification: m = h′/h = v/u (no minus sign, unlike mirrors).
  • Power: P = 1/f with f in metres, in dioptres (D). 1 D is the power of a lens of focal length 1 m. Convex power is positive; concave power is negative.

Worked example 4: convex lens image (NCERT 9.4)

f = +10 cm, u = −15 cm. 1/v = 1/10 + 1/(−15) gives v = +30 cm: a real image on the far side. m = v/u = −2, so a 2 cm object gives a 4 cm inverted image.

Worked example 5: lens power

A convex lens of focal length 0.5 m has P = 1/0.5 = +2 D. An optician's "+2.0 D" therefore means a convex lens of focal length 50 cm.

Q1. Which mirror do dentists use to see enlarged images of teeth? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Concave mirror

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists dentists among the users of concave mirrors. With the tooth between the pole and focus, a concave mirror gives an enlarged, erect image.

Q2. Why is a convex mirror preferred as a rear-view mirror in vehicles? easy

✅ Answer: (a) It gives an erect image and a wider field of view

💡 Explanation: NCERT says convex mirrors always give an erect, though diminished, image and, being curved outwards, show a much larger area than a plane mirror.

Q3. The radius of curvature of a spherical mirror is 20 cm. Its focal length is: easy

✅ Answer: (d) 10 cm

💡 Explanation: For small-aperture spherical mirrors R = 2f, so f = R/2 = 20/2 = 10 cm. This is NCERT intext question 2.

Q4. Where must an object be placed for a convex lens to form a real image of the same size? medium

✅ Answer: (b) At 2F1

💡 Explanation: NCERT's Table 9.4 shows that an object at 2F1 gives a real, inverted, same-size image at 2F2 on the other side of the lens.

Q5. Light passes from air into glass of refractive index 1.50. What is its speed in the glass? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 2 × 10⁸ m/s

💡 Explanation: n = c/v, so v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.5 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s. Light slows down in the optically denser glass.

What are the uses of concave and convex mirrors?

Concave mirrors are used in torches, search-lights and headlights for strong parallel beams, as shaving mirrors, by dentists and in solar furnaces. Convex mirrors are used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles because they give an erect image over a wide field of view.

Which mirror always forms a virtual image?

A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image, whatever the object's position. A plane mirror's image is also always virtual. A concave mirror forms a virtual image only when the object is between the pole and the focus.

What is the difference between the mirror formula and the lens formula?

The mirror formula is 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, and the lens formula is 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Both use the New Cartesian Sign Convention, measuring from the pole for mirrors and from the optical centre for lenses.
