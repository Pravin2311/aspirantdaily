Melting and Recasting Problems: Equate Volumes to Count Pieces, Find a New Radius, or Draw a Wire

Volume is conserved • Number of pieces = big volume ÷ small volume • Several solids into one • Drawing wire • Wastage • How surface area changes

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

When a solid is melted and recast, its shape changes but the amount of metal does not. So every problem in this chapter rests on one equation:

Volume before = volume after

Number of pieces = volume of the big solid ÷ volume of one small piece

SolidVolume
Cube (side a)a³
Cuboidl × b × h
Cylinderπr²h
Cone(1/3)πr²h
Sphere(4/3)πr³
Hemisphere(2/3)πr³

Write both volumes, cancel π and common numbers, and only then multiply out. For the full formulas, see cylinder, cone and sphere and cube and cuboid.

When both shapes are the same kind (sphere into spheres, cube into cubes), the count is just the ratio of sizes cubed.

Worked example 1: one sphere into small spheres

A sphere of radius 6 cm becomes spheres of radius 2 cm. Count = (6/2)³ = 27.

When the shapes differ, write both volumes.

Worked example 2: cylinder into cones

A cylinder of radius 3 cm and height 10 cm is recast into cones of radius 1 cm and height 3 cm. Cylinder = π × 9 × 10 = 90π. Each cone = (1/3)π × 1 × 3 = π. Count = 90.

Wastage changes the usable volume. Apply the loss first, then divide, and round down because only whole pieces count.

Worked example 3: 10% lost in melting

A cube of side 10 cm is melted with 10% wastage and cast into cubes of side 3 cm. Usable volume = 900 cm³; each small cube is 27 cm³; 900 ÷ 27 = 33.3, so 33 cubes can be made.

Sometimes the question asks for one dimension of the new solid. Set the volumes equal and solve.

Worked example 4: three cubes into one

Cubes of sides 3, 4 and 5 cm are melted into a single cube. Total volume = 27 + 64 + 125 = 216 cm³, so the new side is 6 cm.

Worked example 5: sphere into cylinder

A sphere of radius 9 cm is recast into a cylinder of radius 6 cm. (4/3)π × 729 = π × 36 × h, so h = 972 ÷ 36 = 27 cm.

For a hollow solid, only the shell is metal. Its volume is the outer volume minus the inner one: (4/3)π(R³ − r³) for a hollow sphere, or π(R² − r²)h for a pipe.

Worked example 6: hollow sphere into a solid one

A hollow sphere has outer radius 5 cm and inner radius 3 cm. Melted into a solid sphere, (4/3)π(125 − 27) = (4/3)πr³, so r³ = 98 and r ≈ 4.61 cm. The answer is not 5 − 3 = 2 cm; the shell holds far more metal than its thickness suggests.

A hemisphere holds half a sphere's volume, (2/3)πr³. Bowl-into-bottle questions use this.

Worked example 7: emptying a hemispherical bowl

A hemispherical bowl of radius 6 cm is full of liquid, poured into cylindrical bottles of radius 1 cm and height 4 cm. Bowl = (2/3)π × 216 = 144π; each bottle = 4π. The bowl fills 36 bottles. Liquid questions follow exactly the same volume rule as metal.

A wire is a very long, thin cylinder. Its length is the melted volume divided by the area of its cross-section, πr². Watch units: if the radius is in millimetres and the answer is wanted in metres, convert carefully.

Worked example 8: wire from a sphere

A sphere of radius 3 cm is drawn into wire of radius 0.1 cm. Volume = (4/3)π × 27 = 36π. Length = 36π ÷ (π × 0.01) = 3,600 cm = 36 m.

Volume is conserved, surface area is not. Splitting a solid into smaller pieces always increases the total surface area.

Worked example 9: one sphere into 27

From example 1, 27 spheres of radius 2 replace one of radius 6. Total surface area ratio = 27 × 2² ÷ 6² = 3, so the small spheres have three times the surface area. In general, splitting into k equal spheres multiplies the surface area by the cube root of k.

Common traps: using diameter as radius, forgetting the 1/3 for cones, squaring instead of cubing when comparing spheres, mixing cm and m in wire questions, and rounding up the number of pieces after wastage.

Q1. A metal sphere of radius 8 cm is melted and recast into small spheres of radius 2 cm. How many small spheres are made? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 64

💡 Explanation: Number = (big radius ÷ small radius)³ = 4³ = 64, because the 4/3 π cancels.

Q2. A cone of radius 6 cm and height 24 cm is melted into a sphere. What is the sphere's radius? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 6 cm

💡 Explanation: Cone volume = (1/3)π × 36 × 24 = 288π. Setting (4/3)πr³ = 288π gives r³ = 216, so r = 6 cm.

Q3. Cubes of sides 6 cm, 8 cm and 10 cm are melted into one cube. What is its side? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 12 cm

💡 Explanation: 216 + 512 + 1,000 = 1,728 cm³, and the cube root of 1,728 is 12 cm.

Q4. A cylinder of radius 6 cm and height 8 cm is melted into spheres of radius 2 cm. How many spheres are made? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 27

💡 Explanation: Cylinder = π × 36 × 8 = 288π; each sphere = (4/3)π × 8 = 32π/3. 288π ÷ (32π/3) = 27.

Q5. A sphere of radius 6 cm is melted and recast into a cylinder of radius 4 cm. What is the cylinder's height? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 18 cm

💡 Explanation: (4/3)π × 216 = 288π. Dividing by π × 16 gives a height of 18 cm.

Why does π usually cancel?

Spheres, cylinders and cones all have π in their volume formulas. When one is melted into another, π appears on both sides of the equation and cancels, so you rarely need to use 22/7 or 3.14.

Does surface area stay the same after recasting?

No. Only volume is conserved. Breaking one sphere into many small ones increases the total surface area, which is why fine powders react faster than lumps.

How do I handle a hollow shape such as a pipe?

Its volume is the outer volume minus the inner volume: π(R² − r²)h for a pipe, or (4/3)π(R³ − r³) for a hollow sphere. Use that as the melted volume.
