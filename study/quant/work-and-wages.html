Work and Wages Problems: Share the Payment in the Ratio of Work Done, Including Helpers and People Who Leave

Pay follows work done • Two workers together: ratio of efficiencies • A helper's share • A worker who leaves early • Total pay from one share • Daily wages and total cost

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

When a group is paid a lump sum for a job, each person's share is the fraction of the job that person did. That single rule solves almost every question of this type:

Share = total pay × (fraction of the work done by that person)

The fraction of work done is days worked ÷ days that person would need alone. For the basic time and work method, including taking the total work as an LCM, see time and work by the LCM method.

A common mistake is to share the pay by the number of days, or in the ratio of the days each person needs alone. The faster worker needs fewer days but does more work, so that ratio is upside down.

If two people work side by side for the whole job, they work the same number of days, so the shares are in the ratio of their efficiencies (work per day).

Worked example 1: the efficiency ratio

A can finish a job in 10 days and B in 15. Per day A does 1/10 and B does 1/15, a ratio of 3 : 2. Equivalently, with total work 30 units, A does 3 units a day and B 2.

Worked example 2: splitting ₹1,500

They finish together and are paid ₹1,500. A's share is 3/5 × 1,500 = ₹900 and B's is ₹600. Note that the ratio of days alone, 10 : 15, is 2 : 3, the reverse of the pay ratio.

Questions often add a third person, C, whose speed is not given. Work out what A and B did in the actual time; whatever remains is C's part.

Worked example 3: A's share with a helper

A and B can do a job in 12 and 16 days. With C's help they finish in 4 days and are paid ₹2,400. In 4 days A does 4/12 = 1/3 of the job, so A gets ₹800. B does 4/16 = 1/4 and gets ₹600.

Worked example 4: C's share

C did the rest: 1 − 1/3 − 1/4 = 5/12 of the job, so C gets 5/12 × 2,400 = ₹1,000. The three shares add up to 800 + 600 + 1,000 = 2,400, which confirms the answer.

You do not need C's speed alone to answer, though you could find it: C did 5/12 in 4 days, so C alone would need 4 × 12/5 = 9.6 days.

If one worker leaves partway through, that person's work is just their days on the job divided by their time alone. The remaining worker did everything else.

Worked example 5: A leaves after 6 days

A and B can do a job in 20 and 30 days. They work together for 6 days; then A leaves and B finishes. The job pays ₹5,000. A did 6/20 = 3/10 of the work, so A gets ₹1,500 and B gets ₹3,500.

You never need the total time in this kind of question. Only the work done by the person who left matters, because the other worker gets the rest.

Worked example 6: total pay from one share

If a worker who did 1/5 of a job received ₹600, the whole job paid 600 ÷ 1/5 = ₹3,000.

Some questions give a daily wage instead of a lump sum. Then find how long the job takes, and multiply by everyone's daily wage combined.

Worked example 7: hiring both workers

A charges ₹300 a day and needs 10 days; B charges ₹400 a day and needs 6 days. Alone, A costs ₹3,000 and B ₹2,400. Together they need 1 ÷ (1/10 + 1/6) = 3.75 days at ₹700 a day, a total of ₹2,625. Hiring B alone is cheapest, even though B's daily rate is higher.

Comparing total cost, not daily rate, is the point of such questions. A faster, dearer worker can be the cheaper choice.

  • Sharing in the ratio of days alone. 10 days and 15 days means a pay ratio of 3 : 2, not 2 : 3.
  • Sharing by days worked when speeds differ. Use work done, not time spent.
  • Forgetting the helper. If A and B did less than the whole job, someone else did the rest and gets paid for it.
  • Not checking the total. All shares must add up to the full payment.
  • Comparing daily rates. Compare the total cost of the job instead.

Q1. A can do a job in 6 days and B in 9 days. Working together they finish it and are paid ₹2,000. What is A's share? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹1,200

💡 Explanation: Working the same number of days, they share in the ratio of efficiencies, 1/6 : 1/9 = 3 : 2. A gets 3/5 × 2,000 = ₹1,200.

Q2. A and B can do a job in 15 and 10 days. With C's help they finish it in 5 days and are paid ₹4,500. What is C's share? medium

✅ Answer: (b) ₹750

💡 Explanation: In 5 days A does 1/3 and B does 1/2 of the job, so C does 1/6. C gets 4,500/6 = ₹750.

Q3. A and B can do a job in 20 and 30 days. They work together for 6 days, then A leaves and B finishes. The pay is ₹3,000. What is A's share? medium

✅ Answer: (d) ₹900

💡 Explanation: In 6 days A does 6/20 = 3/10 of the work. A gets 3/10 × 3,000 = ₹900, and B gets the rest.

Q4. A and B can do a job in 12 and 18 days. With C they finish in 6 days for ₹5,400. How much does A get? easy

✅ Answer: (a) ₹2,700

💡 Explanation: In 6 days A does 6/12 = 1/2 of the job, so A gets half of ₹5,400, which is ₹2,700.

Q5. C did 3/10 of a job and was paid ₹900 as his share. What was the total payment for the job? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹3,000

💡 Explanation: Pay is proportional to work done, so the total is 900 ÷ 3/10 = ₹3,000.

Are wages shared by the number of days worked?

Only if everyone works at the same rate. In general, pay is shared in the ratio of work done. Two people who work the same number of days but at different speeds earn different amounts.

How do I find a helper's share?

Work out the fraction of the job each named worker did in the given days. Whatever is left was done by the helper, and the helper's share of the pay is that same fraction.

What if wages are given per day?

Then the total cost is the number of days multiplied by the combined daily wage of everyone working. Find the time first, then multiply.
