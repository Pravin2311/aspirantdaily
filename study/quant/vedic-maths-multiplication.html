Fast Multiplication Tricks for Exams: Multiplying by 11, 25 and 99, Numbers Near 100 and Squares Ending in 5

× 11 by adding neighbours • × 25 as ÷ 4 • × 99 as × 100 minus once • Near-base method below, above and across 100 • Base 50 • Squares ending in 5

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Simplification and data-interpretation questions in SSC and bank papers are full of multiplications that are slow by the long method but quick with the right shortcut. Each shortcut below is ordinary algebra in disguise, so you can always check it, and each one only pays off for numbers of the right shape. Learn when to reach for each trick, not just how.

A quick way to choose: look at the multiplier first. If it is 11, 25, 50, 99 or 125, use the matching rule in the next section. If both numbers sit close to 100, 1,000 or 50, use the near-base method. If the number ends in 5 and is being squared, use the last rule. Anything else is usually faster by ordinary column multiplication or by splitting one number, such as 38 × 7 = 40 × 7 − 2 × 7 = 266.

For order-of-operations questions where these products appear, see simplification and BODMAS.

× 11: for a two-digit number ab, write a, then a + b, then b. If a + b is 10 or more, carry the 1 into the first digit.

Worked example 1: 53 × 11

5, then 5 + 3 = 8, then 3: 583.

Worked example 2: 87 × 11, with a carry

8, then 8 + 7 = 15, then 7. Carry the 1 into the 8: 957.

For longer numbers, keep the first and last digits and add each neighbouring pair in between, working right to left so carries move left: 2,341 × 11 = 25,751.

× 25: 25 is 100 ÷ 4, so multiply by 100 and divide by 4.

Worked example 3: 48 × 25

4,800 ÷ 4 = 1200. The same idea gives × 50 as × 100 ÷ 2, and × 125 as × 1,000 ÷ 8.

× 99: 99 is 100 − 1, so multiply by 100 and subtract the number once. Similarly, × 999 is × 1,000 minus once.

Worked example 4: 47 × 99

4,700 − 47 = 4653.

When both numbers are close to 100 (or 1,000), work with how far each is from the base.

  1. Write each number's difference from the base: negative if below, positive if above.
  2. Left part: add one number and the other's difference (cross-add).
  3. Right part: multiply the two differences. It must fill as many places as the base has zeros: two for 100, three for 1,000.

Worked example 5: 97 × 94, both below 100

Differences −3 and −6. Left: 97 − 6 = 91. Right: 3 × 6 = 18. Answer 9118.

Worked example 6: 104 × 108, both above 100

Differences +4 and +8. Left: 104 + 8 = 112. Right: 4 × 8 = 32. Answer 11232.

Worked example 7: 103 × 98, one each side

Differences +3 and −2. Left: 103 − 2 = 101, giving 10,100. Right: 3 × (−2) = −6, so subtract: 10094.

Worked example 8: 996 × 993, base 1,000

Differences −4 and −7. Left: 996 − 7 = 989. Right: 4 × 7 = 28, written as 028 to fill three places. Answer 989028.

For numbers near 50, use 50 as a working base: cross-add as usual, multiply the left part by 50 (instead of 100), then add the product of the differences.

Worked example 9: 48 × 46, base 50

Differences −2 and −4. Left: 48 − 4 = 44, and 44 × 50 = 2,200. Right: 2 × 4 = 8. Answer 2208.

For a number ending in 5, multiply the part before the 5 by the next whole number, then write 25 after it.

Worked example 10: 75²

7 × 8 = 56, then append 25: 5625. It works because (10n + 5)² = 100n(n + 1) + 25.

The same rule gives 105² = 10 × 11 | 25 = 11,025. For other squares and roots, see square roots and cube roots.

  • Forgetting the carry with × 11. 87 × 11 is 957, not 8157.
  • Too few places in the right part. With base 100, 2 × 4 must be written 08.
  • Adding a negative product. For one number above and one below, the right part is subtracted.
  • Using the near-base method on distant numbers. 63 × 78 is faster done directly.
  • Not checking. The unit digit of the product must match the unit digit of the product of the unit digits.

Q1. What is 64 × 11? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 704

💡 Explanation: Write 6 _ 4 and put 6 + 4 = 10 in the middle. Carry the 1 to the 6: 704.

Q2. What is 96 × 98? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 9408

💡 Explanation: Deficits from 100 are 4 and 2. Left part: 96 − 2 = 94. Right part: 4 × 2 = 08. Answer 9408.

Q3. What is 36 × 25? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 900

💡 Explanation: Multiplying by 25 is multiplying by 100 and dividing by 4: 3,600 ÷ 4 = 900.

Q4. What is 107 × 105? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 11235

💡 Explanation: Surpluses over 100 are 7 and 5. Left part: 107 + 5 = 112. Right part: 7 × 5 = 35. Answer 11235.

Q5. What is 85²? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 7225

💡 Explanation: For a square ending in 5, multiply the leading digit by the next number, 8 × 9 = 72, and append 25: 7225.

Are these tricks the same as Vedic maths?

The near-base method and the squares-ending-in-5 rule are often taught under the name Vedic maths. Whatever the label, each trick on this page is ordinary algebra, and each worked example is checked against direct multiplication.

Why does the near-base method work?

For numbers 100 − a and 100 − b, the product is 100(100 − a − b) + ab. The left part, 100 − a − b, is either number minus the other's deficit, and ab fills the last two places.

When should I not use a trick?

When the numbers are far from a convenient base, the trick takes longer than ordinary multiplication. Use the base method only when both numbers are within about 10 of 100, 1,000 or 50.
