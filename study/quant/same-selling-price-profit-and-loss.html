Same Selling Price, Profit x% and Loss x%: Why There Is Always a Loss of x²/100 %

Overall loss = x²/100 % • Why it is never zero • Loss in rupees • Total cost from the loss • Unequal percentages • Same cost price is different

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Suppose two articles are sold at the same selling price. One is sold at x% profit and the other at x% loss. It feels as if the gain and the loss should cancel, but they never do:

Overall result = a loss of x²/100 %

This is the most tested shortcut in same selling price profit loss percentage questions, and it takes seconds once you trust it. The rest of this page shows why it holds, how to get a rupee figure, and when it does not apply. For general profit and loss formulas, see profit and loss formulas.

Worked example 1: 10% each way, SP ₹990

The profitable item cost 990/1.1 = 900; the loss-making item cost 990/0.9 = 1,100. Total cost 2,000, total sale 1,980, so the loss is 20 on 2,000 = 1%. The rule gives 10²/100 = 1%.

Take the common selling price as S. The cost prices are S/(1 + x/100) and S/(1 − x/100). The item sold at a loss must have cost more, because it was sold below cost, while the profitable one cost less. So the x% loss is taken on a bigger amount than the x% profit, and the loss wins.

Adding the two costs and comparing with the total sale 2S gives a loss of exactly x²/100 percent of the total cost. The selling price cancels out, which is why S never appears in the answer.

Worked example 2: 25% each way

x²/100 = 625/100 = 6.25% loss. Computed from the costs with any selling price, the answer is the same.

The loss grows quickly with x: 10% each way costs 1%, 20% costs 4%, and 30% costs 9%. Small percentages give losses so small that options like "no profit, no loss" are tempting traps.

When the question asks for the loss in rupees, either find both cost prices or apply x²/100 % to the total cost.

Worked example 3: two items at ₹4,800 each

20% profit on one and 20% loss on the other. Costs: 4,800/1.2 = 4,000 and 4,800/0.8 = 6,000, total 10,000. Sale 9,600, so the loss is ₹400, which is 4% of 10,000 as the rule predicts.

Turned around, a known rupee loss gives the total cost price directly: total cost = loss ÷ (x²/100 %).

Worked example 4: loss of ₹45 at 15% each way

The loss is 2.25% of the total cost, so the total cost is 45 ÷ 0.0225 = ₹2,000.

If the profit and loss percentages are different, the shortcut no longer applies. Work out each cost price from the common selling price and compare totals.

Worked example 5: 20% profit and 10% loss, SP ₹1,080

Costs: 1,080/1.2 = 900 and 1,080/0.9 = 1,200, total 2,100. Sale 2,160, a gain of 60, which is about 2.86% profit. A bigger profit percentage can outweigh the loss.

Worked example 6: the break-even loss

One item is sold at 20% profit. What loss on the other, at the same price, gives no overall profit or loss? The two costs must add to 2S: S/1.2 + S/(1 − y) = 2S, so 1/(1 − y) = 7/6 and y = 1/7, about 14.29%. Any smaller loss gives an overall profit.

The x²/100 rule needs equal selling prices. With equal cost prices, an x% profit and an x% loss cancel exactly.

Worked example 7: both cost ₹500

One sells for 550 (10% profit) and the other for 450 (10% loss). Total sale 1,000 equals total cost 1,000: 0 profit or loss.

Before applying any shortcut, underline which price is common in the question. For chained percentage changes, which follow a related formula, see successive percentage change.

  • Answering "no profit, no loss". With the same selling price there is always a loss.
  • Using the rule for equal cost prices. Then the profit and loss cancel.
  • Answering x% or 2x%. The loss is x²/100 %, so 10% each way gives 1%, not 10% or 20%.
  • Applying the loss % to the selling price. x²/100 % is a share of the total cost price.
  • Using the shortcut for unequal percentages. Work out both cost prices instead.

Q1. Two watches are sold for the same price. One is sold at a 12% profit and the other at a 12% loss. What is the overall result? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 1.44% loss

💡 Explanation: Equal profit and loss percentages at the same selling price always give a loss of x²/100 % = 144/100 = 1.44%.

Q2. Two scooters are sold for ₹9,600 each, one at a 20% gain and the other at a 20% loss. What is the overall loss in rupees? medium

✅ Answer: (a) ₹800

💡 Explanation: Cost prices: 9,600/1.2 = 8,000 and 9,600/0.8 = 12,000, total 20,000. Total sale 19,200, so the loss is ₹800 (4% of 20,000).

Q3. Two plots are sold at the same price, one at a 30% profit and the other at a 30% loss. What is the overall loss percentage? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 9%

💡 Explanation: With equal percentages at the same selling price, the loss is x²/100 = 900/100 = 9%.

Q4. A trader sells two items at the same price, gaining 12% on one and losing 12% on the other. His total loss is ₹72. What was the total cost price? medium

✅ Answer: (b) ₹5,000

💡 Explanation: The loss is 1.44% of the total cost, so cost = 72 ÷ 0.0144 = ₹5,000.

Q5. Two articles have the same cost price. One is sold at a 25% profit and the other at a 25% loss. What is the overall profit or loss percentage? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 0%

💡 Explanation: With equal cost prices, the gain of 25% on one exactly cancels the loss of 25% on the other. The x²/100 rule applies only when the selling prices are equal.

Why is the result always a loss and never a profit?

For the same selling price, the item sold at a loss must have cost more than the item sold at a profit. So the loss is a percentage of a bigger cost and outweighs the profit, whatever x is.

Does the selling price matter for the percentage?

No. The overall loss percentage is x²/100 % for any common selling price. The selling price only matters when the question asks for the loss in rupees.

What if the cost prices are equal instead?

Then x% profit on one and x% loss on the other cancel exactly, giving no profit and no loss. Read the question to see which price is common.
