Heights and Distances: Angle of Elevation and Depression Problems with 30°, 45° and 60°

Elevation vs depression • One right triangle • Moving observer • Two buildings • Shadow length • Ready values of √3

SSC Railway Defence 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Every question in this chapter is a right triangle in disguise. The height is the vertical side, the distance along the ground is the horizontal side, and the line of sight is the hypotenuse.

  • Angle of elevation: the angle between the horizontal and the line of sight when you look up at an object.
  • Angle of depression: the angle between the horizontal and the line of sight when you look down. It equals the angle of elevation measured from the object back to you, so you can always place it at ground level.

Almost every exam question uses 30°, 45° or 60°. Keep these values ready:

AnglesincostanHeight : base
30°1/2√3/21/√3 ≈ 0.5771 : √3
45°1/√21/√211 : 1
60°√3/21/2√3 ≈ 1.732√3 : 1

The last column is the quickest tool: at 60° the height is √3 times the base, at 30° the base is √3 times the height, and at 45° they are equal. For the full table, see trigonometric ratios.

Worked example 1: tower from the ground

From 20 m away, the top of a tower is seen at 60°. tan 60° = h/20, so h = 20√3 ≈ 34.64 m.

Worked example 2: a kite string

A kite is flying on a 100 m string that makes 30° with the ground. The string is the hypotenuse, so height = 100 × sin 30° = 50 m.

Worked example 3: angle of depression

From the top of a 50 m cliff, a boat is seen at an angle of depression of 30°. Move the angle to the boat: tan 30° = 50/d, so d = 50√3 ≈ 86.6 m.

When the observer walks a distance d towards the object and the angle rises from α to β, the height is:

h = d × tan α × tan β ÷ (tan β − tan α)

It comes from writing the two ground distances, h/tan α and h/tan β, and setting their difference equal to d. For the common pair 30° and 60°, tan α × tan β = 1 and tan β − tan α = 2/√3, so the formula shortens to h = d√3/2, and the observer's final distance from the tower is d/2.

Worked example 4: 30° to 60°

The angle rises from 30° to 60° after walking 40 m. h = 40√3/2 = 20√3 ≈ 34.64 m, and the observer ends 20 m from the tower.

The same algebra works for two ships seen from one tower, where the gap between the ships replaces d.

When you look from the top of one building at another, draw a horizontal line from your eye. It splits the taller building into two parts: the part below the line equals your own height, and the part above is found from the angle of elevation.

Worked example 5: tower seen from a roof

From the top of a 12 m building, the top of a tower is at 45° elevation and its foot at 30° depression. The gap between the buildings is 12/tan 30° = 12√3 ≈ 20.78 m. The tower rises 20.78 × tan 45° = 20.78 m above eye level, so its height is 12 + 20.78 ≈ 32.78 m.

Shadows work the same way, with the sun's altitude as the angle of elevation. As the sun sinks, the shadow lengthens.

Worked example 6: a lengthening shadow

A 24 m tower's shadow is measured when the sun is at 60° and again at 30°. The shadows are 24/√3 ≈ 13.86 m and 24√3 ≈ 41.57 m, so the shadow grows by about 27.71 m.

  • Drawing the depression angle at the wrong corner. Move it down to the object on the ground as an angle of elevation before writing tan.
  • Using tan with a slanted length. A ladder or string is the hypotenuse, which needs sin or cos.
  • Forgetting the observer's height. If a person 1.5 m tall sees the top, add 1.5 m to the computed height.
  • Rounding too early. Keep √3 until the end, then use 1.732.
  • Mixing up 30° and 60°. A higher angle always means a taller object or a closer observer.

Q1. From a point 30 m from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of its top is 30°. What is the tower's height? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 17.32 m

💡 Explanation: tan 30° = h/30, so h = 30 ÷ √3 = 10√3 ≈ 17.32 m. 51.96 m would be the answer for 60°.

Q2. A 10 m ladder leans against a wall and makes 60° with the ground. How high up the wall does it reach? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 8.66 m

💡 Explanation: The ladder is the hypotenuse, so height = 10 × sin 60° = 5√3 ≈ 8.66 m. Its foot is 10 × cos 60° = 5 m from the wall.

Q3. From the top of a 75 m lighthouse, the angles of depression of two ships on the same side are 30° and 45°. How far apart are the ships? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 54.9 m

💡 Explanation: Distances are 75/tan 30° = 75√3 ≈ 129.9 m and 75/tan 45° = 75 m. The gap is 75(√3 − 1) ≈ 54.9 m.

Q4. A pole casts a shadow equal to its own height. What is the sun's angle of elevation? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 45°

💡 Explanation: tan θ = height ÷ shadow = 1, so θ = 45°. A shorter shadow means a higher sun.

Q5. Walking 60 m towards a tower, the angle of elevation of its top changes from 30° to 60°. What is the tower's height? hard

✅ Answer: (c) 51.96 m

💡 Explanation: h = d × tan 30° × tan 60° ÷ (tan 60° − tan 30°) = 60 × 1 ÷ (2/√3) = 30√3 ≈ 51.96 m.

Is the angle of depression equal to the angle of elevation?

Yes. The angle of depression from A to B equals the angle of elevation from B to A, because they are alternate angles between the horizontal line at A and the horizontal ground. Exams use this to move the angle into the right triangle.

Should I leave answers in √3 form?

Options often appear as 10√3 or 17.32, so be ready for both. Use √3 ≈ 1.732 and 1/√3 ≈ 0.577; work in surd form until the last step to avoid rounding errors.

When do I use sin instead of tan?

Use tan when you know or want the horizontal distance and the height. Use sin or cos when a slanted length, such as a ladder, kite string or ramp, is involved.
