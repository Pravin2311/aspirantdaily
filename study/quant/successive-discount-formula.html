Successive Discount Formula: Turning Two or Three Discounts into One

Equivalent discount • Three discounts • Finding the second discount • Comparing offers

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-20

A successive discount is a chain of reductions where each new percentage is applied to the price left after the previous one. A shop that advertises "20% off, plus an extra 10% at the counter" does not give 30% off the tag price. It first cuts the tag price by 20%, and then takes 10% off what remains.

Exam questions test whether you can turn such a chain into one equivalent single discount, work backwards to the marked price, or choose the better of two offers. All three tasks become quick once you think of each discount as a multiplier rather than an amount to subtract.

A discount of r% leaves (100 − r)% of the price, so the price is multiplied by (1 − r/100). A 20% discount multiplies by 0.80; a 15% discount multiplies by 0.85; a 5% discount multiplies by 0.95. A chain of discounts is simply a product of these multipliers.

For two discounts of a% and b%, the price kept is (1 − a/100)(1 − b/100). Expanding the brackets gives 1 − a/100 − b/100 + ab/10,000. The fraction removed is therefore a/100 + b/100 − ab/10,000, which, written as a percentage, is:

Equivalent discount = a + b − ab/100

The term ab/100 is the part of the second discount that would have been charged on money already removed by the first. Subtracting it corrects the double-counting. Because the formula is symmetric in a and b, the order of the two discounts never changes the answer.

Worked example 1: 20% and 10%

Equivalent discount = 20 + 10 − (20 × 10)/100 = 30 − 2 = 28%. Check with multipliers: 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72, so 72% of the price is kept and 28% is removed.

The same formula handles price rises if you write an increase as a negative discount, or more simply use the general rule for successive percentage changes: x + y + xy/100, where an increase is positive and a decrease is negative. A 30% rise followed by a 20% cut is 30 − 20 − 6 = a 4% net rise.

With three discounts the expanded formula becomes long, so use the multiplier method directly. Multiply the three retention factors, then subtract the product from 1.

Worked example 2: 10%, 20% and 30%

Price kept = 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.7 = 0.504. The equivalent single discount is 1 − 0.504 = 49.6%, well short of the 60% you would get by adding.

A useful shortcut is to apply the two-discount formula twice. Combine 10% and 20% into 28%, then combine 28% and 30%: 28 + 30 − (28 × 30)/100 = 58 − 8.4 = 49.6%. Both routes agree, and the second one is easier to do in your head when the numbers are round.

When the selling price is given, divide by the product of the multipliers instead of multiplying.

Worked example 3: finding the marked price

A bag sells for ₹1,530 after discounts of 15% and 10%. The price kept is 0.85 × 0.9 = 0.765 of the marked price, so the marked price is 1,530 ÷ 0.765 = ₹2,000.

Worked example 4: finding the selling price

A jacket marked ₹2,500 carries discounts of 20% and 15%. Selling price = 2,500 × 0.8 × 0.85 = 2,000 × 0.85 = ₹1,700.

To find a missing discount, divide the overall retention factor by the known one. If the overall discount is 40% and the first discount is 25%, the second multiplier is 0.60 ÷ 0.75 = 0.80, so the second discount is 20%.

Worked example 5: the missing second discount

Overall discount 40%, first discount 25%. Second multiplier = 0.6 ÷ 0.75 = 0.8, so the second discount is 20%.

Comparing a flat discount with a chain is a common question. Convert the chain to its equivalent single discount and compare the two percentages; the larger one is better for the buyer. To express the difference in rupees, multiply the gap in percentage points by the marked price.

Worked example 6: flat 30% or 20% + 10%?

On a ₹4,000 item, the chain 20% + 10% is a 28% discount (₹1,120 off), while a flat 30% is ₹1,200 off. The flat offer saves the buyer ₹80 more.

Discount questions often combine with mark-up. If a trader marks goods 40% above cost and then allows a 25% discount, the selling price is 1.4 × 0.75 = 1.05 times the cost, a 5% profit. The same multiplier chain works for any sequence of mark-ups and discounts.

Worked example 7: mark-up then discount

Cost ₹100 → marked ₹140 → after 25% discount ₹105. Profit = 5%.

  • ✘ Adding the discounts. Two 50% discounts leave 25% of the price, not zero.
  • ✘ Applying the second discount to the marked price instead of the reduced price.
  • ✘ Dividing by the wrong factor when working backwards. Use the product of all retention factors.
  • ✘ Treating "buy 3, get 1 free" as 33⅓% off. You pay for 3 items and receive 4, so the discount is 1/4 = 25% of the total value.

Q1. What single discount is equivalent to successive discounts of 25% and 20%? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 40%

💡 Explanation: Using a + b − ab/100: 25 + 20 − (25 × 20)/100 = 45 − 5 = 40%. Adding the two (45%) overstates the discount.

Q2. An article marked at ₹1,200 is sold after successive discounts of 10% and 5%. What is the selling price? easy

✅ Answer: (d) ₹1,026

💡 Explanation: Multiply by the retention factors: 1,200 × 0.90 × 0.95 = 1,080 × 0.95 = ₹1,026.

Q3. Three successive discounts of 10% each are equivalent to a single discount of: medium

✅ Answer: (a) 27.1%

💡 Explanation: The price kept is 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729 of the marked price, so the discount is 1 − 0.729 = 27.1%.

Q4. After successive discounts of 20% and 5%, a shirt sells for ₹1,368. What is its marked price? medium

✅ Answer: (c) ₹1,800

💡 Explanation: Selling price = MP × 0.8 × 0.95 = 0.76 MP, so MP = 1,368 ÷ 0.76 = ₹1,800.

Q5. A price is raised by 30% and the new price is then discounted by 20%. What is the net change in price? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 4% increase

💡 Explanation: Treat the rise as +30 and the discount as −20: 30 − 20 + (30 × −20)/100 = 10 − 6 = +4%, an increase.

Q6. Two successive discounts are equivalent to a single discount of 46%. If the first is 10%, what is the second? hard

✅ Answer: (d) 40%

💡 Explanation: The price kept is 0.54 = 0.90 × x, so x = 0.6 and the second discount is 1 − 0.6 = 40%.

Why can't I simply add two successive discounts?

The second discount is taken on a price that is already reduced, so it removes less money than the same percentage of the marked price. The overlap is ab/100, which is why the equivalent discount is a + b − ab/100 rather than a + b.

Does the order of successive discounts matter?

No. Each discount multiplies the price by a factor such as 0.8 or 0.9, and multiplication gives the same result in any order. A 20% then 10% discount and a 10% then 20% discount both leave 72% of the marked price.
