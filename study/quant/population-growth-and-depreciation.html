Population Increase Percentage Formula: P(1 + r/100)ⁿ for Growth, P(1 − r/100)ⁿ for Depreciation, and Backward Cases

Growth: P(1 + r/100)ⁿ • Depreciation: P(1 − r/100)ⁿ • Going back in time • Different rates each year • Birth rate minus death rate • People moving away

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

When a population grows by r% a year, each year's increase is r% of the population at the start of that year, not of the original. That is compounding, so the population increase percentage formula is the compound interest formula in a new setting:

Population after n years = P × (1 + r/100)ⁿ

Depreciation, where a machine, car or building loses r% of its value every year, uses the same idea with a minus sign:

Value after n years = P × (1 − r/100)ⁿ

For the interest version and the rule for half-yearly periods, see compound interest formulas.

Worked example 1: 50,000 at 10% for 2 years

50,000 × 1.1 = 55,000 after one year, and 55,000 × 1.1 = 60,500 after two. Simple addition of 20% would give 60,000, missing the 500 growth on the first year's increase.

Worked example 2: a machine losing 10% a year

A machine costs ₹80,000. After 3 years at 10% depreciation: 80,000 × 0.9³ = 80,000 × 0.729 = ₹58,320.

To find an earlier value, undo each year by dividing.

  • Population n years ago = present ÷ (1 + r/100)ⁿ
  • Original cost before depreciation = present value ÷ (1 − r/100)ⁿ

Worked example 3: population 3 years ago

A town has 66,550 people and has grown by 10% a year. 66,550 ÷ 1.331 = 50,000. Check forwards: 50,000 → 55,000 → 60,500 → 66,550.

Worked example 4: original cost of a scooter

A scooter is worth ₹43,740 after 2 years of 10% depreciation. 43,740 ÷ 0.81 = ₹54,000.

Do not reverse a growth by subtracting the same percentage. A 10% rise followed by a 10% fall does not return to the start, so 66,550 minus 30% is not the population 3 years ago.

When the rate changes, multiply by a separate factor for each year. A rise of r% is the factor (1 + r/100) and a fall of r% is (1 − r/100).

Worked example 5: +10% then +20%

20,000 × 1.1 × 1.2 = 26,400. The order of the two years does not matter, because multiplication can be done in any order.

Worked example 6: +20% then −10%

10,000 × 1.2 × 0.9 = 10,800, a net rise of 8%. The quick check is the successive-change formula: 20 − 10 − 200/100 = 8%. See successive percentage change.

If a question gives a birth rate and a death rate, the growth rate is their difference. Treat the net rate as r and use the usual formula.

Worked example 7: birth rate 4%, death rate 1.5%

The net growth rate is 2.5%. A population of 80,000 becomes 80,000 × 1.025² = 84,050 after 2 years.

When a fixed number of people move away (or arrive) each year, the formula no longer applies because the change is not a percentage. Work year by year: grow first, then subtract the migrants, unless the question says otherwise.

Worked example 8: 5% growth and 2,000 leaving each year

Year 1: 100,000 × 1.05 = 105,000, minus 2,000 = 103,000. Year 2: 103,000 × 1.05 = 108,150, minus 2,000 = 106,150.

Some questions give the start and end values and ask for the rate or the time. Divide the end value by the start value to get the total growth factor, then take the right root or count the steps.

Worked example 9: the growth rate

A population rises from 40,000 to 48,400 in 2 years. The factor is 48,400 ÷ 40,000 = 1.21, and √1.21 = 1.1, so the rate is 10% a year. Squares to recognise: 1.21 (10%), 1.1025 (5%), 1.44 (20%), 1.5625 (25%).

Worked example 10: the number of years

A property worth ₹100,000 depreciates by 10% a year. When is it worth ₹72,900? The factor is 0.729 = 0.9³, so after 3 years: 100,000 × 0.9³ = 72,900. Multiplying 0.9 repeatedly (0.9, 0.81, 0.729, 0.6561) until you hit the factor is quicker than logarithms in an exam.

If the factor is not an exact power, the question probably expects the nearest whole year, or you have misread which value is the start.

  • Adding the percentages. 10% for 2 years is 21%, not 20%.
  • Reversing growth by subtracting. Divide by the growth factor instead.
  • Expecting +x% and −x% to cancel. They leave a net loss of x²/100 %.
  • Adding birth and death rates. The net rate is births minus deaths.
  • Using the formula with a fixed migration figure. Work year by year instead.

Q1. A town's population is 62,500 and grows by 4% a year. What will it be after 2 years? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 67,600

💡 Explanation: 62,500 × 1.04 × 1.04 = 65,000 × 1.04 = 67,600. Adding 8% at once (67,500) ignores growth on the first year's increase.

Q2. A car bought for ₹400,000 loses 20% of its value each year. What is it worth after 2 years? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹256,000

💡 Explanation: 400,000 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 256,000. Subtracting 40% at once (240,000) takes the second year's fall on the original price.

Q3. A village has 79,860 people today. If its population grew by 10% each year, what was it 3 years ago? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 60,000

💡 Explanation: Divide by 1.1 three times: 79,860 ÷ 1.331 = 60,000.

Q4. A population of 50,000 rises by 10% in one year and falls by 10% the next. What is it after 2 years? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 49,500

💡 Explanation: 50,000 × 1.1 × 0.9 = 49,500. Equal percentage rises and falls do not cancel; the net change is −1%.

Q5. A machine is worth ₹25,600 after 3 years of 20% annual depreciation. What did it cost? medium

✅ Answer: (a) ₹50,000

💡 Explanation: Divide by 0.8 three times: 25,600 ÷ 0.512 = ₹50,000.

Is population growth the same as compound interest?

Yes. Each year's growth is calculated on the population at the start of that year, exactly as interest is added to a growing balance. The formula P(1 + r/100)ⁿ is the compound interest amount formula.

How do I find the population some years ago?

Divide instead of multiplying. Population n years ago = present population ÷ (1 + r/100)ⁿ. For depreciation, divide by (1 − r/100)ⁿ.

What if the rate changes each year?

Multiply by a separate factor for each year, such as 1.10 × 1.20 for +10% then +20%. Use a factor below 1, such as 0.90, for a year of decrease.
