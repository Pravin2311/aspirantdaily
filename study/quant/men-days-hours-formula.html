M1D1H1/W1 = M2D2H2/W2: The Chain Formula for Men, Days, Hours and Work

Man-hours as the unit of work • Changing the amount of work • Men, women and boys • Workers joining or leaving • Common traps

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

If one man working one hour is the basic unit of effort, then 12 men working 8 hours a day for 10 days spend 12 × 10 × 8 = 960 man-hours. The same job always needs the same number of man-hours, whoever does it, as long as everyone works at the same rate. That single idea gives the chain formula:

M1 × D1 × H1 ÷ W1 = M2 × D2 × H2 ÷ W2

M = number of workers, D = days, H = hours per day, W = amount of work (walls, metres, chairs, or simply 1 for "the job").

Write down what you know for both groups, leave the unknown, and cross-multiply. If W is the same on both sides, it cancels.

Worked example 1: same job, different team

12 men working 8 hours a day build a wall in 10 days. How long will 20 men take working 6 hours a day? D2 = 12 × 10 × 8 ÷ (20 × 6) = 960 ÷ 120 = 8 days.

Worked example 2: how many workers?

24 men working 9 hours a day finish a job in 20 days. To finish it in 15 days at 8 hours a day, M2 = 24 × 20 × 9 ÷ (15 × 8) = 4,320 ÷ 120 = 36 men.

More work needs proportionally more effort, so W stays in the formula when the two jobs differ in size. A useful check: if the work doubles and everything else stays the same, the days should double.

Worked example 3: digging a longer trench

15 men dig a 30 m trench in 12 days. How many days will 20 men take to dig 60 m? D2 = 15 × 12 × 60 ÷ (30 × 20) = 10,800 ÷ 600 = 18 days. The work doubled, but the extra men cut the time from 24 days to 18.

The formula assumes everyone works equally hard. When a question says "2 men do as much as 3 women", convert everyone into one kind of worker first. Here 1 man = 1.5 women, so 4 men and 6 women are 6 + 6 = 12 women-equivalents.

Worked example 4: mixed team

2 men do the work of 3 women. 4 men and 6 women finish a job in 10 days. How long will 8 men and 3 women take? First team = 12 women-equivalents; second team = 12 + 3 = 15. Days = 12 × 10 ÷ 15 = 8 days.

Choose the smaller unit (women or boys) as the base when you can, so the counts stay whole numbers.

Split the job at the moment the team changes. Find the man-days already spent, subtract from the total, and divide what is left by the new team size.

Worked example 5: workers leave

20 men can finish a job in 30 days. After 10 days, 4 men leave. Work left = 20 × 20 = 400 man-days. The 16 remaining men need 400 ÷ 16 = 25 more days, so the job ends on day 35.

Worked example 6: extra men to meet a deadline

30 men are hired to finish a project in 40 days. After 25 days only half is done. The first half took 30 × 25 = 750 man-days, so the second half also needs 750. With 15 days left, 750 ÷ 15 = 50 men are needed: 20 extra men.

For problems given as "A alone takes x days", the LCM method for time and work is usually faster than man-days.

  • Putting days on the wrong side. More men means fewer days. If your answer grows when men increase, you have inverted something.
  • Dropping H on one side only. Either include hours per day for both groups or for neither.
  • Mixing workers of different speeds. Convert women, boys or machines into one unit before using the formula.
  • Using the full job after a change. When workers leave midway, only the remaining work goes into the second calculation.
  • Forgetting the days already worked. If the question asks for the total time, add the first stretch back.

Q1. 10 men working 6 hours a day finish a job in 12 days. How many days will 8 men take, working 5 hours a day? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 18 days

💡 Explanation: Total work = 10 × 12 × 6 = 720 man-hours. With 8 men at 5 hours a day, 40 man-hours are done daily, so 720 ÷ 40 = 18 days.

Q2. 18 carpenters make 54 chairs in 6 days. How many chairs will 12 carpenters make in 9 days? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 54

💡 Explanation: W2 = 54 × (12 × 9) ÷ (18 × 6) = 54 × 108 ÷ 108 = 54 chairs. The man-days are the same, so the output is the same.

Q3. 3 men do as much work as 5 boys. 6 men and 5 boys finish a job in 12 days. How long will 9 men and 15 boys take? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 6 days

💡 Explanation: One man = 5/3 boys. The first team is 10 + 5 = 15 boys, the second 15 + 15 = 30 boys. Twice the workforce takes half the time: 6 days.

Q4. 45 workers finish a road in 16 days working 8 hours a day. How many workers are needed to finish it in 12 days working 10 hours a day? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 48

💡 Explanation: M2 = 45 × 16 × 8 ÷ (12 × 10) = 5,760 ÷ 120 = 48 workers.

Q5. 24 men can finish a job in 16 days. After 4 days, 6 men leave. In how many days in all is the job finished? hard

✅ Answer: (a) 20 days

💡 Explanation: Work left after 4 days = 24 × 12 = 288 man-days. 18 men take 288 ÷ 18 = 16 more days, so the total is 4 + 16 = 20 days.

Why does W go in the denominator?

M × D × H measures the effort spent. Dividing by W gives effort per unit of work, which is fixed for a given kind of job. Equating the two sides says the same kind of work needs the same effort per unit.

Can I add efficiency E to the formula?

Yes. If one group works faster, multiply its side by its efficiency: M1D1H1E1/W1 = M2D2H2E2/W2. Converting women or boys into man-equivalents first does the same job.

What if the hours per day are not given?

Leave H out of both sides. The formula then becomes M1D1/W1 = M2D2/W2, which assumes both groups work the same hours each day.
