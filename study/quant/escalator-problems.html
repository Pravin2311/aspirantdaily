Escalator Problems: Visible Steps, Walking With and Against the Escalator, and Finding the Escalator's Speed

Visible steps N = steps you take + steps the escalator moves • With: speeds add • Against: speeds subtract • Time-only versions • N = 2ab ÷ (a + b)

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-03

An escalator has a fixed number of visible steps, N, the length of the moving staircase measured in steps. When you walk on it, you cover part of that length with your own feet and the escalator covers the rest by carrying you. Every escalator question rests on that split.

Walking with it: N = (your speed + escalator speed) × time
Walking against it: N = (your speed − escalator speed) × time

Speeds are usually given in steps per second. This is the same structure as boats and streams: the escalator is the current. It is also a case of relative speed, where speeds in the same direction add and speeds in opposite directions subtract.

Worked example 1: standing still

An escalator has 60 visible steps and moves at 2 steps per second. Standing on it, you reach the top in 60 ÷ 2 = 30 seconds.

If you also walk upwards, your speed adds to the escalator's, so you arrive sooner and take fewer steps than N.

Worked example 2: walking up at 1 step per second

Combined speed = 1 + 2 = 3 steps per second, so the trip takes 60 ÷ 3 = 20 seconds. In that time you take 20 steps yourself and the escalator supplies the other 40.

The number of steps you take is your speed times the time. It is never the same as N unless the escalator is stopped.

Walking down an escalator that moves up, you lose ground every second to the escalator, so the useful speed is your speed minus its speed. Your speed must be higher, or you never get anywhere.

Worked example 3: walking down at 3 steps per second

Useful speed = 3 − 2 = 1 step per second, so the trip takes 60 seconds. You step at 3 per second the whole time, so you take 3 × 60 = 180 steps, three times the visible count.

Some escalator problems in aptitude papers give no step counts at all, only times. Treat the escalator's length as one job, as in time and work, and add or subtract the rates.

Worked example 4: walking up a moving escalator

Standing, the escalator takes you up in 60 seconds. Walking on it while it is stopped takes 40 seconds. Together: 1/60 + 1/40 = 5/120 = 1/24, so the trip takes 24 seconds.

Worked example 5: walking down the same up escalator

Your rate is 1/40 and the escalator works against you at 1/60. Net: 1/40 − 1/60 = 1/120, so the trip takes 120 seconds.

The most common exam version gives the steps a person takes at two different walking speeds and asks for N. Write N twice, once for each trip, and set the two expressions equal. Let the escalator's speed be e.

Worked example 6: two walking speeds

At 1 step per second a man takes 20 steps, which means 20 seconds on the escalator, so N = 20 + 20e. At 2 steps per second he takes 30 steps in 15 seconds, so N = 30 + 15e. Setting them equal: 5e = 10, e = 2 and N = 60.

When the question gives a ratio instead of speeds, use the ratio directly to find how many steps the escalator moved.

Worked example 7: a ratio of steps

A woman takes 3 steps for every 2 steps the escalator moves, and she takes 45 steps to reach the top. The escalator moved 45 × 2/3 = 30 steps, so N = 45 + 30 = 75.

If the same person walks up and then down the up-moving escalator at the same stepping speed, taking a steps up and b steps down, a shortcut gives the answer directly: N = 2ab ÷ (a + b). It works only when the stepping speed is the same both ways.

Worked example 8: 24 steps up, 72 steps down

N = 2 × 24 × 72 ÷ 96 = 36. Check: the escalator moves at half his stepping speed, so going up he covers 24 + 12 = 36, and going down he covers 72 − 36 = 36.

  • Taking N to be the steps walked. N also counts the steps the escalator carried you.
  • Adding speeds when walking against it. Against the motion, subtract.
  • Mixing steps and seconds. Time on the escalator = your steps ÷ your stepping speed.
  • Using 2ab ÷ (a + b) with different speeds. The shortcut needs the same stepping speed both ways.

Q1. An escalator has 48 visible steps and moves at 2 steps per second. A man walks up it at 2 steps per second. How long does he take to reach the top? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 12 s

💡 Explanation: Walking with the escalator, the speeds add: 2 + 2 = 4 steps per second, so 48 ÷ 4 = 12 seconds.

Q2. A moving escalator carries a standing person up in 30 seconds. Walking up it when it is stopped takes 20 seconds. How long does walking up the moving escalator take? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 12 s

💡 Explanation: Add the rates: 1/30 + 1/20 = 5/60 = 1/12 of the escalator per second, so the trip takes 12 seconds.

Q3. Walking up an up-moving escalator, a girl takes 30 steps. Walking down the same up-moving escalator at the same stepping speed, she takes 90 steps. How many steps are visible? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 45

💡 Explanation: With the same stepping speed both ways, N = 2ab ÷ (a + b) = 2 × 30 × 90 ÷ 120 = 45 steps.

Q4. An up escalator has 75 visible steps. A boy walks up it at 1 step per second and takes 25 steps. What is the escalator's speed? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 2 steps/s

💡 Explanation: He walks for 25 seconds. In that time the escalator supplies the other 50 steps, so its speed is 50 ÷ 25 = 2 steps per second.

Q5. An up escalator has 40 visible steps and moves at 1 step per second. A man walks down it, against its motion, at 3 steps per second. How long does he take? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 20 s

💡 Explanation: Against the escalator the speeds subtract: 3 − 1 = 2 steps per second, so 40 ÷ 2 = 20 seconds.

What does 'visible steps' mean?

It is the number of steps you can see between the bottom and the top at any instant. It is the length of the escalator measured in steps, and it stays the same whether the escalator is moving or not.

Why do I take more steps walking against the escalator?

The escalator keeps carrying you back, so you need longer to cover the same length. You keep stepping for that whole time, so the count of your own steps goes up.

Is an escalator problem the same as a boats and streams problem?

Yes, in structure. The escalator plays the part of the stream: going with it, the speeds add; going against it, they subtract. Only the units change, from km/h to steps per second.
