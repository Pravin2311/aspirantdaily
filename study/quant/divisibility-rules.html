Divisibility Rules from 2 to 19, Including the Osculator Method

Last-digit rules • Digit sums • Alternating sum for 11 • Osculators for 7, 13, 17, 19 • Composite divisors

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-20

Divisibility tests let you decide whether one number divides another without doing the long division. In the exam they do three jobs. They simplify fractions before you multiply, they eliminate wrong options in number-system questions, and they find missing digits in questions such as "what must replace the star so that the number is divisible by 9?"

The rules fall into four families. Some look only at the last digits, some add the digits, one uses an alternating sum, and the rest use a repeated shortcut called an osculator. Learn the family first and the individual rule follows naturally.

These work because 10, 100 and 1,000 are themselves divisible by 2, 4, 5 and 8. Everything to the left of the last few digits is already a multiple of the divisor, so only the tail matters.

Add the digits. If the sum is a multiple of 3, so is the number; if the sum is a multiple of 9, so is the number. You can repeat the process on the sum until it is small enough to recognise.

For 37,452 the digits add to 3 + 7 + 4 + 5 + 2 = 21. Since 21 is a multiple of 3 but not of 9, the number is divisible by 3 and not by 9.

The rule for 6 combines two earlier tests: the number must be even and its digit sum must be a multiple of 3. The rule for 12 is "divisible by 3 and by 4", and the rule for 18 is "divisible by 2 and by 9".

Worked example 1: testing for 12

37,452 has digit sum 21 (a multiple of 3), and its last two digits 52 = 4 × 13. It passes both tests, so it is divisible by 12. Indeed 37,452 ÷ 12 leaves remainder 0.

Number the digits from the right: the units digit is in place 1, the tens digit in place 2, and so on. Add the digits in odd places, add the digits in even places, and subtract. If the difference is 0 or a multiple of 11, the number is divisible by 11. If not, the difference (taken modulo 11) is the remainder.

Worked example 2: a remainder from the rule

For 87,432 the digits from the right are 2, 3, 4, 7, 8. Odd places: 2 + 4 + 8 = 14. Even places: 3 + 7 = 10. The difference is 4.

So 87,432 is not divisible by 11, and dividing it by 11 leaves remainder 4.

Worked example 3: a difference of 11

For 92,345 the digits from the right are 5, 4, 3, 2, 9. Odd places: 5 + 3 + 9 = 17. Even places: 4 + 2 = 6. The difference is 11, a multiple of 11.

So 92,345 is divisible by 11, which a direct division confirms.

For these primes there is no neat last-digit rule. Instead, each prime has a multiplier called its osculator. Take the last digit, multiply it by the osculator, and add the result to (or subtract it from) the number formed by the remaining digits. Repeat until the number is small enough to test by eye. The original number is divisible by the prime exactly when the final number is.

Worked example 4: testing 1,694 for 7

One step: 169 − 2 × 4 = 161. Another: 16 − 2 × 1 = 14, which is 7 × 2. So 1,694 is divisible by 7, with remainder 0.

If the first step's result is already familiar, stop there: 161 = 7 × 23, so one step was enough.

Worked example 5: testing 4,537 for 13

453 + 4 × 7 = 481. Then 48 + 4 × 1 = 52 = 13 × 4. So 4,537 is divisible by 13 (remainder 0).

The first step, 453 + 28 = 481, already gives 13 × 37 if you recognise it.

Worked example 6: testing 3,757 for 17

375 − 5 × 7 = 340, and 340 = 17 × 20. So 3,757 is divisible by 17 (remainder 0).

A single osculator step turned a four-digit test into a product you can see at a glance.

Worked example 7: testing 2,147 for 19

214 + 2 × 7 = 228, and 228 = 19 × 12. So 2,147 is divisible by 19 (remainder 0).

Note that 228 is the first-step value; checking it is easier than checking 2,147.

A useful link between three of these primes: 7 × 11 × 13 = 1,001. Any six-digit number made by repeating a three-digit block, such as 452,452, equals the block times 1,001, so it is divisible by 7, 11 and 13 at once.

  • ✘ Combining rules for numbers that share a factor. To test for 24 use 3 and 8, not 4 and 6.
  • ✘ Counting places for the 11 rule from the left. Count from the units digit.
  • ✘ Using the osculator with the wrong sign. It is minus for 7 and 17, plus for 13 and 19.
  • ✘ Stopping the digit-sum test at a large sum. Keep adding until the answer is obvious.

Divisibility tests feed directly into the Quant cheat code number-system shortcuts, and they make fraction-heavy questions such as successive discounts faster to simplify.

Q1. For 9,281,563, find (sum of digits in odd places from the right) minus (sum of digits in even places). Is the number divisible by 11? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 16, so not divisible

💡 Explanation: From the right the digits are 3, 6, 5, 1, 8, 2, 9. Odd places: 3 + 5 + 8 + 9 = 25. Even places: 6 + 1 + 2 = 9. The difference 16 is not 0 or a multiple of 11, so 11 does not divide the number.

Q2. Using the osculator for 7, what number do you get after one step from 1003? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 94

💡 Explanation: Drop the last digit 3 and subtract twice it from 100: 100 − 6 = 94. Since 94 is not a multiple of 7, 1003 is not divisible by 7.

Q3. What is the smallest digit d that makes 57d32 divisible by 9? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 1

💡 Explanation: The known digits 5, 7, 3 and 2 add to 17. The next multiple of 9 is 18, so d = 1 brings the digit sum to 18.

Q4. Which of these numbers is divisible by 13? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 5304

💡 Explanation: The osculator for 13 is +4: 530 + 4 × 4 = 546, then 54 + 4 × 6 = 78, which is 6 × 13. So 5304 is divisible by 13.

Q5. 4872 is divisible by 24. Which pair of tests proves it? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 3 and 8

💡 Explanation: 24 = 3 × 8, and 3 and 8 share no factor. The digit sum 21 is a multiple of 3, and the last three digits 872 = 8 × 109. Pairs such as 4 and 6 share a factor, so passing both does not prove divisibility by 24.

Q6. 1615 is divisible by 19. What is the quotient? hard

✅ Answer: (c) 85

💡 Explanation: The osculator for 19 is +2: 161 + 2 × 5 = 171, then 17 + 2 × 1 = 19, so 19 divides 1615. Dividing, 1615 ÷ 19 = 85.

Why does the digit-sum rule work for 3 and 9 but not for 7?

Every power of 10 leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3 or 9, so a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder. Powers of 10 leave different remainders when divided by 7, so the digit sum tells you nothing about 7.

Can I combine two rules to test a composite number?

Yes, but only when the two numbers share no common factor. Divisible by 3 and by 8 means divisible by 24. Divisible by 4 and by 6 does not prove divisibility by 24, because 12 passes both tests and 24 does not divide it.
