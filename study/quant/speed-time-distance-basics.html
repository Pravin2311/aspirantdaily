Speed, Time and Distance: Formulas, km/h to m/s and Proportion Rules

Distance = speed × time • ×5/18 and ×18/5 • Same distance: time ∝ 1/speed • Fraction of usual speed • Late and early • Stoppages

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-23

Every question in this chapter rests on one relation between three quantities:

Distance = Speed × Time, so Speed = Distance ÷ Time and Time = Distance ÷ Speed

The formula only works if the units match. Speed in km/h needs distance in kilometres and time in hours; speed in m/s needs metres and seconds. Most wrong answers come from mixing units, not from the formula.

Worked example 1: time for a journey

A bus covers 240 km at 60 km/h. Time = 240 ÷ 60 = 4 hours.

Worked example 2: minutes into hours

A walker at 6 km/h takes 2 hours 30 minutes. That is 2.5 hours, so the distance is 6 × 2.5 = 15 km.

One kilometre is 1,000 metres and one hour is 3,600 seconds, so 1 km/h = 1,000 ÷ 3,600 = 5/18 m/s.

Worked example 3: 72 km/h

72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s.

In the other direction, 15 m/s × 18/5 = 54 km/h. A useful habit is to remember a few pairs: 18 km/h = 5 m/s, 36 = 10, 54 = 15 and 72 = 20.

When the distance is fixed, doubling the speed halves the time. More generally, if the speeds are in the ratio a : b, the times are in the ratio b : a. This removes the need to know the distance at all.

Worked example 4: speed ratio 3 : 4

Two people walk the same road at speeds in the ratio 3 : 4. The faster one takes 30 minutes less. Their times are in the ratio 4 : 3, a difference of 1 part, so 1 part is 30 minutes. The slower one takes 4 × 30 = 120 minutes.

The other two proportions are direct. For a fixed time, distance is proportional to speed; for a fixed speed, distance is proportional to time.

Worked example 5: the same time

Two cars leave together and drive for 3 hours at 50 km/h and 70 km/h. In the same time the distances are in the ratio 5 : 7, and the faster car ends up 3 × (70 − 50) = 60 km ahead.

A special case appears often: "at a fraction of his usual speed, he is late by t minutes". At speed p/q of usual, the time becomes q/p of usual, and the extra time is (q − p)/p of the usual time.

Worked example 6: three-quarters of the usual speed

At 3/4 of her usual speed a woman is 20 minutes late. Her time becomes 4/3 of usual, so the extra is 1/3 of the usual time. Usual time = 20 × 3 = 60 minutes.

"At speed s₁ he is late; at speed s₂ he is early" means the two journey times differ by the late minutes plus the early minutes. Since the distance is the same:

Distance = (s₁ × s₂ × time difference) ÷ (s₂ − s₁)

Worked example 7: 40 and 50 km/h

At 40 km/h a man is 10 minutes late, and at 50 km/h he is 5 minutes early. The times differ by 15 minutes = 1/4 hour. Distance = 40 × 50 × 1/4 ÷ 10 = 50 km. Check: 50/40 h = 75 min and 50/50 h = 60 min, a 15-minute gap.

If both speeds make him late, by different amounts, the time difference is the larger delay minus the smaller one.

A vehicle that stops on the way has two speeds: its running speed without stops, and its lower average speed including stops. The distance lost each hour, run at full speed, gives the minutes spent stopped.

Worked example 8: 60 and 45 km/h

Without stops a bus runs at 60 km/h; with stops it averages 45 km/h. Each hour it loses 15 km, which at 60 km/h takes 15 ÷ 60 × 60 = 15 minutes. So it stops for 15 minutes every hour.

  • ✘ Multiplying by 18/5 when converting km/h to m/s. It is 5/18.
  • ✘ Using the speed ratio instead of its inverse for times over the same distance.
  • ✘ In late-and-early questions, subtracting the minutes instead of adding them.

Q1. What is 90 km/h in metres per second? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 25 m/s

💡 Explanation: Multiply by 5/18 to convert km/h to m/s: 90 × 5 ÷ 18 = 25 m/s.

Q2. A car covers 360 km in 4 hours 30 minutes. What is its speed? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 80 km/h

💡 Explanation: 4 hours 30 minutes is 4.5 hours. Speed = 360 ÷ 4.5 = 80 km/h. Using 4.3 hours for 4 h 30 min is the usual slip.

Q3. Walking at 4/5 of his usual speed, a man reaches office 15 minutes late. What is his usual time for the journey? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 60 minutes

💡 Explanation: At 4/5 of the speed he takes 5/4 of the time, which is 1/4 of the usual time extra. That extra is 15 minutes, so the usual time is 4 × 15 = 60 minutes.

Q4. At 30 km/h a student is 20 minutes late; at 40 km/h he is 10 minutes early. What is the distance? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 60 km

💡 Explanation: The two times differ by 20 + 10 = 30 minutes = 1/2 hour. Distance = product of speeds × time difference ÷ difference of speeds = 30 × 40 × 1/2 ÷ 10 = 60 km.

Q5. Without stoppages a bus runs at 54 km/h; with stoppages its average is 45 km/h. For how many minutes per hour does it stop? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 10 minutes

💡 Explanation: In each hour the bus loses 54 − 45 = 9 km of running. At 54 km/h, 9 km takes 9 ÷ 54 × 60 = 10 minutes, so it stops 10 minutes per hour.

Q6. Two cyclists cover the same route at speeds in the ratio 5 : 6. The slower takes 12 minutes longer. How long does the slower cyclist take? hard

✅ Answer: (a) 72 minutes

💡 Explanation: For the same distance, times are in the inverse ratio 6 : 5. The difference of 1 part is 12 minutes, so the slower cyclist takes 6 × 12 = 72 minutes.

Why does 5/18 convert km/h to m/s?

One kilometre is 1,000 metres and one hour is 3,600 seconds. So 1 km/h = 1,000 ÷ 3,600 m/s, which simplifies to 5/18 m/s. Going the other way, multiply by 18/5.

How do I write 4 hours 30 minutes in the formula?

Convert minutes to a fraction of an hour by dividing by 60. Thirty minutes is 0.5 hours, so the time is 4.5 hours. Never write it as 4.30, which would mean 4 hours and 18 minutes.
