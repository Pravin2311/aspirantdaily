Weighted Average Formula: How to Combine Averages, Percentages and Prices

The formula • Weights as counts or percentages • Combined pass rates • Mixed prices • Finding a missing group size • Why the answer sits nearer the heavier weight

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

A simple average treats every value as equally important. A weighted average lets some values count for more than others. Each value is multiplied by its weight, the products are added, and the total is divided by the sum of the weights:

Weighted average = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ) ÷ (w₁ + w₂ + … + wₙ)

The weight is whatever tells you how much each value matters. In a combined class average it is the number of students in each section. In a mixture it is the quantity of each ingredient. In an exam with weightage it is the percentage each part carries. Whatever the context, the arithmetic is the same.

The most common use is merging two or more groups whose averages are known. Multiply each average by its group size to get each group's total, add the totals, and divide by the combined size.

Worked example 1: two batches

A morning batch of 25 students averages 72 in a mock test, and an evening batch of 35 averages 60. The combined average is (25 × 72 + 35 × 60) ÷ 60 = (1,800 + 2,100) ÷ 60 = 3,900 ÷ 60 = 65.

Notice that 65 is closer to 60 than to 72, because the evening batch is larger. A useful way to see it: the answer starts at the smaller average and moves towards the larger one by a fraction equal to the larger average's share of the total weight. Here that is 60 + (72 − 60) × 25 ÷ 60 = 60 + 5 = 65.

Two facts follow and make good checks. The weighted average always lies between the smallest and largest values. And it sits nearer the value with the heavier weight. If your answer falls outside the range, or on the wrong side of the middle, something has gone wrong.

Recruitment and school results often combine several parts with fixed weightage. When the weights are percentages that add to 100, multiply each score by its weight as a decimal and add. There is no need to divide, because the weights already total 1.

Worked example 2: a three-part score

A selection counts a written test as 20%, a skill test as 30% and an interview as 50%. A candidate scores 80, 70 and 64. The final score is 0.2 × 80 + 0.3 × 70 + 0.5 × 64 = 16 + 21 + 32 = 69.

The simple average of 80, 70 and 64 is about 71.3, higher than 69, because the candidate's weakest score carried the most weight. This is exactly the situation the weighted formula exists to capture.

A percentage is itself an average, so two percentages from groups of different sizes must also be weighted. The safest route is to convert back to counts.

Worked example 3: pass rates of two centres

At one centre 90% of 200 candidates passed; at another 70% of 300 passed. Passes are 180 + 210 = 390 out of 500, which is 78%, not the 80% you would get by averaging 90 and 70.

The same logic applies to prices per kilogram, marks per subject and rates per hour. Whenever the value is a 'per something' figure, weight it by how much of that something there is.

Worked example 4: an average price

A trader buys 12 kg of rice at ₹150 per kg and 8 kg at ₹200 per kg. The average cost is (12 × 150 + 8 × 200) ÷ 20 = (1,800 + 1,600) ÷ 20 = ₹170 per kg.

Sometimes the combined average is known and a group size is not. Each group's distance from the combined average, multiplied by its weight, must balance the other group's. So the weights are in the inverse ratio of the distances.

w₁ : w₂ = (x₂ − A) : (A − x₁), where A is the weighted average and x₁ < A < x₂

Worked example 5: how many joined?

15 employees average 60 in a training test. Some new employees who average 70 join, and the overall average becomes 68. The weights are in the ratio (70 − 68) : (68 − 60) = 2 : 8 = 1 : 4 for the original group against the new one. So the new group is 4 times the original: 4 × 15 = 60 new employees.

Check: (15 × 60 + 60 × 70) ÷ 75 = (900 + 4,200) ÷ 75 = 68. This balancing idea is the heart of the alligation method, covered in full on the alligation and mixture page.

Weights do not need to be actual counts. A ratio works just as well, because scaling every weight by the same number does not change the answer.

Worked example 6: a 2 : 3 split

Boys and girls in a school are in the ratio 2 : 3. Boys average 40 kg and girls 60 kg. The average weight of all students is (2 × 40 + 3 × 60) ÷ 5 = 260 ÷ 5 = 52 kg, whatever the actual numbers of students.

When the weights are equal, the weighted average reduces to the simple average: two values of 90 and 70 with equal weight average 80.

Change the weights to 1 : 3 and the same two values average 75, pulled towards 70.

  • ✘ Averaging two percentages or two averages without weighting by group size.
  • ✘ Dividing by the number of groups instead of the total weight.
  • ✘ Using the direct ratio of distances for the weights. It is the inverse: the group farther from the combined average is the smaller one.
  • ✘ Forgetting to convert percentage weightages to decimals before multiplying.

Q1. A class has 40 students averaging 45 marks and another has 60 students averaging 55. What is the combined average? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 51

💡 Explanation: (40 × 45 + 60 × 55) ÷ 100 = (1,800 + 3,300) ÷ 100 = 51. The answer is closer to 55 because that class is larger.

Q2. An exam counts the written test as 40% and the interview as 60%. A candidate scores 60 in the written test and 85 in the interview. What is the final score? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 75

💡 Explanation: 0.4 × 60 + 0.6 × 85 = 24 + 51 = 75. The simple average, 72.5, ignores the weightage.

Q3. 10 workers earn ₹20 an hour on average. How many workers earning ₹30 an hour must join so that the overall average becomes ₹26? hard

✅ Answer: (c) 15

💡 Explanation: The weights are in the inverse ratio of the distances from ₹26: (30 − 26) : (26 − 20) = 4 : 6 for the ₹20 group against the ₹30 group. So the new group is 6 ÷ 4 times the old one: 10 × 6 ÷ 4 = 15. Check: (10 × 20 + 15 × 30) ÷ 25 = 650 ÷ 25 = 26.

Q4. A shop sells 5 kg of one tea at ₹120 per kg and 15 kg of another at ₹160 per kg. What is the average price per kg? easy

✅ Answer: (b) ₹150

💡 Explanation: (5 × 120 + 15 × 160) ÷ 20 = (600 + 2,400) ÷ 20 = 3,000 ÷ 20 = ₹150.

Q5. In school P, 80% of 100 students passed; in school Q, 60% of 400 students passed. What is the pass percentage of the two schools together? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 64%

💡 Explanation: Passes: 80 + 240 = 320 out of 500 students, which is 64%. Averaging 80% and 60% to get 70% ignores the school sizes.

When is a simple average enough?

Only when every item carries the same weight, for example when two groups have the same number of members or two subjects count equally. As soon as group sizes, quantities or percentage weightages differ, use the weighted formula.

Do the weights have to add up to 1 or 100?

No. Weights can be counts, kilograms, hours or percentages. The formula divides by the total weight, so 2 and 3 give the same answer as 40% and 60%. If the weights are percentages that already add to 100, you can skip the division by multiplying each value by its decimal weight.
