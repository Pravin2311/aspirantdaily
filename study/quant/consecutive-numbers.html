Sum of Consecutive Numbers: The Middle-Term Method for Consecutive, Odd and Even Numbers, Products and Squares

Middle term = sum ÷ count • Odd and even numbers step by 2 • Even counts: the average sits between two terms • Products and squares • Difference of squares = sum • Divisibility facts

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Consecutive numbers are evenly spaced: 23, 24, 25 step by 1, while consecutive odd numbers such as 21, 23, 25 step by 2, and so do consecutive even numbers. Evenly spaced numbers balance around their middle, so for any sum of consecutive numbers:

Middle term = average = sum ÷ count

From the middle term, count outwards in steps of 1 (or 2 for odd and even numbers). This is quicker and less error-prone than writing x, x + 1, x + 2 and solving an equation, although both methods give the same answer.

Worked example 1: three consecutive numbers that add to 72

The middle number is 72 ÷ 3 = 24, so the numbers are 23, 24 and 25.

Worked example 2: five consecutive odd numbers that add to 125

The middle term is 125 ÷ 5 = 25. Stepping by 2, the numbers are 21, 23, 25, 27 and 29, so the smallest is 21.

With an even count there is no single middle term. The average sits halfway between the two middle terms.

Worked example 3: four consecutive even numbers that add to 100

The average is 25, between the middle pair 24 and 26. The numbers are 22, 24, 26 and 28, so the largest is 28, which is 25 + 3.

If the average is given instead of the sum, you already have the middle.

Worked example 4: seven consecutive numbers with average 20

The middle (4th) number is 20, so the numbers run from 17 to 23.

For a sum starting from a known number, use the AP sum. n consecutive numbers starting at a add to n × a + n(n − 1)/2.

Worked example 5: ten numbers from 15

10 × 15 + 10 × 9/2 = 150 + 45 = 195. That is 15 + 16 + … + 24. For the general AP formulas, see AP and GP.

For a product, take the square root (or cube root, for three numbers) to land near the numbers, then test the neighbours.

Worked example 6: two consecutive numbers with product 156

√156 is about 12.5, so try 12 and 13: 12 × 13 = 156. The larger is 13.

Worked example 7: sum of squares 145

Half of 145 is about 72, and √72 is about 8.5, so try 8 and 9: 64 + 81 = 145. The larger is 9.

A neat fact: for consecutive numbers n and n + 1, the difference of their squares equals their sum, because (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1.

Worked example 8: 15² − 14²

225 − 196 = 29, which is 15 + 14. Reverse it: if the difference of the squares is 29, the numbers add to 29, so they are 14 and 15.

Some questions ask what must always divide a product of consecutive numbers.

  • Of any two consecutive numbers, one is even, so their product is divisible by 2.
  • Of any three consecutive numbers, one is a multiple of 3 and at least one is even, so their product is divisible by 6.
  • The product of two consecutive even numbers is divisible by 8.
  • The sum of an odd count of consecutive numbers is divisible by that count.

Worked example 9: 7 × 8 × 9

504 ÷ 6 = 84 exactly, remainder 0, as the rule predicts. See divisibility rules for more.

Worked example 10: two consecutive even numbers

12 × 14 = 168, and 168 ÷ 8 = 21 with remainder 0. The reason: write the numbers as 2k and 2k + 2. Their product is 4k(k + 1), and k(k + 1) is a product of two consecutive numbers, so it is even. That makes the whole product a multiple of 4 × 2 = 8.

In "which of these must divide" questions, test the smallest case first, such as 2 × 4 = 8, to rule out options quickly.

  • Stepping by 1 for odd or even numbers. Consecutive odd and even numbers differ by 2.
  • Treating the average as a term with an even count. It sits between the two middle terms.
  • Answering with the middle term. Check whether the question wants the smallest or largest.
  • Skipping the substitution check. For products and squares, multiply back to confirm.
  • Forgetting negative solutions. −13 and −12 also have product 156; use the context to choose.

Q1. The sum of three consecutive numbers is 87. What is the largest? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 30

💡 Explanation: The middle number is 87 ÷ 3 = 29, so the numbers are 28, 29 and 30. The largest is 30.

Q2. The sum of four consecutive odd numbers is 64. What is the smallest? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 13

💡 Explanation: The average is 16, which sits between the 2nd and 3rd terms, 15 and 17. So the numbers are 13, 15, 17 and 19, and the smallest is 13.

Q3. The product of two consecutive even numbers is 168. What is the larger number? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 14

💡 Explanation: √168 is about 13, so try 12 and 14: 12 × 14 = 168. The larger is 14.

Q4. The average of five consecutive numbers is 42. What is the largest? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 44

💡 Explanation: With an odd count, the average is the middle number. The numbers are 40 to 44, so the largest is 44.

Q5. The difference of the squares of two consecutive numbers is 37. What is the larger number? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 19

💡 Explanation: For consecutive numbers, (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1, their sum. So the numbers add to 37 and are 18 and 19. The larger is 19.

Why does dividing the sum by the count give the middle number?

Consecutive numbers are evenly spaced, so they balance around the middle. The average of evenly spaced numbers is always the middle term, and the average is the sum divided by the count.

What if there is an even number of terms?

Then the average falls halfway between the two middle terms. For four consecutive even numbers with average 25, the middle two are 24 and 26.

Are consecutive odd numbers handled the same way as consecutive even numbers?

Yes. Both step by 2, so the method is identical. Only the starting value tells you whether the numbers are odd or even.
