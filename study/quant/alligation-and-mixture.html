Alligation: The Cross Method for Mixtures, and When It Fails

Mean price • Mixing ratio • Concentrations • Group averages • Profit mixtures • Repeated replacement

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-20

Alligation answers one question: in what ratio must two ingredients be mixed to reach a given average? The ingredients might be grades of rice at different prices, acid solutions of different strengths, two groups of students with different average marks, or two parts of a stock sold at different profits. In every case there is a cheaper value (c), a dearer value (d) and a mean value (m) somewhere between them.

Quantity of cheaper : quantity of dearer = (d − m) : (m − c)

Each ingredient's share is the distance of the other ingredient from the mean. If the mean lies close to the dearer value, you need a lot of the dearer item and only a little of the cheaper one, which is exactly what the rule gives.

Suppose x kg of the cheaper item and y kg of the dearer item make a mixture worth m per kg. The total value is unchanged by mixing, so cx + dy = m(x + y). Rearranging gives x(m − c) = y(d − m), so x : y = (d − m) : (m − c). The "cross" diagram that coaching books draw is just a picture of this equation.

Because the proof uses nothing but a weighted average, the rule works for anything that averages by quantity: prices per kg, percentage concentrations, average marks per student, profit percentages on cost, or speeds when each speed is held for a time.

Worked example 1: two prices

Rice at ₹40 a kg is mixed with rice at ₹60 a kg to give a mixture worth ₹46 a kg. Cheaper : dearer = (60 − 46) : (46 − 40) = 14 : 6 = 7 : 3.

Worked example 2: adding water

Water costs nothing, so its value is 0. To bring milk worth ₹50 a litre down to ₹40 a litre, water : milk = (50 − 40) : (40 − 0) = 1 : 4. One litre of water goes into every four litres of milk.

Worked example 3: concentrations

To make 30 litres of 40% acid from 30% and 60% solutions, 30% : 60% = (60 − 40) : (40 − 30) = 2 : 1. So 20 litres of the 30% solution and 10 litres of the 60% solution are needed.

Worked example 4: group averages

A class averages 60 marks; boys average 55 and girls 70. Boys : girls = (70 − 60) : (60 − 55) = 2 : 1.

Worked example 5: profit and loss

A trader sells part of his goods at a 10% profit and the rest at a 20% loss, ending with a 4% profit overall. Treat the loss as −20. Profit part : loss part = (4 − (−20)) : (10 − 4) = 24 : 6 = 4 : 1.

A different type of question takes some liquid out of a container, replaces it with water, and repeats. The strength drops each time, but by a fraction of what is left rather than by a fixed amount, so there is no single pair of values to cross. Use the replacement formula instead. If a container holds V litres and x litres are replaced n times:

Pure liquid left = V × (1 − x/V)n

Worked example 6: two replacements

A 50-litre can of milk has 10 litres drawn off and replaced with water, twice. Each step keeps 40/50 = 0.8 of the milk. Milk left = 50 × 0.8² = 32 litres, so the can now holds 18 litres of water.

It is tempting to subtract 10 litres of milk twice and answer 30 litres. That is wrong because the second draw removes a mixture that already contains water, so it takes out only 8 litres of milk.

  • ✘ Writing the ratio the wrong way round. The cheaper item's share is (d − m), not (m − c).
  • ✘ Using alligation when the mean is not between the two values. Then no mixture can reach it.
  • ✘ Forgetting that water has a price of 0 and a concentration of 0%.
  • ✘ Using alligation for repeated replacement. Use the power formula.

Q1. In what ratio must tea at ₹120 a kg be mixed with tea at ₹180 a kg so that the mixture is worth ₹160 a kg? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 1 : 2

💡 Explanation: Cheaper : dearer = (180 − 160) : (160 − 120) = 20 : 40 = 1 : 2. The mean is closer to ₹180, so more of the dearer tea is needed.

Q2. How much water must be added to 40 litres of milk costing ₹45 a litre so that the mixture is worth ₹36 a litre? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 10 litres

💡 Explanation: Water costs ₹0. Water : milk = (45 − 36) : (36 − 0) = 9 : 36 = 1 : 4, so 40 litres of milk needs 10 litres of water.

Q3. Solutions with 20% and 50% alcohol are mixed to make 60 litres of a 30% solution. How many litres of the 20% solution are used? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 40 litres

💡 Explanation: 20% : 50% = (50 − 30) : (30 − 20) = 2 : 1. Two-thirds of 60 litres is 40 litres of the 20% solution.

Q4. A firm of 60 people has an average salary of ₹15,000. Officers average ₹30,000 and the other staff ₹12,000. How many officers are there? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 10

💡 Explanation: Officers : others = (15,000 − 12,000) : (30,000 − 15,000) = 1 : 5. One-sixth of 60 is 10 officers.

Q5. From 80 litres of milk, 8 litres are taken out and replaced with water. This is done three times in all. How much milk is left? hard

✅ Answer: (b) 58.32 litres

💡 Explanation: Each step keeps 72/80 = 0.9 of the milk. After three steps, milk = 80 × 0.9³ = 80 × 0.729 = 58.32 litres. Subtracting 24 litres is wrong because later draws remove a mixture.

Q6. A trader sells part of his stock at an 8% gain and the rest at an 18% gain, making 14% overall. What percentage of the stock was sold at the 8% gain? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 40%

💡 Explanation: 8% part : 18% part = (18 − 14) : (14 − 8) = 4 : 6 = 2 : 3, so the 8% part is 2/5 = 40% of the stock.

Which quantity goes on which side of the alligation cross?

The difference between the dearer value and the mean gives the share of the cheaper item, and the difference between the mean and the cheaper value gives the share of the dearer item. Each ingredient's share is the distance of the other ingredient from the mean.

Why does alligation fail for repeated replacement?

Alligation mixes two fixed ingredients once. In repeated replacement, each draw removes a mixture whose strength keeps changing, so there is no single pair of values to cross. Use the multiplier formula final = initial × (1 − x/V) to the power n instead.
