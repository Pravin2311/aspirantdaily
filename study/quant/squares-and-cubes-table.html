The NCERT Grade 8 textbook makes a simple observation: squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. If a number ends in 2, 3, 7 or 8, it is definitely not a square. The test only works one way, though. The textbook's own example is 26, which ends in 6 but is not a square, so a good last digit proves nothing.

Zeros follow a rule too. A perfect square ends in an even number of zeros, so 4,000 cannot be a square while 40,000 (200²) is.

Cubes behave differently. A cube can end in any digit, and the last digit of a cube points straight to the last digit of its root. A cube ending in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9 has a root ending in the same digit; a cube ending in 8 has a root ending in 2, and the reverse; a cube ending in 3 has a root ending in 7, and the reverse.

Worked example 1: the cube root of 4,913

4,913 lies between 10³ = 1,000 and 20³ = 8,000, so the root is between 10 and 20. The cube ends in 3, so the root ends in 7. The answer is 17, and the table confirms 17³ = 4,913.

Add the odd numbers in order and you get the squares: 1, then 1 + 3 = 4, then 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, and so on. In NCERT's words, the sum of the first n odd numbers is n². The flip side is that the gap between two consecutive squares is always an odd number: n² and (n + 1)² differ by n + (n + 1).

Worked example 2: one square from the next

Knowing 35² = 1,225, find 36². The gap is 35 + 36 = 71, so 36² = 1,225 + 71 = 1,296.

The same idea gives a slow but certain test for a perfect square: keep subtracting 1, 3, 5, 7 and so on. If you land exactly on zero, the number is a square, and the count of subtractions is its root.

Numbers ending in 5. Multiply the tens part by the next whole number, then write 25 after it.

Worked example 3: 35²

The tens part is 3, and 3 × 4 = 12. Write 25 after it: 35² = 1,225.

This works because (10a + 5)² = 100a(a + 1) + 25, so the last two digits are always 25.

Numbers near 50. Write the number as 50 plus or minus a small k. Then the square is 2,500, plus or minus 100k, plus k².

Worked example 4: 53²

53 = 50 + 3, so 53² = 2,500 + 300 + 9 = 2,809.

Worked example 5: 46²

46 = 50 − 4, so 46² = 2,500 − 400 + 16 = 2,116.

Both results match the table. The same method works near 100 using 10,000 and 200k.

For cubes, consecutive cubes grow apart fast: 25³ and 24³ already differ by 1,801. That is why estimating between two known cubes, as in worked example 1, settles most cube root questions.

  • Only square numbers have an odd number of factors, because one factor pairs with itself.
  • Every perfect square has two square roots, one positive and one negative; exam questions mean the positive one unless they say otherwise.
  • 1729 is the smallest number that can be written as a sum of two cubes in two different ways: 1³ + 12³ and 9³ + 10³. It is called the Hardy–Ramanujan number, and numbers like it are called taxicab numbers. The next two are 4104 and 13832.
  • The earliest known tables of squares and cubes come from the Babylonians, about 1700 BCE, written on clay tablets.

Q1. Which of these cannot be a perfect square? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 3,248

💡 Explanation: A square never ends in 2, 3, 7 or 8, so 3,248 is ruled out at once. The others are 37², 49² and 64².

Q2. What is 85²? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 7,225

💡 Explanation: For a number ending in 5, multiply the tens digit by the next number and write 25 after it: 8 × 9 = 72, so 85² = 7,225.

Q3. Given 35² = 1,225, what is 36²? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 1,296

💡 Explanation: Consecutive squares differ by the sum of the two numbers: 36² = 35² + 35 + 36 = 1,225 + 71 = 1,296.

Q4. What is the cube root of 4,913? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 17

💡 Explanation: 4,913 lies between 10³ = 1,000 and 20³ = 8,000, so the root is a teen. It ends in 3, and only cubes of numbers ending in 7 end in 3, so the root is 17.

Q5. How many odd numbers, starting from 1, add up to 49? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 7

💡 Explanation: The sum of the first n odd numbers is n², and 49 = 7², so it takes the first 7 odd numbers: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 13 = 49.

How far should I learn squares and cubes by heart?

Squares to 30 and cubes to 15 are worth knowing cold. Squares from 31 to 50 follow quickly from the shortcuts on this page, especially the one built around 50² = 2,500, so practise those rather than memorising every line.

Can a cube end in any digit?

Yes. Unlike squares, cubes can end in every digit from 0 to 9. Digits 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 and 9 repeat in the cube, 2 and 8 swap, and 3 and 7 swap. That swap is what makes cube roots of perfect cubes quick to guess.
