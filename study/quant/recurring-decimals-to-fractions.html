Convert Recurring Decimal to Fraction: The 9s and 0s Rule for Pure and Mixed Recurring Decimals

Pure recurring: block over 9s • Mixed recurring: subtract and use 9s then 0s • Whole-number parts • Why 0.999… = 1 • Adding recurring decimals

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

A recurring decimal repeats a block of digits forever, written with a bar over the block or with dots, such as 0.3636… A pure recurring decimal repeats from the very first digit after the point.

Pure recurring: fraction = repeating block ÷ (one 9 for each digit in the block)

Why it works: let x = 0.3636… Multiply by 100 (one zero per repeating digit) to get 100x = 36.3636… Subtract the first line: 99x = 36, so x = 36/99. The repeating tails cancel exactly, which is why the denominator is made of 9s.

Worked example 1: one repeating digit

0.777… = 7/9.

Worked example 2: two repeating digits

0.3636… = 36/99 = 4/11 after dividing top and bottom by 9.

Worked example 3: a six-digit block

0.142857142857… = 142857/999999 = 1/7. Recognising 1/7 = 0.142857… saves time in approximation questions.

A mixed recurring decimal has some digits after the point that do not repeat before the repeating block starts, as in 0.2333… (only the 3 repeats).

Mixed recurring: fraction = (all digits after the point − the non-repeating digits) ÷ (9s for the repeating digits, then 0s for the non-repeating digits)

Worked example 4: 0.2333…

All digits as one number: 23. Non-repeating part: 2. One repeating digit gives one 9; one non-repeating digit gives one 0. Fraction = (23 − 2)/90 = 21/90 = 7/30. Check: 7 ÷ 30 = 0.2333…

Worked example 5: 0.124545…

Here 12 does not repeat and 45 does. Fraction = (1245 − 12)/9900 = 1233/9900 = 137/1100. Two 9s for 45, two 0s for 12.

The algebra is the same as before with one extra step: first multiply to move the non-repeating digits in front of the point, then use the pure method on what is left.

If there is a whole-number part, keep it aside, convert the decimal part, and add back.

Worked example 6: 2.666…

2 + 6/9 = 2 + 2/3 = 8/3.

The 9s rule also settles a famous question.

Worked example 7: 0.999…

One repeating 9 over one 9: 9/9 = 1. The recurring decimal 0.999… is exactly 1, not a number just below it.

Do not add recurring decimals digit by digit; convert to fractions first, add, then simplify.

Worked example 8: 0.333… + 0.666…

3/9 + 6/9 = 9/9 = 1. Adding the decimals digit by digit gives 0.999…, which is the same thing, but fractions avoid the confusion.

To compare two recurring decimals, write enough digits of each to see where they differ, or convert both to fractions with a common denominator.

Going the other way helps you recognise answers. Write a fraction in lowest terms and factorise its denominator:

  • If the denominator has only 2s and 5s as prime factors, the decimal terminates.
  • If it has any other prime, the decimal recurs.
  • If it mixes 2s or 5s with another prime, the decimal is mixed recurring: a few digits first, then the repeating block.

Worked example 9: a terminating fraction

3/8 has denominator 2³, so it terminates: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375.

Worked example 10: a recurring fraction

1/11 has the prime 11 in its denominator, so it recurs: 1/11 = 0.0909… = 09/99 = 9/99, which simplifies back to 1/11.

Useful blocks to recognise: thirds repeat one digit (0.333…, 0.666…), elevenths repeat two (0.0909…, 0.1818…), and sevenths repeat the six digits 142857 in rotation. A sixth (0.1666…) and a twelfth (0.08333…) are mixed recurring because their denominators contain 2 as well as 3.

  • Using 10s instead of 9s. 0.333… is 3/9, not 3/10. A denominator of 10 belongs to the terminating decimal 0.3.
  • Mixing up the order of 9s and 0s. 9s (for the repeating digits) come first, then 0s.
  • Forgetting to subtract the non-repeating part. 0.2333… is (23 − 2)/90, not 23/90.
  • Not simplifying. Options are usually in lowest terms, so 36/99 appears as 4/11.
  • Dropping the whole-number part. Convert only the decimal part, then add the whole number back.

Q1. Write 0.454545… as a fraction in lowest terms. easy

✅ Answer: (b) 5/11

💡 Explanation: Two repeating digits, so put 45 over 99. 45/99 simplifies to 5/11.

Q2. Write 0.1666… (only the 6 repeats) as a fraction. medium

✅ Answer: (a) 1/6

💡 Explanation: Mixed recurring: (16 − 1) over 90, one 9 for the repeating 6 and one 0 for the 1. 15/90 = 1/6.

Q3. What is 0.272727… + 0.727272…? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 1

💡 Explanation: 27/99 + 72/99 = 99/99 = 1. Writing the decimals out and adding gives 0.999…, which equals 1.

Q4. Write 1.333… as a fraction. easy

✅ Answer: (c) 4/3

💡 Explanation: Keep the whole part: 1 + 3/9 = 1 + 1/3 = 4/3.

Q5. Write 0.3181818… (the block 18 repeats) as a fraction. hard

✅ Answer: (b) 7/22

💡 Explanation: (318 − 3) over 990: two 9s for the repeating 18 and one 0 for the 3. 315/990 = 7/22.

What is the difference between pure and mixed recurring decimals?

In a pure recurring decimal, every digit after the point repeats, as in 0.3636…. In a mixed recurring decimal, some digits come first and do not repeat, as in 0.2333…, where only the 3 repeats.

Does 0.999… really equal 1?

Yes. By the 9s rule, 0.999… = 9/9 = 1. Another way to see it: 1/3 = 0.333…, and three times that is 0.999…, but three times 1/3 is 1.

Which fractions give recurring decimals?

A fraction in lowest terms gives a terminating decimal only if its denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5. Any other prime factor, such as 3, 7 or 11, makes the decimal recur.
