Table Data Interpretation: Columns, Rates and Missing Values

Add a derived column • Overall rate vs average of rates • Missing values • Ratios across rows

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-24

A table gives you exact numbers, so there is no reading error from a scale, but it hides the answers in columns you have to build yourself. A typical bank or SSC table has four to six rows and two to four columns, and the questions ask for rates, totals and comparisons that are not printed.

The table below holds invented practice data: the number of students who appeared for, and passed, an exam in five schools.

SchoolAppearedPassedPass rate (derived)
A40032080%
B50035070%
C25022590%
D60045075%
E35024570%
Total2,1001,590—

The last column and the total row are not given in a real question; you add them. Spending a few seconds on derived columns before reading the questions pays off, because most questions in the set use them.

Worked example 1: rate for one row

School D: 450 passed out of 600, so the pass rate is 450 ÷ 600 × 100 = 75%.

Worked example 2: the best row

Comparing 320/400, 350/500, 225/250, 450/600 and 245/350, the highest is School C at 225 ÷ 250 × 100 = 90%. C has the fewest candidates but the best rate.

Worked example 3: average per row

On average each school had 1,590 ÷ 5 = 318 students passing.

Worked example 4: ratio built from two columns

Failures are appeared minus passed. A had 80 failures and E had 105, so the ratio is 80 : 105 = 16 : 21.

This is the single most common trap in table DI. The overall pass rate is total passed ÷ total appeared, not the average of the five pass rates.

Worked example 5: overall pass rate

Across all schools, 1,590 of 2,100 passed: 1,590 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = 75.71%.

Worked example 6: the simple average is different

The average of the five rates is (80 + 70 + 90 + 75 + 70) ÷ 5 = 77%. It is higher than 75.71% because School C, with the best rate, is the smallest school and gets too much weight in a simple average.

If the options include both numbers, the examiner is testing this exact point. Use totals unless the question says "average of the pass percentages".

Some tables leave cells blank and give a rule or a total that lets you fill them. Solve for the missing cells first, then answer the questions.

Worked example 7: missing passes

A sixth school, F, had 300 candidates and an 85% pass rate. Passed = 85 ÷ 100 × 300 = 255.

Worked example 8: missing candidates

School G had 312 passes at a 78% pass rate. Appeared = 312 ÷ 0.78 = 400. A quick check: 78% of 400 is 312.

In caselets, the whole table is "missing" and has to be built from a paragraph; the method is in caselet DI.

A table set usually has five questions on the same rows, and they rarely need every column. A short routine keeps the set quick:

  1. Skim all five questions first. Note which derived figures they need, such as a rate, a failure count or a total.
  2. Build only those columns, in pencil beside the table, and add a total row if any question mentions "overall" or "all schools".
  3. Answer the look-up questions first. A single-row rate or ratio takes seconds once the column exists.
  4. Leave the "how many rows satisfy" questions for last. They need a comparison against every row, but by then all the rates are already written down.

Two habits prevent most errors. Read the column headings for units, because one column may be in hundreds and another in actual numbers. And when a question names two rows, circle both before reading across, since adjacent rows in a dense table are easy to swap.

  • ✘ Averaging percentages when the question needs the overall rate.
  • ✘ Using the wrong row as the base in "more than" and "percentage of" questions.
  • ✘ Forgetting that a column can be derived (failures = appeared − passed).
  • ✘ Mis-copying a number from a crowded table; underline the row before you read across.

Q1. Using the schools table, how many students failed in all five schools together? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 510

💡 Explanation: 2,100 students appeared and 1,590 passed, so 2,100 − 1,590 = 510 failed. 1,590 is the number who passed.

Q2. The number who passed in School B is approximately what percentage of the number who passed in School A? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 109.38%

💡 Explanation: B had 350 passes and A had 320. 350 ÷ 320 × 100 = 109.38%. Dividing the other way, 320 ÷ 350, gives 91.43%.

Q3. By what percentage is the number who appeared in School D more than in School B? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 20%

💡 Explanation: D had 600 and B had 500 candidates. The difference of 100 is taken on B's 500: 100 ÷ 500 × 100 = 20%. Using 600 as the base gives 16.67%.

Q4. If School E's pass rate rose to 80% with the same number appearing, how many more students would pass? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 35

💡 Explanation: 80% of E's 350 candidates is 280. E currently has 245 passes, so 280 − 245 = 35 more students would pass.

Q5. What is the ratio of students who passed in School C to those who passed in School E? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 45 : 49

💡 Explanation: C had 225 passes and E had 245. Both are divisible by 5, giving 45 : 49, and 45 and 49 have no common factor.

Q6. How many schools have a pass rate higher than the overall pass rate of all five schools combined? hard

✅ Answer: (c) 2

💡 Explanation: The overall rate is 1,590 ÷ 2,100 = 75.71%. Only A (80%) and C (90%) are above it; D, at 75%, falls just short, as do B and E at 70%.

Why is the overall percentage different from the average of the percentages?

The average of percentages gives every row equal weight, but rows have different sizes. The overall percentage weights each row by its size, so large rows count for more. Always divide the column totals unless the question asks for a simple average.

How do I find a missing value in a table?

Look for a relationship between columns, such as passed equals appeared times pass rate, or a total row. Write that relationship as an equation with the missing value as the unknown, then solve it before attempting the questions.
