Income Expenditure Savings Problems: When Income and Spending Change by Different Percentages

Income = Expenditure + Savings • Take income as 100 • New savings after both change • Finding the expenditure change • Original savings rate • Weighted-average shortcut

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Whatever part of income is not spent is saved. Every income expenditure savings question rests on that single identity, which holds both before and after any change:

Income = Expenditure + Savings    (before the change, and again after it)

The percentages in these questions usually refer to different bases: the savings rate is a share of income, while the rise in expenditure is a share of the old expenditure. Mixing those bases is where most mistakes come from.

Worked example 1: the basic split

A person earns ₹20,000 and saves 15%. Savings are ₹3,000, so expenditure is 20,000 × 0.85 = ₹17,000.

The fastest method is to give income the value 100. The savings rate then tells you savings and expenditure directly, and every later change is simple arithmetic.

  1. Write old income = 100, old savings = the savings rate, old expenditure = 100 minus that.
  2. Apply each percentage change to its own base.
  3. Find the missing new value from income = expenditure + savings.
  4. Compare new with old to get the percentage asked.

Worked example 2: both rise, savings jump

A man saves 25% of his income. Income rises by 20% and expenditure by 10%. Old: income 100, expenditure 75, savings 25. New: income 120, expenditure 82.5, so savings are 37.5. Savings rise by 12.5 on 25, which is 50%.

Worked example 3: the new savings rate

In the same case, the new savings rate is 37.5 ÷ 120 = 31.25% of the new income. A question may ask for this rate rather than the change in savings, so read the last line carefully.

Notice how a modest 20% rise in income produced a 50% rise in savings. Because savings are a small slice of income, any gap between the income change and the expenditure change is magnified when it lands on savings.

When the savings change is given, the expenditure is the unknown. Find new savings first, then subtract from new income.

Worked example 4: expenditure rise from savings

A person saves 20%. Income rises by 25% and savings by 50%. New income 125, new savings 30, so new expenditure is 95 against 80 before. Expenditure rises by 15 on 80, which is 18.75%.

Worked example 5: savings can fall

A person saves 30%. Income rises by 10% and expenditure by 20%. New income 110, new expenditure 84, so new savings are 26 against 30. Savings fall by about 13.33%, even though income went up.

Sometimes all three changes are given and the original savings rate is missing. Call it s. Then the old expenditure is 100 − s, and the new values must still add up:

New income = (100 − s) × (1 + e) + s × (1 + v), where e and v are the expenditure and savings changes.

Worked example 6: income +15%, expenditure +20%, savings +5%

115 = 1.20(100 − s) + 1.05s = 120 − 0.15s, so 0.15s = 5 and s = 33.33%. Check: expenditure 66.67 grows to 80, savings 33.33 grow to 35, and 80 + 35 = 115. The income change (15%) must always lie between the other two changes (5% and 20%); if a question's numbers break that rule, re-read it.

Worked example 7: income from a rupee change

A man saves 20%. Income rises by 10% and expenditure by 15%, and his savings fall by ₹600. Per 1 rupee of income, new savings are 1.10 − 0.8 × 1.15 = 0.18 against 0.20, a fall of 0.02. So 0.02 × income = 600, and income = ₹30,000.

The change in income is the weighted average of the changes in expenditure and savings, weighted by their original amounts. This is the same idea as alligation.

Worked example 8: checking example 2

Expenditure 75 rose by 10% and savings 25 rose by 50%. Weighted average: (75 × 10 + 25 × 50) ÷ 100 = 20%, exactly the income change. If your answer fails this check, recheck the bases.

For questions where price and consumption change together, see price and consumption, and for chained changes see successive percentage change.

  • Subtracting the percentages. Income +20% and expenditure +10% does not mean savings +10%.
  • Applying the expenditure change to income. A 10% rise in expenditure is 10% of the old expenditure, not of income.
  • Confusing savings change with savings rate. "Savings rise by 50%" and "savings are 31.25% of income" answer different questions.
  • Assuming savings must rise when income rises. A faster rise in spending can cut savings.
  • Skipping the final check. New expenditure plus new savings must equal new income.

Q1. A woman earns ₹50,000 a month and spends 68% of it. How much does she save? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹16,000

💡 Explanation: She saves 100% − 68% = 32% of ₹50,000, which is ₹16,000. ₹34,000 is her expenditure.

Q2. A man saves 20% of his income. His income rises by 30% and his expenditure by 25%. By what percentage do his savings rise? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 50%

💡 Explanation: Take income 100: expenditure 80, savings 20. New income 130, new expenditure 100, new savings 30. Savings rise by 10 on 20, which is 50%.

Q3. A clerk saves 40% of her income. Her income rises by 20% and her savings by 35%. By what percentage does her expenditure rise? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 10%

💡 Explanation: Income 100, savings 40, expenditure 60. New income 120, new savings 54, so new expenditure is 66. That is a rise of 6 on 60, or 10%.

Q4. Income rises by 20%, expenditure by 15% and savings by 40%. What percentage of the original income was saved? hard

✅ Answer: (b) 20%

💡 Explanation: With savings s: 120 = 1.15(100 − s) + 1.40s, so 120 = 115 + 0.25s and s = 20%.

Q5. A man saves 40% of his income. His income rises by 10% but his expenditure rises by 25%. What happens to his savings? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 12.5% decrease

💡 Explanation: Income 100, expenditure 60, savings 40. New income 110, new expenditure 75, new savings 35. Savings fall by 5 on 40, which is 12.5%.

Why take income as 100?

Every percentage in the question becomes a plain number. With income 100 and savings 20, a 30% rise in income is +30 and a 25% rise in an expenditure of 80 is +20, so the arithmetic stays in whole numbers.

Can savings fall when income rises?

Yes. If expenditure rises by a bigger percentage than income, and expenditure is the larger part, the extra spending can exceed the extra income. Savings then fall even though income went up.

What is the weighted-average shortcut?

The percentage change in income is the weighted average of the changes in expenditure and savings, weighted by their original sizes. It lets you find any one of the three changes from the other two, much like alligation.
