Time and Work by the LCM Method: Whole Numbers, No Fractions

Total work = LCM • Units per day • Pairs of workers • Efficiency • Leaving midway • Wages

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-20

The classic method for time and work uses fractions: if A finishes a job in 12 days, A does 1/12 of it each day. That is correct, but adding 1/12 and 1/18 under exam pressure is slow and error-prone. The LCM method removes the fractions by choosing a convenient size for the job.

Take the total work to be the LCM of the given numbers of days. Every worker then completes a whole number of units each day. Add the units of the people working together, divide the total work by that sum, and you have the time.

Total work = LCM of days  •  Daily units = total work ÷ days  •  Time = total work ÷ combined daily units

Worked example 1: two workers

A finishes a job in 12 days and B in 18. Total work = LCM(12, 18) = 36 units. A does 3 units a day and B does 2. Together they do 5 a day, so the job takes 36 ÷ 5 = 7.2 days.

Pair questions give the time for A + B, B + C and C + A. Adding the three pair rates counts each person twice, so halve the sum to get everyone working together. Then subtract a pair from the total to isolate one person.

Worked example 2: pairs

A + B take 10 days, B + C take 15 days and C + A take 12 days. Total work = LCM(10, 15, 12) = 60. The pairs do 6, 4 and 5 units a day. Then 2(A + B + C) = 15, so all three together do 7.5 units a day and finish in 60 ÷ 7.5 = 8 days.

To find C alone, subtract the A + B rate from the total: 7.5 − 6 = 1.5 units a day, so C alone needs 60 ÷ 1.5 = 40 days.

Sometimes the question gives a ratio instead of times: "A is twice as efficient as B." Efficiency is units per day, so let A do 2 units a day and B do 1. If they finish together in 14 days, the job is 14 × 3 = 42 units, and each person's own time follows.

Worked example 3: from efficiency to time

A is twice as efficient as B, and together they take 14 days. Total work = 14 × (2 + 1) = 42 units. B alone, at 1 unit a day, needs 42 days, and A alone needs 21.

Efficiency and time are inversely proportional for the same job. If A is three times as efficient as B, A takes one-third of B's time.

Split the job into stages. Work out how many units were completed while everyone was present, subtract from the total, and divide what is left by the rate of those still working.

Worked example 4: A leaves after 5 days

A takes 20 days and B 30. Total work = 60; A does 3 units a day and B 2. In the first 5 days they complete 25 units. B finishes the remaining 35 units in 35 ÷ 2 = 17.5 days.

When a question talks about numbers of identical workers, use worker-days instead of the LCM. The job size is workers × days, and that product stays the same however the work is divided.

Worked example 5: worker-days

12 workers finish a job in 15 days, so the job is 180 worker-days. 20 workers need 180 ÷ 20 = 9 days.

Payment is shared in proportion to the work done. When people work together for the whole job, that is the ratio of their daily units, which the LCM method gives you directly.

Worked example 6: wages

A takes 10 days and B takes 15, and together they earn ₹1,500. With total work = 30, A does 3 units a day and B does 2, so A's share is 1,500 × 3/5 = ₹900.

  • ✘ Adding days instead of rates. Two workers are always faster than the faster one alone.
  • ✘ Forgetting to halve the sum of the pair rates.
  • ✘ Sharing wages by days worked instead of work done.
  • ✘ Using the full-time rate for a worker who left partway through.

Pipes and tanks follow exactly the same method with outlets counted as negative work; see pipes and cisterns. More shortcuts are in the Quant cheat code.

Q1. A can do a job in 15 days and B in 10 days. How long do they take working together? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 6 days

💡 Explanation: Take total work = LCM(15, 10) = 30 units. A does 2 units a day and B 3, so together 5 units a day and 30 ÷ 5 = 6 days.

Q2. A and B finish a job in 12 days, B and C in 15 days, and C and A in 20 days. How long do A, B and C take together? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 10 days

💡 Explanation: Total work = 60 units. The pairs do 5, 4 and 3 units a day; their sum, 12, counts everyone twice, so A + B + C = 6 units a day and the job takes 10 days.

Q3. A is three times as efficient as B and finishes a job 20 days sooner than B. How long do they take together? hard

✅ Answer: (d) 7.5 days

💡 Explanation: If A takes t days, B takes 3t, and 3t − t = 20 gives t = 10. Total work = 30 units, with A at 3 and B at 1 a day, so together 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5 days.

Q4. A can do a job in 18 days and B in 24 days. They work together for 6 days, then A leaves. How many more days does B take to finish? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 10 days

💡 Explanation: Total work = 72 units: A does 4 a day, B 3. In 6 days they do 42 units, leaving 30, which B finishes in 30 ÷ 3 = 10 days.

Q5. 15 workers can finish a job in 20 days. After 8 days, 5 workers leave. How many more days does the job take? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 18 days

💡 Explanation: The job is 15 × 20 = 300 worker-days. In 8 days, 120 are done, leaving 180 for 10 workers: 180 ÷ 10 = 18 days.

Q6. A can do a job in 12 days and B in 16 days. They do it together and are paid ₹2,800. What is B's share? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹1,200

💡 Explanation: Total work = 48 units: A does 4 a day and B 3. Working for the same days, pay is shared 4 : 3, so B gets 2,800 × 3/7 = ₹1,200.

Why does the LCM method work?

The size of the job is arbitrary, so you can pick any convenient number of units. Choosing the LCM of all the given days makes every worker's daily output a whole number, so you add and subtract integers instead of fractions such as 1/12 and 1/18.

How are wages shared when workers work for different numbers of days?

Wages follow the work actually done, not the time spent. Multiply each worker's daily units by the days they worked, then share the payment in the ratio of those totals.
