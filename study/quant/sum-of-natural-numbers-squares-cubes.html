Sum of Squares of n Natural Numbers, Plus the Sum of Numbers and Cubes, with Odd, Even and Range Variants

The first formula comes from pairing terms: 1 + n, 2 + (n − 1), and so on, each pair adding to n + 1, with n/2 pairs. The cube formula is simply the first one squared, so in practice there are only two formulas to learn.

Worked example 1: 1 to 100

100 × 101 ÷ 2 = 5,050.

Worked example 2: squares 1 to 10

10 × 11 × 21 ÷ 6 = 2,310 ÷ 6 = 385.

Worked example 3: cubes 1 to 10

(10 × 11 ÷ 2)² = 55² = 3,025.

First find n, the number of terms. For odd numbers up to 49, n = (49 + 1)/2 = 25. For even numbers up to 100, n = 100/2 = 50.

Worked example 4: odd numbers 1 to 49

There are 25 odd numbers, so the sum is 25² = 625.

Worked example 5: even numbers 2 to 100

50 terms: 50 × 51 = 2,550, which is twice the sum 1 to 50.

Worked example 6: squares of the first five odd numbers

5 × 9 × 11 ÷ 3 = 165. Direct check: 1 + 9 + 25 + 49 + 81 = 165.

The even-squares formula follows from taking out a factor of 4: 2² + 4² + … + (2n)² = 4(1² + 2² + … + n²). This shortcut is often faster than remembering a separate formula.

When a sum starts above 1, subtract two sums that both start at 1:

a + … + b = (sum to b) − (sum to a − 1)

Worked example 7: squares from 11 to 20

Sum of squares to 20 = 20 × 21 × 41 ÷ 6 = 2,870. Sum to 10 = 385. So 11² + … + 20² = 2,485.

For an arithmetic progression that does not step by 1, such as 7 + 11 + 15 + …, use the AP formula instead: number of terms × (first term + last term) ÷ 2. The pairing idea is the same.

Test any formula on a tiny case before trusting it in an exam. For n = 2: numbers 1 + 2 = 3 (2 × 3 ÷ 2), squares 1 + 4 = 5 (2 × 3 × 5 ÷ 6), cubes 1 + 8 = 9 (3²). If the small case fails, you have misremembered the formula.

Worked example 8: direct addition as a check

Adding 1 + 9 + 25 + 49 + 81 by hand gives 165, matching the odd-squares formula in example 6. For small n, quick direct addition is a reliable check.

  • Wrong n. For odd or even sums, n is the number of terms, not the last term.
  • Forgetting to halve or divide by 6. The divisors are part of the formula.
  • Range errors. Subtract the sum to a − 1, not to a, or you lose the first term.
  • Squaring the sum of squares. Only the sum of cubes equals a square of the plain sum.
  • Mixing the odd and even square formulas. Derive the even one from 4 × (sum of squares) if unsure.

For identities such as (a + b)², see algebraic identities.

Q1. What is 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 50? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 1275

💡 Explanation: n(n + 1)/2 = 50 × 51 ÷ 2 = 1,275. 2,550 is the sum before halving.

Q2. What is 1² + 2² + … + 15²? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 1240

💡 Explanation: n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6 = 15 × 16 × 31 ÷ 6 = 1,240.

Q3. What is 1³ + 2³ + … + 8³? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 1296

💡 Explanation: The sum of cubes is the square of the sum: (8 × 9 ÷ 2)² = 36² = 1,296.

Q4. What is 51 + 52 + … + 100? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 3775

💡 Explanation: Sum to 100 minus sum to 50: 5,050 − 1,275 = 3,775.

Q5. What is 2² + 4² + 6² + … + 20²? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 1540

💡 Explanation: Each term is 4 times a square: 4(1² + … + 10²) = 4 × 385 = 1,540.

How can I remember the sum of squares formula?

n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6: three factors in a row, n, then one more, then 'double plus one', all over 6. Test it on n = 2: 2 × 3 × 5 ÷ 6 = 5 = 1 + 4.

Why is the sum of cubes the square of the sum?

It is a well-known identity: 1³ + 2³ + … + n³ = (1 + 2 + … + n)². For example 1 + 8 + 27 = 36 = 6². It means you only need to remember the first formula and square it.

How do I find a sum that does not start at 1?

Subtract two sums: the sum from a to b equals the sum to b minus the sum to (a − 1). For squares from 11 to 20, take the sum to 20 and subtract the sum to 10.
