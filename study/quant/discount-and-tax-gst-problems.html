GST Profit and Loss Problems: Discount Then Tax, Price Before Tax, the Discount Hidden in a Bill, and Profit

Final price = MP × (1 − d) × (1 + t) • Tax on the discounted price • Price before tax = bill ÷ (1 + t) • Finding the discount from a bill • Profit is worked before tax

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-03

Shops print a marked price (MP), take a discount off it, and then add GST on the price after the discount. Written as one line:

Final price = MP × (1 − discount%) × (1 + tax%)

GST profit loss problems in exams always state the tax rate in the question, so use the rate given rather than any rate you remember. Rates have changed over time; the arithmetic has not.

Worked example 1: 10% off, then 12% tax

MP ₹2,000. After the discount: 2,000 × 0.9 = 1,800. With tax: 1,800 × 1.12 = ₹2,016.

Worked example 2: does the order change the bill?

2,000 × 1.12 × 0.9 = ₹2,016, the same total, because multiplication can be done in any order. The tax amount, though, is 12% of the discounted 1,800, which is ₹216, not 12% of 2,000.

With more than one discount, apply them one after another before the tax. See successive discounts.

Worked example 3: two discounts and a tax

MP ₹5,000, discounts of 20% and 10%, tax 5%. 5,000 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 3,600, and 3,600 × 1.05 = ₹3,780.

The tax amount is the rate times the taxable value, the price after any discount.

Worked example 4: tax on ₹2,500 at 18%

0.18 × 2,500 = ₹450, so the bill is ₹2,950.

To go backwards from a bill that already includes tax, divide by (1 + rate). Subtracting the rate from the bill takes off too much, because the tax was calculated on the smaller, pre-tax amount.

Worked example 5: a bill of ₹1,180 at 18%

1,180 ÷ 1.18 = ₹1,000 before tax. Subtracting 18% of 1,180 would give 967.60, which is wrong.

Some questions give the marked price and the final bill and ask for the discount. Remove the tax first, then compare with the marked price.

Worked example 6: MP ₹1,500, paid ₹1,512 with 12% tax

Before tax: 1,512 ÷ 1.12 = 1,350. The discount is 150 on 1,500, which is 10%.

Comparing the bill directly with the marked price, 1,512 against 1,500, would suggest the item cost more than its label, which hides the discount altogether.

The seller collects GST on behalf of the government, so in exam questions the tax is not part of the seller's profit. Compare the selling price before tax with the cost price. For the core formulas, see profit and loss formulas and marked price and discount.

Worked example 7: profit after a discount

Cost ₹800, marked ₹1,200, discount 20%. The selling price is 960, so the profit is 160 on 800 = 20%, whatever tax the customer pays on top.

To hit a target profit after a discount, work backwards from the selling price you need.

Worked example 8: what should the marked price be?

A trader's cost is ₹1,000. He wants a 25% profit and plans a 20% discount. He needs a selling price of 1,250, so the marked price is 1,250 ÷ 0.8 = ₹1,562.50.

Sometimes a question asks only for the overall percentage change from the marked price to the bill. Multiply the two factors and compare with 1. A discount pulls the factor below 1 and a tax pushes it above 1, so the result can land on either side depending on the sizes.

Worked example 9: 10% off and 12% tax

0.9 × 1.12 = 1.008, so the customer pays 0.8% more than the marked price. Simply subtracting 10 from 12 would give 2%, which overstates the rise because the tax is charged on the smaller, discounted price.

The same factor method handles any chain: list every discount as (1 − d), every tax or markup as (1 + t), multiply them all, and read off the change. It also lets you compare two offers quickly without working out the rupee amounts, since the offer with the smaller combined factor is the cheaper one for the buyer.

  • Taxing the marked price. Tax is charged on the price after discount.
  • Subtracting the rate to remove tax. Divide by (1 + rate) instead.
  • Adding discount and tax percentages. 10% off and 12% tax is not a 2% rise; multiply the factors.
  • Counting tax as profit. Profit uses the pre-tax selling price.
  • Using a remembered rate. Always use the rate stated in the question.

Q1. An item marked ₹4,000 is sold at a 15% discount, and 18% GST is charged on the discounted price. What does the buyer pay? easy

✅ Answer: (a) ₹4,012

💡 Explanation: Discounted price = 4,000 × 0.85 = 3,400. With 18% tax: 3,400 × 1.18 = ₹4,012.

Q2. A bill of ₹2,100 includes 5% GST. What was the price before tax? easy

✅ Answer: (c) ₹2,000

💡 Explanation: Divide by 1.05: 2,100 ÷ 1.05 = ₹2,000. Taking 5% off 2,100 (₹1,995) is the common mistake.

Q3. A jacket is marked ₹2,500. After a discount and 18% GST, the customer pays ₹2,478. What was the discount? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 16%

💡 Explanation: Price before tax = 2,478 ÷ 1.18 = 2,100. The discount is 400 on 2,500, which is 16%.

Q4. What is the GST on a taxable value of ₹3,500 at 12%? easy

✅ Answer: (d) ₹420

💡 Explanation: Tax = rate × taxable value: 0.12 × 3,500 = ₹420, so the full bill would be ₹3,920.

Q5. A shopkeeper buys an item for ₹600 and marks it at ₹900. He gives a 10% discount and collects GST separately on top. What is his profit percentage? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 35%

💡 Explanation: His selling price before tax is 900 × 0.9 = 810. The tax is collected for the government, so profit = 210 on 600 = 35%.

Does it matter whether the discount or the tax is applied first?

For the final amount, no: multiplying by 0.9 and then 1.12 gives the same result as the reverse. But the tax amount itself is charged on the discounted price, so if a question asks how much tax was paid, use the price after discount.

Why divide by 1.18 instead of subtracting 18%?

The 18% was added to the pre-tax price, not to the bill. Subtracting 18% of the bill takes off too much. Dividing by 1.18 reverses the multiplication exactly.

Should GST be counted in the seller's profit?

In exam questions, no, unless the question says otherwise. The tax collected is passed on to the government, so profit compares the selling price before tax with the cost price.
