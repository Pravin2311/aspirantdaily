Successive Percentage Change: Combine Two or Three Changes with a + b + ab/100

Two changes • Decreases as negative numbers • Three changes • Area and volume • Working backwards

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

When a quantity changes twice in a row, the second change acts on the new value, not the original. A price that rises 20% and then 10% does not rise 30%: the second 10% is taken on a price that is already 20% higher, so it is worth more than 10% of the original. The extra piece is the "interest on interest" that the simple sum misses.

Take a base of 100 to see it. After a 20% rise the value is 120. A 10% rise on 120 adds 12, giving 132. The net change is 32%, and the extra 2% is 10% of the first 20%.

For two successive changes of a% and b%, the net percentage change is:

Net change = a + b + (a × b)/100

It comes straight from multiplying the growth factors: (1 + a/100)(1 + b/100) = 1 + (a + b)/100 + ab/10,000. Subtract 1 and multiply by 100 to get the formula.

Enter every decrease as a negative number. A positive answer means a net increase; a negative answer means a net decrease.

Worked example 1: two increases

Rent rises 20% one year and 10% the next. Net change = 20 + 10 + 200/100 = 32% increase.

Worked example 2: equal rise and fall

A price rises 20% and then falls 20%. Net change = 20 − 20 − 400/100 = −4%, a 4% fall. Whenever a quantity rises and falls by the same x%, in either order, it ends x²/100 per cent lower.

Apply the formula to the first two changes, then combine that result with the third. Or, faster, multiply the factors directly: a 10% rise is 1.1, a 25% fall is 0.75.

Worked example 3: three changes

Sales rise 10%, then 20%, then fall 25%. Factor = 1.1 × 1.2 × 0.75 = 0.99, a net change of −1%. Using the formula: 10 and 20 give 32; then 32 − 25 − 800/100 = −1%.

Worked example 4: the same rise three times

A value grows 10% a year for three years. Factor = 1.1³ = 1.331, a total rise of 33.1%, not 30%. This is exactly how compound interest works.

Any quantity that is a product of two others changes by the successive formula. Area = length × breadth, so a change of a% in length and b% in breadth changes area by a + b + ab/100. A square or circle uses the same change twice, because area depends on side × side or radius × radius.

Worked example 5: rectangle

Length rises 25% and breadth falls 20%. Area change = 25 − 20 − 500/100 = 0%. The area is unchanged, since 1.25 × 0.8 = 1.

Worked example 6: square

Each side of a square grows 10%. Area change = 10 + 10 + 100/100 = 21%.

The same idea covers expenditure = price × consumption. For the case where expenditure must stay fixed, see product constancy.

Year-on-year growth or decline is successive change applied to a real number. Multiply the starting value by each year's factor.

Worked example 7: population

A town of 50,000 grows 10% in the first year and 20% in the second. Population = 50,000 × 1.1 × 1.2 = 66,000.

To find the original value from the final one, divide by the factors instead of multiplying.

Worked example 8: finding the start

After rises of 20% and 25%, a machine's price is ₹9,000. Original = 9,000 ÷ (1.2 × 1.25) = 9,000 ÷ 1.5 = ₹6,000. A common error is to subtract 45% from 9,000.

  • Adding the percentages. 20% and 10% make 32%, not 30%.
  • Forgetting the sign. A fall of 25% is −25 in the formula, and the product term then becomes negative.
  • Expecting a rise and equal fall to cancel. They never do; the result is always a loss of x²/100 per cent.
  • Reversing a change by the same percentage. Undoing a 20% rise needs a fall of 20/120 = 16.67%, not 20%.
  • Discounts. Successive discounts follow the same rule; see the successive discount formula for shop-offer questions.

Q1. A price rises by 30% and then by a further 20%. What is the overall increase? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 56%

💡 Explanation: Net change = 30 + 20 + (30 × 20)/100 = 56%. Adding 30 and 20 misses the 6% earned on the first increase.

Q2. A salary is cut by 10% and the new salary is then raised by 10%. By what percentage is the final salary lower than the original? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 1%

💡 Explanation: Net change = 10 − 10 − (10 × 10)/100 = −1%, so the salary ends 1% lower. Equal rise and fall always leaves a loss of x²/100 per cent.

Q3. The radius of a circle is increased by 20%. By what percentage does its area increase? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 44%

💡 Explanation: Area depends on radius × radius, so apply 20% twice: 20 + 20 + 400/100 = 44%.

Q4. A share price rises 50%, falls 20% and then rises 10%. What is the net percentage change? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 32% increase

💡 Explanation: Multiply the factors: 1.5 × 0.8 × 1.1 = 1.32, a net increase of 32%.

Q5. A town of 1,20,000 people shrinks by 10% in one year and grows by 15% the next. What is the population after two years? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 1,24,200

💡 Explanation: 1,20,000 × 0.9 = 1,08,000, and 1,08,000 × 1.15 = 1,24,200. The net change is +3.5%, not +5%.

Does the order of two percentage changes matter?

No. A 20% rise followed by a 10% fall gives the same result as a 10% fall followed by a 20% rise, because multiplication by 1.2 and 0.9 can be done in either order. The formula a + b + ab/100 is symmetric for the same reason.

How is this different from the successive discount formula?

It is the same formula. A discount is just a decrease, so two discounts of a% and b% combine to a + b − ab/100 when written as positive numbers, which is the general rule with both changes entered as negatives.

When should I multiply factors instead of using a + b + ab/100?

With three or more changes, or with awkward percentages, multiplying factors such as 1.1 × 1.2 × 0.75 is quicker and less error-prone than applying the formula twice.
