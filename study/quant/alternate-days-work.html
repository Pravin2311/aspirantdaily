Working on Alternate Days Problems: Two-Day Cycles, Who Starts First, and Workers Who Leave Before the End

Give the job a size • Two-day cycles • Leftover work on the last day • Who starts matters • Leaving k days before completion • Helpers on every third day

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

All alternate-day problems become simple arithmetic once the job has a concrete size. Take the LCM of the individual times as the total work in units; then each worker's daily output is a whole number. This is the same idea as the LCM method for time and work.

If A takes 10 days and B 15, call the job 30 units. A does 3 units a day and B does 2.

Working in whole units avoids adding fractions such as 1/10 + 1/15 again and again, and it makes the leftover on the final day easy to see. If the LCM is large, any common multiple works just as well; the answer does not depend on the size you choose.

When two people take turns, group the days into cycles. One cycle (A's day plus B's day) does a fixed amount of work. Count how many whole cycles fit, then finish the remainder day by day.

Worked example 1: an exact fit

A takes 10 days and B 15; they alternate, A first. The job is 30 units and each cycle does 3 + 2 = 5 units in 2 days. Six cycles do 30 units exactly, so the job takes 12 days.

Worked example 2: work left over

A takes 12 days and B 18; they alternate, A first. Job = 36 units, A 3 a day, B 2 a day, 5 units per cycle. Seven cycles (14 days) do 35 units. One unit remains, and day 15 is A's, who needs 1/3 of a day. Total: 14⅓ days.

The key step is to stop the cycles before the job is finished, then hand the leftover to whoever's turn comes next.

With an exact fit, the order makes no difference. With a leftover, it does.

Worked example 3: B starts instead

Same job as example 2, but B goes first. After seven cycles (14 days) the same unit remains, and day 15 is now B's. B does 2 units a day, so needs half a day: total 14.5 days, longer than 14⅓. The faster worker should take the last turn, so starting with them usually saves time.

Two wordings are common. Set each up differently.

  • "A leaves after d days": work together for d days, subtract that work, and let the remaining worker finish.
  • "A leaves k days before the job is completed": let the total time be T. The stayer works T days and the leaver T − k days. Write one equation and solve for T.

Worked example 4: leaving after 4 days

A (10 days) and B (15 days) work together for 4 days, then A leaves. Job = 30 units; 4 days together do 20. B alone does the last 10 units at 2 a day in 5 days. Total: 9 days.

Worked example 5: leaving before the end

A (20 days) and B (30 days) start together, but A leaves 5 days before the job is finished. Job = 60 units; A does 3, B 2. 3(T − 5) + 2T = 60 gives 5T = 75, so T = 15 days.

The cycle idea extends to any repeating pattern: a helper every third day, or two helpers taking turns beside a regular worker.

Worked example 6: help every third day

A (20 days) works daily and B (30 days) joins on every third day. Job = 60 units; a 3-day cycle does 3 + 3 + (3 + 2) = 11 units. Five cycles (15 days) give 55. Day 16 is A alone, bringing the total to 58, and A needs 2/3 of day 17 for the last 2 units. Total: about 16.67 days.

  • Running the last cycle in full. The job may end partway through a cycle.
  • Using the cycle average for the leftover. On the last day, only the worker on duty counts.
  • Mixing up "leaves after" and "leaves before". The equations are different.
  • Forgetting the whole cycles in the final count. Add the cycle days to the leftover fraction.

For men, days and hours together, see the M1D1H1/W1 formula.

Q1. A can finish a job in 6 days and B in 12 days. They work on alternate days, A starting. In how many days is the job done? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 8 days

💡 Explanation: Job = 12 units; A does 2 a day and B 1. Each 2-day cycle does 3 units, so 4 cycles (8 days) finish the 12 units exactly.

Q2. A can finish a job in 8 days and B in 12 days. They work on alternate days, A starting. How long does the job take? medium

✅ Answer: (a) 9.5 days

💡 Explanation: Job = 24 units; A does 3 and B 2 a day. 4 cycles (8 days) give 20 units. Day 9 (A) brings it to 23, and B needs half of day 10 for the last unit: 9.5 days.

Q3. A can do a job in 15 days and B in 10 days. They start together, but B leaves 3 days before the job is finished. How long does the job take? hard

✅ Answer: (d) 7.8 days

💡 Explanation: Job = 30 units; A does 2 and B 3 a day. If the job takes T days, 2T + 3(T − 3) = 30, so 5T = 39 and T = 7.8 days.

Q4. A can do a job in 12 days and B in 24 days. They work together for 4 days, then A leaves. How many days in all does the job take? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 16 days

💡 Explanation: Job = 24 units; A does 2 and B 1 a day. Four days together give 12 units. B alone does the other 12 in 12 days, so the total is 16 days.

Q5. A, B and C can finish a job in 10, 12 and 15 days. A works every day, helped by B on odd-numbered days and by C on even-numbered days. How long does the job take? hard

✅ Answer: (b) 5.7 days

💡 Explanation: Job = 60 units; A 6, B 5, C 4 a day. Days alternate between 11 and 10 units. After 5 days 53 units are done, and day 6 (A + C, 10 units) needs 0.7 of the day for the last 7.

Why does it matter who starts?

When the job does not end exactly on a cycle boundary, the leftover work falls to whoever's turn it is. A faster worker finishes the leftover sooner, so starting with the faster worker usually gives a slightly shorter total.

Can I use average daily work for alternate days?

Only for whole cycles. The average of 2.5 units a day in a 3-and-2 cycle is correct over pairs of days, but the last part must be done day by day, because the worker on duty that day does it alone.

How do I set up 'leaves k days before completion'?

Let the total time be T. The worker who stays works T days and the one who leaves works T − k days. Add their contributions, set the sum equal to the job, and solve for T.
