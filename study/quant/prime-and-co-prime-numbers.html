How to Check a Prime Number Quickly: The Square-Root Test, 6k ± 1, Co-Prime Pairs and Counting Co-Primes

Prime, composite, neither • Test primes up to √n • The 6k ± 1 filter • Disguised composites like 221 and 323 • Co-prime pairs • How many numbers are co-prime to n

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and so on. A composite number has more than two factors, such as 12 = 2 × 2 × 3. The number 1 is neither, because it has only one factor.

  • 2 is the only even prime. Every other even number is divisible by 2.
  • There are 25 primes up to 100 and 168 up to 1,000.
  • Every whole number above 1 is either prime or a product of primes in exactly one way.

Prime questions in SSC and railway papers test two things: whether you can check a number quickly, and whether you recognise numbers that only look prime.

The answer to how to check a prime number quickly is to try dividing only by primes up to its square root. If none divides it, the number is prime.

  1. Find the whole-number part of √n. For 97 it is 9, because 9² = 81 and 10² = 100.
  2. List the primes up to that value: 2, 3, 5, 7.
  3. Test each one. If any divides n, it is composite; if none does, it is prime.

Worked example 1: is 97 prime?

97 is odd, its digit sum 16 is not a multiple of 3, it does not end in 0 or 5, and 97 ÷ 7 leaves 6. All 4 tests fail to divide it, so 97 is prime.

Use the divisibility rules for 2, 3, 5 and 11 to clear those primes in a glance, and divide only for 7, 13, 17 and beyond. For numbers up to 1,000 you never need a prime above 31, since 31² = 961 and 37² is already 1,369.

Some numbers pass the quick checks for 2, 3, 5 and 7 and look prime, but are products of two larger primes. Examiners like these because they catch anyone who stops testing early.

Worked example 2: 221

√221 is about 14.9, so test up to 13. 221 ÷ 13 = 17 exactly, with remainder 0. 221 = 13 × 17 is composite.

Worked example 3: 323

√323 is about 17.97, so test up to 17. 323 ÷ 17 = 19, remainder 0. 323 = 17 × 19.

Worked example 4: 1,001

1,001 = 7 × 11 × 13 = 1001. It is worth memorising, along with 91 = 7 × 13, 119 = 7 × 17, 143 = 11 × 13, 187 = 11 × 17 and 209 = 11 × 19.

Every prime greater than 3 is one more or one less than a multiple of 6. Numbers of the form 6k, 6k + 2 and 6k + 4 are even, and 6k + 3 is a multiple of 3, which leaves only 6k − 1 and 6k + 1.

Worked example 5: 89

89 ÷ 6 leaves remainder 5, so 89 = 6 × 15 − 1 and passes the filter. Testing 2, 3, 5 and 7 confirms it is prime.

The filter only rules numbers out. 25 = 6 × 4 + 1 passes it but is 5 × 5, so you still need the square-root test for anything that survives.

Two numbers are co-prime (relatively prime) when their HCF is 1, that is, they share no prime factor. Neither number has to be prime.

Worked example 6: 15 and 28

15 = 3 × 5 and 28 = 2² × 7 share no prime, so the HCF is 1: they are co-prime.

Worked example 7: 21 and 35

Both are multiples of 7, so the HCF is 7 and they are not co-prime.

Two facts make co-primes useful. Any two consecutive numbers are co-prime, and for a co-prime pair the LCM is simply the product.

Worked example 8: LCM of 8 and 15

They are co-prime, so LCM = 8 × 15 = 120. For more on this, see HCF and LCM of fractions.

To count the numbers from 1 to n that are co-prime to n, write n's distinct prime factors p, q, … and use n × (1 − 1/p) × (1 − 1/q) × …

Worked example 9: co-primes to 36

36 = 2² × 3², so the count is 36 × 1/2 × 2/3 = 12: 1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31 and 35. The powers do not matter, only the distinct primes.

  • Calling 1 a prime. It is neither prime nor composite.
  • Stopping at 7. For numbers above 121, test 11, 13 and beyond up to the square root.
  • Thinking co-primes must be primes. 8 and 15 are co-prime but both composite.
  • Trusting the 6k ± 1 filter alone. 25, 35 and 49 all pass it.
  • Using repeated primes in the co-prime count. For 36, use 2 and 3 once each.

For counting all the factors of a number, see number of factors.

Q1. Which of these numbers is prime? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 113

💡 Explanation: 91 = 7 × 13, 119 = 7 × 17 and 133 = 7 × 19. 113 is not divisible by 2, 3, 5 or 7, and √113 is below 11, so it is prime.

Q2. Which pair of numbers is co-prime? easy

✅ Answer: (a) (16, 27)

💡 Explanation: 16 = 2⁴ and 27 = 3³ share no prime factor, so their HCF is 1. The other pairs share 7, 5 and 6 respectively.

Q3. How many prime numbers lie between 50 and 70? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 4

💡 Explanation: Check the odd numbers 51 to 69 for 3, 5 and 7: only 53, 59, 61 and 67 survive. That makes 4 primes.

Q4. To test whether 437 is prime, what is the largest prime you need to try as a divisor? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 19

💡 Explanation: √437 is about 20.9, so the largest prime to try is 19. In fact 437 = 19 × 23, so it is composite.

Q5. How many numbers from 1 to 30 are co-prime to 30? hard

✅ Answer: (c) 8

💡 Explanation: 30 = 2 × 3 × 5, so the count is 30 × (1/2) × (2/3) × (4/5) = 8: 1, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 and 29.

Is 1 a prime number?

No. A prime has exactly two distinct factors, 1 and itself. The number 1 has only one factor, so it is neither prime nor composite.

Why is it enough to test divisors up to the square root?

If n = a × b with both factors above √n, their product would exceed n. So any composite number has at least one factor no bigger than √n, and checking primes up to √n is enough.

Do co-prime numbers have to be prime?

No. Co-prime only means the HCF is 1. 8 and 15 are both composite but co-prime, because they share no prime factor.
