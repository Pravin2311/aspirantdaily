Bus Stops Per Hour Problems: Speed With and Without Stoppages, Stoppage Time and Number of Stops

Stoppage minutes per hour • Speed with stoppages from the stop time • Speed without stoppages • Total stopped time on a journey • Number of stops • Moving-time fraction

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

A bus that stops at stations covers less ground in an hour than its running speed suggests. Two speeds describe it:

  • Speed excluding stoppages (S₁): how fast it goes while actually moving.
  • Speed including stoppages (S₂): total distance divided by total time, stops included.

In one hour the bus covers S₂ km instead of S₁ km. The missing S₁ − S₂ km are the distance it would have run during the minutes it stood still. At the running speed S₁, that distance takes (S₁ − S₂)/S₁ hours.

Stoppage time per hour = (S₁ − S₂) ÷ S₁ × 60 minutes

Bus stops per hour problems in SSC and railway papers almost always reduce to this one line, read forwards or backwards. For the underlying speed, time and distance relation, see speed, time and distance basics.

Worked example 1: 60 and 50 km/h

The bus loses 10 km each hour. At 60 km/h, 10 km takes 10/60 of an hour, which is 10 minutes.

Worked example 2: 80 and 60 km/h

(80 − 60)/80 × 60 = 20/80 × 60 = 15 minutes per hour. A common wrong answer is 20 minutes, which is the speed difference read as time.

A useful way to see it: the bus is moving for S₂/S₁ of every hour. Here that is 60/80 = 3/4, so it moves for 45 minutes and stands for 15.

Worked example 3: the moving fraction

For 60 and 50 km/h, the bus is moving for 50/60 = 5/6 of each hour, that is 50 minutes, and stopped for the other 10.

If the stop time t (in minutes per hour) is given, the bus moves for (60 − t) minutes each hour.

  • Speed with stoppages: S₂ = S₁ × (60 − t)/60
  • Speed without stoppages: S₁ = S₂ × 60/(60 − t)

Worked example 4: S₁ = 72, stops 10 minutes

It moves for 50 minutes each hour: 72 × 50/60 = 60 km/h including stoppages.

Worked example 5: S₂ = 42, stops 12 minutes

The 42 km are covered in 48 minutes of movement, so S₁ = 42 × 60/48 = 52.5 km/h.

Before multiplying, check that the answer will be the right way round: the speed excluding stoppages must always be the larger of the two.

For a full journey, compare the total time at each speed. The difference is the total time spent standing still.

Worked example 6: a 300 km journey

S₁ = 75 and S₂ = 60. Total time = 300/60 = 5 hours; moving time = 300/75 = 4 hours. The bus is stopped for 1 hour in all. Per hour that is 12 minutes, the same as (75 − 60)/75 × 60.

If each stop lasts a fixed time, divide the stoppage minutes per hour by the length of one stop to get the number of stops.

Worked example 7: 2-minute stops

A train runs at 90 km/h without stoppages and 78 km/h with them, and each stop lasts 2 minutes. Stoppage time = 12/90 × 60 = 8 minutes per hour, so the train makes 4 stops an hour.

Comparison questions mix a vehicle that stops with one that does not. Convert the stopping vehicle to its speed including stoppages first, because that is the speed that decides who arrives first.

Worked example 8: express bus or local bus?

A local bus runs at 60 km/h but stops for 10 minutes every hour. An express bus runs non-stop at 54 km/h. The local bus averages 60 × 50/60 = 50 km/h, so the express is faster by 4 km/h, even though its running speed is lower. Over a 270 km route the express takes 5 hours and the local bus 5 hours 24 minutes.

The same reasoning works for anything that pauses at regular intervals, such as a cyclist who rests or a machine that halts for maintenance. For a related idea, see average speed formula.

  • Dividing by the slower speed. The lost distance is run at S₁, so divide by S₁.
  • Answering with the speed difference. 20 km/h lost is not 20 minutes stopped.
  • Forgetting the × 60. (S₁ − S₂)/S₁ is a fraction of an hour; convert to minutes.
  • Swapping the two speeds. Including stoppages is always the lower speed.
  • Ignoring stop length. For the number of stops, divide total stop minutes by minutes per stop.

Q1. Excluding stoppages, a bus runs at 48 km/h; including stoppages, its speed is 40 km/h. For how many minutes per hour does it stop? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 10 minutes

💡 Explanation: It loses 8 km of travel each hour, which takes 8/48 of an hour at full speed: 8/48 × 60 = 10 minutes.

Q2. A train's speed is 75 km/h without stoppages and 60 km/h with stoppages. How many minutes per hour does it stop? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 12 minutes

💡 Explanation: (75 − 60)/75 × 60 = 15/75 × 60 = 12 minutes. 15 is the speed difference, not the time.

Q3. A train runs at 90 km/h without stoppages and stops for 8 minutes every hour. What is its average speed including stoppages? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 78 km/h

💡 Explanation: It moves for 52 minutes each hour, covering 90 × 52/60 = 78 km.

Q4. Including stoppages, a bus averages 36 km/h, and it stops for 20 minutes every hour. What is its speed without stoppages? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 54 km/h

💡 Explanation: The 36 km is covered in 40 minutes of movement, so the running speed is 36 × 60/40 = 54 km/h.

Q5. A bus travels 240 km. Its speed is 80 km/h without stoppages and 60 km/h with stoppages. How long does it spend stopped on the whole journey? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 1 hour

💡 Explanation: The journey takes 240/60 = 4 hours in total but only 240/80 = 3 hours of movement, so it is stopped for 1 hour.

Why divide by the speed without stoppages and not the speed with stoppages?

The lost distance per hour (the speed difference) would have been covered at the running speed. Time = distance ÷ speed, so the minutes lost are the difference divided by the speed without stoppages.

Does the length of the journey matter?

Not for the minutes stopped per hour, which depend only on the two speeds. The journey length matters only when the question asks for the total time spent stopped.

What does 'excluding stoppages' mean?

It is the speed while the vehicle is actually moving. 'Including stoppages' is the average speed over the whole time, counting the minutes spent standing at stops.
