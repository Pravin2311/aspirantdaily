Mean, Median and Mode Formula for Grouped Data, with Ungrouped Examples

Ungrouped mean, median, mode • Grouped mean Σfx ÷ Σf • Median L + (N/2 − cf) ÷ f × h • Mode with f₀, f₁, f₂ • Empirical relation • Effect of changing every value

SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-23

Mean, median and mode each describe the centre of a set of data in a different way. The mean adds everything and divides by the count. The median is the middle value when the data are in order. The mode is the value that occurs most often. Questions either give a short list of numbers, ungrouped data, or a frequency table with classes such as 0–10 and 10–20, grouped data.

Worked example 1: the mean

The mean of 4, 7, 9, 10 and 15 is 45 ÷ 5 = 9.

For the median, sort the values first. With an odd count, the median is the middle value. With an even count, it is the average of the two middle values. For example, 3, 8, 5, 12, 7 sorts to 3, 5, 7, 8, 12, with median 7.

Worked example 2: an even count

4, 9, 2, 11, 6 and 8 sort to 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11. The two middle values are 6 and 8, so the median is 7.

The mode needs no calculation, only a count. In 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 3, 5 the value 3 appears three times, more than any other, so the mode is 3. A set can have no mode, or more than one.

For a frequency table, take the midpoint x of each class, multiply by its frequency f, add the products, and divide by the total frequency:

Mean = Σfx ÷ Σf

Worked example 3: grouped mean

Σfx = 380 and Σf = 20, so the mean is 380 ÷ 20 = 19.

For the median, find N/2, then the median class: the first class whose cumulative frequency reaches N/2. Then:

Median = L + ((N/2 − cf) ÷ f) × h

Here L is the lower limit of the median class, cf the cumulative frequency before it, f its frequency and h the class width.

Worked example 4: grouped median

N/2 = 10. The cumulative frequency first reaches 10 in the class 10–20, so L = 10, cf = 4, f = 6 and h = 10. Median = 10 + (10 − 4) ÷ 6 × 10 = 20.

For the mode, the modal class is the one with the highest frequency, f₁. Let f₀ be the frequency of the class before it and f₂ the class after it:

Mode = L + ((f₁ − f₀) ÷ (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)) × h

Worked example 5: grouped mode

The modal class is 20–30 with f₁ = 8, f₀ = 6 and f₂ = 2. Mode = 20 + (8 − 6) ÷ (16 − 6 − 2) × 10 = 20 + 2.5 = 22.5.

For moderately skewed data the three measures are linked approximately by:

Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean

Worked example 6: finding the mode

If the mean is 30 and the median is 28, the mode is about 3 × 28 − 2 × 30 = 24.

If every value is changed in the same way, the mean, median and mode all change in that way too. Adding a constant shifts each measure by that constant; multiplying scales each by the same factor.

Worked example 7: transforming data

Data with mean 9 are doubled and then increased by 5. The new mean is 2 × 9 + 5 = 23.

When two groups are joined, the new mean is not the simple average of the two means unless the groups are the same size. Multiply each mean by its group size to get each total, add the totals, and divide by the combined size. This is the weighted mean, and it is the same idea as Σfx ÷ Σf with the group means playing the part of the midpoints.

Worked example 8: two sections of a class

Section A has 20 students with a mean score of 15, and section B has 30 students with a mean of 20. The totals are 300 and 600, so the combined mean is 900 ÷ 50 = 18, closer to the larger section's mean.

  • ✘ Using the median class's own cumulative frequency as cf. Use the one before it.
  • ✘ Treating Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean as exact.

Q1. What is the mean of 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 18

💡 Explanation: The sum is 90 and there are 5 values, so the mean is 18. Because the values are evenly spaced, the mean is also the middle value.

Q2. What is the median of 10, 3, 7, 15, 12 and 6? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 8.5

💡 Explanation: In order the values are 3, 6, 7, 10, 12, 15. With an even count, the median is the average of the two middle values: (7 + 10) ÷ 2 = 8.5.

Q3. For a moderately skewed distribution the mean is 40 and the mode is 34. What is the median, by the empirical relation? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 38

💡 Explanation: Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean, so 34 = 3 Median − 80. Then 3 Median = 114 and the median is 38.

Q4. Find the mean of the data: class 0–20 with frequency 3, 20–40 with frequency 5, 40–60 with frequency 2. medium

✅ Answer: (b) 28

💡 Explanation: Use class midpoints 10, 30 and 50. Σfx = 30 + 150 + 100 = 280 and Σf = 10, so the mean is 280 ÷ 10 = 28.

Q5. Find the mode of the data: class 10–20 with frequency 4, 20–30 with frequency 10, 30–40 with frequency 6. hard

✅ Answer: (c) 26

💡 Explanation: The modal class is 20–30, with f₁ = 10, f₀ = 4 and f₂ = 6. Mode = 20 + (10 − 4) ÷ (20 − 4 − 6) × 10 = 20 + 6 = 26.

Q6. The mean of 10 numbers is 25. Each number is multiplied by 3 and then increased by 4. What is the new mean? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 79

💡 Explanation: The mean changes in exactly the same way as each value: 25 × 3 + 4 = 79.

Why are class midpoints used for the grouped mean?

In grouped data the individual values are lost; only the class and its frequency are known. The midpoint is taken as a fair representative of every value in the class, so the grouped mean is an estimate, not an exact value.

Is Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean always true?

No. It is an approximate empirical relation that works well for moderately skewed distributions. Use it when a question asks for it or gives two of the three measures and no data, but do not rely on it for exact calculations.
