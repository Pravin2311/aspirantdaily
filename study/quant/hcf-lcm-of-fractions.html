HCF and LCM of Fractions Formula, Plus Decimals: Numerator and Denominator Rules with Worked Examples

HCF = HCF of numerators ÷ LCM of denominators • LCM = LCM of numerators ÷ HCF of denominators • Reduce first • Decimals by scaling • Bells and tiles

SSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

For fractions in their lowest terms:

HCF of fractions = HCF of the numerators ÷ LCM of the denominators

LCM of fractions = LCM of the numerators ÷ HCF of the denominators

A way to remember it: the HCF should be small, so it takes the small part on top (HCF) and the big part below (LCM). The LCM should be large, so it is the other way round.

Why this works: a fraction p/q divides a/b exactly when (a/b) ÷ (p/q) = aq/(bp) is a whole number. That needs p to divide every numerator and q to be a multiple of every denominator. The largest such fraction therefore has the biggest possible p (the HCF of the numerators) over the smallest possible q (the LCM of the denominators). The LCM argument runs the same way in reverse. Once you see this, you never need to memorise which way round the rules go; you can rebuild them in a few seconds during the exam.

Worked example 1: HCF of 2/3, 4/9 and 8/15

HCF of 2, 4, 8 is 2. LCM of 3, 9, 15 is 45. HCF = 2/45.

Worked example 2: LCM of the same fractions

LCM of 2, 4, 8 is 8. HCF of 3, 9, 15 is 3. LCM = 8/3.

Check: 8/3 ÷ 2/3 = 4, 8/3 ÷ 4/9 = 6 and 8/3 ÷ 8/15 = 5, all whole numbers, so 8/3 is a common multiple of the three fractions.

The rules only work for fractions in lowest terms. Equal fractions written differently would otherwise give different answers.

Worked example 3: 4/6 and 6/9

Both reduce to 2/3, so the HCF is 2/3. Using the unreduced fractions gives HCF(4, 6)/LCM(6, 9) = 2/18 = 1/9, which is wrong.

The product rule also carries over to two fractions: HCF × LCM = product of the fractions.

Worked example 4: checking the product rule

For 2/3 and 4/9: HCF = 2/9 and LCM = 4/3. Their product is 8/27, and 2/3 × 4/9 is also 8/27: true.

Scale every decimal to whole numbers by multiplying by the same power of 10, find the HCF or LCM of the whole numbers, then divide back by that power of 10.

  1. Give every number the same count of decimal places (add zeros where needed).
  2. Remove the decimal point.
  3. Find the HCF or LCM.
  4. Put the decimal point back with the same number of places.

Worked example 5: HCF of 0.6, 0.9 and 1.5

As whole numbers: 6, 9, 15. HCF = 3, so the answer is 0.3.

Worked example 6: LCM of the same decimals

LCM of 6, 9, 15 is 90, so the answer is 9 (90 tenths).

Worked example 7: different decimal places

HCF of 1.2 and 0.36: write as 1.20 and 0.36, scale to 120 and 36, HCF 12, so the answer is 0.12.

The usual HCF and LCM word problems (largest tile, bells ringing together) appear with fractions and decimals too. Decide first whether you need HCF (cutting into the largest equal pieces) or LCM (things coinciding), exactly as with whole numbers. See HCF and LCM word problems.

Worked example 8: bells at fractional intervals

Three bells ring every 1/2, 3/4 and 5/6 of an hour. They ring together again after the LCM: LCM(1, 3, 5)/HCF(2, 4, 6) = 15/2 hours, or 7 hours 30 minutes.

With units such as metres and centimetres, converting to the smaller unit often removes the fractions entirely and turns the question into a whole-number HCF.

Worked example 9: the longest measuring rod

Three ropes are 1.8 m, 2.7 m and 4.5 m long. What is the longest rod that measures each rope an exact number of times? In centimetres the lengths are 180, 270 and 450, whose HCF is 90 cm. The rod fits 2, 3 and 5 times respectively, which confirms the answer.

  • Swapping the rules. HCF uses the LCM of denominators; LCM uses the HCF of denominators.
  • Not reducing fractions first. 4/6 must become 2/3 before applying either rule.
  • Different decimal places. Pad with zeros so every number has the same number of places.
  • Forgetting to rescale. After finding the HCF of 120 and 36, put the decimal point back.
  • Mixing units. Convert metres and centimetres to one unit before finding the HCF.

Q1. What is the HCF of 3/4, 9/10 and 6/25? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 3/100

💡 Explanation: HCF of numerators (3, 9, 6) = 3; LCM of denominators (4, 10, 25) = 100. HCF = 3/100.

Q2. What is the LCM of 2/5, 3/10 and 6/25? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 6/5

💡 Explanation: LCM of numerators (2, 3, 6) = 6; HCF of denominators (5, 10, 25) = 5. LCM = 6/5.

Q3. What is the HCF of 1.75, 5.6 and 7? medium

✅ Answer: (d) 0.35

💡 Explanation: Scale to two decimal places: 175, 560 and 700. Their HCF is 35, so the answer is 0.35.

Q4. What is the LCM of 0.8, 1.2 and 2? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 12

💡 Explanation: Scale by 10: 8, 12 and 20 have LCM 120. Divide back by 10 to get 12.

Q5. A floor is 4.5 m by 3.75 m. What is the side of the largest square tile that fits exactly, with no cutting? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 75 cm

💡 Explanation: In centimetres the sides are 450 and 375. The largest tile side is their HCF, 75 cm.

Why does the HCF of fractions use the LCM of the denominators?

The HCF must divide each fraction a whole number of times. A bigger denominator makes a fraction smaller, so dividing by the LCM of the denominators gives the largest fraction that still fits into every one.

Do I need to reduce fractions to lowest terms first?

Yes. 4/6 and 6/9 are both 2/3, so their HCF is 2/3. Using 4/6 and 6/9 as written gives the wrong answer 1/9.

How do I handle a mix of fractions and decimals?

Convert everything to fractions in lowest terms, or everything to decimals with the same number of places, and then apply one method throughout.
