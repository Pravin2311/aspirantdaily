Races and Circular Track Problems: Beats, Starts, Dead Heats, Circular Track Meeting Time and Meeting at the Start

Beats by metres and by seconds • Chained races • Starts and dead heats • First meeting on a circular track • Meeting at the starting point • Number of meeting points

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

Race questions use a few fixed phrases. Translate each into a statement about distances run in the same time:

  • A beats B by x m in an L m race: when A finishes L m, B has run L − x m.
  • A beats B by t seconds: B finishes t seconds after A.
  • A gives B a start of x m: B begins x m ahead, so B runs only L − x m.
  • Dead heat: both finish at the same moment.

In the same time, distances are in the ratio of speeds. That single fact drives every race calculation; see relative speed for the underlying idea.

When a question gives both a distance margin and a time margin, they describe the same gap: the loser needs t seconds to cover the last x metres. So the loser's speed is x ÷ t.

Worked example 1: 20 m or 4 seconds

In a 400 m race, A beats B by 20 m or 4 s. B covers 20 m in 4 s, so B's speed is 5 m/s and B takes 400 ÷ 5 = 80 s. A finishes 4 s earlier, in 76 s.

Chained races need scaling, because each margin applies to a different distance.

Worked example 2: A beats B, B beats C

In a 100 m race, A beats B by 10 m and B beats C by 10 m. When A runs 100, B runs 90. B and C run in the ratio 100 : 90, so while B runs 90, C runs 81. A beats C by 19 m, not 20.

For a dead heat, the slower runner's distance must be in the same ratio to the full distance as the speeds.

Worked example 3: how much start?

A runs 5/3 times as fast as B. In a 200 m race, while A runs 200 m, B runs 200 × 3/5 = 120 m. For a dead heat, A must give B a start of 200 − 120 = 80 m.

If a question gives both a start and a margin, add or subtract them carefully: if A gives B a 10 m start and still wins by 5 m, then while A runs the full distance L, B runs only L − 15 m.

Two runners leaving one point on a circular track meet again whenever the gap between them equals a whole lap. So the track length plays the role of the distance in a relative-speed problem:

Opposite directions: first meeting time = track length ÷ (a + b)

Same direction: first meeting time = track length ÷ (a − b)

Worked example 4: opposite directions

A 600 m track, speeds 4 m/s and 6 m/s in opposite directions. They first meet after 600 ÷ 10 = 60 s.

Worked example 5: same direction

Same track and speeds, same direction. The faster runner gains 2 m/s and needs a full lap's lead: 600 ÷ 2 = 300 s.

Meeting anywhere is not the same as meeting at the starting point. They are back at the start together only when each has finished a whole number of laps, so use the LCM of the lap times.

Worked example 6: two runners at the start

On the 600 m track, the lap times are 150 s and 100 s. They are next at the start together after LCM(150, 100) = 300 s, whichever direction they run.

Worked example 7: three runners

Lap times 30, 40 and 60 s. All three are next at the start after LCM = 120 s.

To count distinct meeting points, write the speeds in lowest terms as a : b. In the same direction they meet at |a − b| distinct points; in opposite directions, at a + b points.

Worked example 8: how many meeting points?

Speeds 4 and 6 m/s reduce to 2 : 3. Running in opposite directions they meet at 2 + 3 = 5 distinct points; in the same direction at only 1, the starting point.

Common traps: adding margins in chained races, using the first-meeting formula when the question asks about the starting point, and forgetting to convert km/h to m/s on tracks measured in metres.

Q1. In a 1 km race, A beats B by 50 m and B beats C by 40 m. By how much does A beat C? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 88 m

💡 Explanation: When A runs 1,000 m, B runs 950 m. B runs 1,000 m while C runs 960 m, so in 950 m C runs 950 × 0.96 = 912 m. A wins by 88 m, not 90.

Q2. Two runners start together on a 400 m circular track and run in opposite directions at 5 m/s and 3 m/s. When do they first meet? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 50 s

💡 Explanation: Opposite directions: they close the 400 m loop at 5 + 3 = 8 m/s, so they meet after 400 ÷ 8 = 50 s.

Q3. The same two runners now run in the same direction. When does the faster one first catch the slower one? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 200 s

💡 Explanation: Same direction: the faster runner gains 5 − 3 = 2 m/s and must gain one full lap of 400 m. 400 ÷ 2 = 200 s.

Q4. Three cyclists complete a lap in 12, 18 and 30 minutes. If they start together from the same point, after how long are they next all at the start together? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 180 min

💡 Explanation: They meet at the start when each has done a whole number of laps: LCM(12, 18, 30) = 180 minutes.

Q5. In a 300 m race, A beats B by 30 m or by 6 seconds. How long does A take to finish? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 54 s

💡 Explanation: B covers 30 m in 6 s, so B's speed is 5 m/s and B's time is 60 s. A finishes 6 s sooner, in 54 s.

Why can't I add the margins in a chained race?

Because each margin is measured over a different stretch. A beats B over the full distance, but B beats C over B's full distance, which is longer than what B ran when A finished. Scale the second margin to the distance B actually ran.

What is a dead heat?

A dead heat is a race where both runners finish at exactly the same moment. Questions ask how much start the slower runner needs, or how much shorter the slower runner's distance must be, for this to happen.

Do circular track answers depend on where they meet?

The first-meeting time does not. It depends only on the track length and the relative speed. Meeting at the starting point is a separate question, answered with the LCM of the lap times.
