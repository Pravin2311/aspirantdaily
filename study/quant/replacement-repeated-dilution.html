Milk Replaced by Water Repeatedly: The Formula V(1 − x/V)ⁿ, Final Ratios and Backward Questions

Remaining = V(1 − x/V)ⁿ • Why it compounds • Final milk-to-water ratio • Finding the quantity removed • Finding the number of operations • Starting from a mixture

SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-02

A vessel holds V litres of pure milk. Each time, x litres are taken out and replaced with x litres of water, so the total volume stays V. After the first operation, the milk is V − x. After that, every draw removes a mixture, so less milk leaves each round.

The fix is to see that every operation keeps the same fraction of the milk: (V − x)/V. Repeating it n times multiplies by that fraction n times. That gives the milk replaced by water repeatedly formula:

Milk left after n operations = V × (1 − x/V)ⁿ    Water = V − milk left

It is the same pattern as depreciation: a fixed percentage lost each round. See population growth and depreciation.

Worked example 1: 40 litres, 4 litres three times

Each round keeps 36/40 = 0.9 of the milk. After 3 rounds: 40 × 0.9³ = 40 × 0.729 = 29.16 litres. Subtracting 4 litres three times would wrongly give 28.

Tracing it by hand shows why: after round 1 the can holds 36 L milk and 4 L water. Round 2 removes 4 L of mixture, which is 90% milk, so it takes 3.6 L of milk, leaving 32.4. Round 3 takes 3.24 L, leaving 29.16.

Once you have the milk left, the water is the rest of the vessel. The ratio follows directly.

Worked example 2: the ratio after example 1

Milk 29.16 and water 10.84 litres. Scaling both by 25 gives 729 : 271, so the ratio is 729 : 271. A neat way to see it: milk is 0.729 of the can, and 0.729 = 729/1,000.

Worked example 3: water after two rounds

80 litres of milk; 8 litres replaced twice. Milk left = 80 × 0.81 = 64.8, so the water is 80 − 64.8 = 15.2 litres.

Worked example 4: as a percentage

A cask holds 100 litres of wine; 10 litres are drawn and replaced with water twice. Wine is (0.9)² = 81% of the cask. The percentage form avoids working with the volume at all.

If the final quantity is given, divide by V and take the n-th root to find the fraction kept per round.

Worked example 5: how much was removed each time?

A 50-litre can of milk goes through two equal replacements and ends with 32 litres of milk. (1 − x/50)² = 32/50 = 0.64, so 1 − x/50 = 0.8 and x = 10 litres.

Worked example 6: how many operations?

64 litres of milk; 16 litres replaced each time; 27 litres of milk remain. The fraction kept is 3/4, and (3/4)ⁿ = 27/64 = (3/4)³, so there were 3 operations. Check: 64 × (3/4)³ = 27.

Exam numbers are chosen so these roots come out exactly. If you get an awkward root, recheck whether the question gives the milk left or the water added.

If the vessel already holds a mixture, apply the formula to the starting quantity of the liquid you are tracking. The fraction kept per round is still (1 − x/V), with V the whole vessel.

Worked example 7: a 3 : 1 mixture

A 60-litre vessel holds milk and water in the ratio 3 : 1, so 45 litres of milk. 12 litres are removed and replaced with water twice. Milk left = 45 × (48/60)² = 45 × 0.64 = 28.8 litres.

If the replacement liquid is not water but the same as what you are tracking, or a mixture itself, the simple formula no longer applies; work round by round instead. For mixing two solutions once, use the alligation cross.

  • Subtracting x each time. Only the first draw is pure milk.
  • Using the formula for water. It gives the original liquid left; water is the rest.
  • Applying it to the whole vessel of a mixture. Start from the milk only.
  • Forgetting that the volume must stay V. If the replacement differs in amount, work round by round.
  • Taking the wrong root. Two operations need a square root, three a cube root.

Q1. A can holds 50 litres of milk. 5 litres are taken out and replaced with water, and this is done twice. How much milk is left? easy

✅ Answer: (d) 40.5 litres

💡 Explanation: 50 × (1 − 5/50)² = 50 × 0.81 = 40.5 litres. Subtracting 10 litres in total (40) ignores that the second draw is already part water.

Q2. A tank holds 100 litres of milk. 20 litres are removed and replaced with water three times. How much milk remains? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 51.2 litres

💡 Explanation: 100 × 0.8³ = 100 × 0.512 = 51.2 litres of milk. 48.8 litres is the water.

Q3. From 90 litres of milk, 30 litres are replaced with water twice. How much milk is left? easy

✅ Answer: (a) 40 litres

💡 Explanation: Each step keeps 60/90 = 2/3 of the milk: 90 × (2/3)² = 40 litres.

Q4. A vessel has 81 litres of pure acid. 27 litres are drawn off and replaced with water, four times. How much acid remains? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 16 litres

💡 Explanation: 81 × (2/3)⁴ = 81 × 16/81 = 16 litres.

Q5. A 40-litre can of milk has the same quantity removed and replaced with water twice. The final milk-to-water ratio is 16 : 9. How many litres were removed each time? hard

✅ Answer: (b) 8 litres

💡 Explanation: Milk is 16/25 of the can, so (1 − x/40)² = 16/25. Then 1 − x/40 = 4/5 and x = 8 litres.

Why can't I just subtract the milk removed each time?

After the first replacement the vessel holds a mixture. The second draw removes some water as well as milk, so less milk leaves each time. The amount left shrinks by the same fraction every round, which is why the formula uses a power.

Does the formula change if the liquid is wine or acid?

No. Any liquid that is drawn off and replaced with another works the same way. The formula tracks the original liquid; the other liquid is the total minus that.

What if the vessel starts as a mixture?

Apply the formula to the original quantity of the liquid you are tracking, not to the whole vessel. For 45 litres of milk in a 60-litre mixture, use 45 × (1 − x/60)ⁿ.
