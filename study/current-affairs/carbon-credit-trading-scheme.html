🌍 Carbon Credit Trading Scheme

Emission Reduction Credits • Power Exchange Platform • Climate Finance • Paris Agreement Compliance

High Yield GS-3 + GS-1 + Essay ⚡ Climate Action + Market Mechanisms 📅 Last updated: Oct 2026
  • What is the CCTS? → The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023 sets up the Indian Carbon Market (ICM). Industries that cut emissions faster than required earn tradable Carbon Credit Certificates (CCCs); those that fall short must buy them.
  • Legal basis: Notified by the Ministry of Power on 28 June 2023 under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001, as amended in 2022 (Section 14(w)), and amended on 19 December 2023 to add the offset mechanism.
  • Not cap-and-trade: India sets emission intensity targets (tonnes of CO₂e per tonne of product), not an absolute cap on total emissions. Industry can grow while getting cleaner per unit.
  • Two mechanisms: a Compliance Mechanism for notified energy-intensive industries, and a voluntary Offset Mechanism for others.
  • UPSC Angle: Market-based climate policy, India's NDCs, the shift from PAT to CCTS, and links with Article 6 and the EU CBAM.

📌 Institutional Framework

  • National Steering Committee for the Indian Carbon Market: Co-chaired by the Secretaries of the Ministry of Power and the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change; oversees the market.
  • Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE): The Administrator; proposes targets and recommends issuance of certificates.
  • Grid Controller of India (Grid-India): The Registry that holds and tracks certificates.
  • Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC): Regulates trading of certificates, which happens on power exchanges.
  • Accredited Carbon Verification Agencies: Check the emissions data reported by entities.

📌 Compliance Mechanism

  • Obligated Entities: Emission-intensive plants notified by the Government, each given a Greenhouse Gas Emission Intensity (GEI) target.
  • Beat the target: earn Carbon Credit Certificates (1 CCC = 1 tonne of CO₂ equivalent).
  • Miss the target: buy CCCs from others to cover the shortfall.
  • Sectors (7): aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, pulp and paper (targets notified October 2025, 282 entities); petroleum refineries, petrochemicals and textiles, plus secondary aluminium (notified 13 January 2026, 208 more entities). Total: about 490 obligated entities.
  • From PAT to CCTS: These sectors moved over from the Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) energy-efficiency scheme. Thermal power plants have not been moved to the CCTS.

📌 Offset Mechanism

  • Who: Non-obligated entities can voluntarily register projects that reduce, remove or avoid greenhouse gas emissions.
  • What they get: Carbon Credit Certificates, issued against approved methodologies.
  • Status: Offset methodologies, monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) rules and verifier accreditation procedures are in place.

📌 International Context

  • Paris Agreement Article 6: Lets countries transfer mitigation outcomes. In 2023 India finalised a list of activities it would consider for trading under Article 6.2, aimed at bringing in emerging technologies and international finance.
  • EU CBAM: The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism charges imports of steel, aluminium, cement and some other goods for their embedded carbon. A credible domestic carbon price is one way India's exporters may be able to respond.
  • Kyoto legacy: India was a large host of Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) projects under the Kyoto Protocol, which built early MRV experience.

✅ Timeline

  • December 2022: Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act empowers the Centre to specify a carbon credit trading scheme.
  • 28 June 2023: CCTS notified; 19 December 2023: amended to add the offset mechanism.
  • October 2025: First GEI targets: aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, pulp and paper (282 entities).
  • 13 January 2026: Targets for petroleum refineries, petrochemicals, textiles and secondary aluminium (208 entities; about 490 in total).
  • March 2026: Indian Carbon Market Portal launched by the Union Power Minister.
💡 Prelims Trap: India's scheme is intensity-based, not an absolute cap-and-trade like the EU ETS. It was notified under the Energy Conservation Act (Ministry of Power), not the Environment Protection Act. Thermal power is not in the compliance mechanism.

🎯 Carbon Credit Trading: Multi-Dimensional Analysis

🔹 Economic Rationale

  • Least-cost abatement: Plants that can cut emissions cheaply do more and sell certificates; plants with high abatement costs buy instead. The same total reduction happens at lower cost.
  • Intensity targets suit a growing economy: Output can rise while emissions per tonne of product fall, in line with India's NDC goal of reducing the emissions intensity of GDP.
  • Built on PAT: A decade of PAT energy-efficiency trading gave BEE and industry experience with targets, verification and tradable certificates.

🔹 Environmental Integrity

  • Target stringency: If GEI targets are too lenient, certificates flood the market and prices collapse, which is what happened with CDM credits after 2012.
  • MRV: Accredited verifiers and a single registry (Grid-India) are meant to stop double counting.
  • Offsets: Projects must show real, additional reductions; weak methodologies would undermine trust.

🔹 Trade and Competitiveness

  • CBAM pressure: Steel, aluminium and cement exporters to the EU face carbon charges, so a domestic carbon price matters.
  • Article 6: High-quality credits could earn international finance, but India must keep enough low-cost reductions for its own NDC.

🔹 Critical Challenges & Way Forward

  • Coverage: Thermal power, the largest emitting sector, is outside the compliance mechanism.
  • Market depth: With about 490 entities, trading may be thin at first; prices could be volatile.
  • Data and capacity: Smaller obligated plants need emissions inventories and enough accredited verifiers.
  • Way forward: Tighten targets over time, add sectors such as iron and steel, keep the registry transparent, and seek recognition of Indian credits abroad.

🔹 Mains Answer Framework

  1. Contextualize: NDC targets, net zero by 2070, and the EU CBAM.
  2. Explain the design: Intensity targets, the compliance and offset mechanisms, and the BEE–Grid-India–CERC roles.
  3. Critically evaluate: Target stringency, coverage, market depth and MRV capacity.
  4. Way forward: Gradual tightening, wider coverage and international linkage.

📌 Case 1: From PAT to CCTS

  • PAT: Set energy-efficiency targets (energy per unit of output) for designated consumers, with tradable Energy Saving Certificates.
  • CCTS: Sets GHG emission intensity targets for the same kinds of industries, with Carbon Credit Certificates.
  • Change: The unit of the target moves from energy to carbon, so fuel switching and renewables now count, not just efficiency.
  • UPSC Link: Policy evolution + Market-based instruments + Institutional continuity (BEE).

📌 Case 2: Intensity Target vs Absolute Cap

  • EU ETS: Sets a declining cap on total emissions from covered sectors; allowances are auctioned or allocated.
  • India's CCTS: Sets emissions per unit of product. A cement plant that doubles output can still meet its target if each tonne of cement is cleaner.
  • Trade-off: Intensity targets protect growth but do not guarantee that total emissions fall.
  • UPSC Link: Common but Differentiated Responsibilities + Development needs + Climate ambition.

Q1. With reference to India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023, consider the following statements:
1. It was notified under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001.
2. It sets an absolute cap on total emissions from each covered sector.
3. Thermal power plants are covered under its compliance mechanism.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

✅ Answer: (a) 1 only

💡 Explanation: The scheme sets emission intensity targets, not absolute caps, and thermal power plants have not been moved from PAT to the CCTS compliance mechanism.

Q2. Under the Indian Carbon Market, one Carbon Credit Certificate represents:

✅ Answer: (b) One tonne of CO₂ equivalent

💡 Explanation: One CCC equals one tonne of CO₂ equivalent. Option (d) describes the Energy Saving Certificate under the older PAT scheme.

Q3. Consider the following pairs about the Indian Carbon Market:
Institution | Role
1. Bureau of Energy Efficiency | Administrator
2. Grid Controller of India | Registry
3. Securities and Exchange Board of India | Regulator of certificate trading

How many pairs are correctly matched?

✅ Answer: (b) Only two

💡 Explanation: Trading of carbon credit certificates is regulated by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC), because it happens on power exchanges, not by SEBI.

Q4. Which international provision enables the transfer of mitigation outcomes between countries?

✅ Answer: (a) Paris Agreement Article 6

💡 Explanation: Article 6 sets up cooperative approaches, including internationally transferred mitigation outcomes (ITMOs, Article 6.2) and a UN-supervised mechanism (Article 6.4). Annex B of the Kyoto Protocol lists developed countries' emission targets.

Q5. Which of the following sectors was among the first four for which Greenhouse Gas Emission Intensity targets were notified in October 2025?

✅ Answer: (b) Cement

💡 Explanation: The October 2025 targets covered aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, and pulp and paper. Refineries and textiles followed in January 2026; thermal power is not covered.

🔁 Carbon Credit Trading in 10 Seconds

  • Notified: 28 June 2023 (offset mechanism added 19 December 2023)
  • Legal Base: Energy Conservation Act, 2001, as amended in 2022 (Ministry of Power)
  • Design: Emission intensity targets, not an absolute cap
  • Unit: 1 Carbon Credit Certificate = 1 tonne CO₂e
  • Institutions: Steering Committee (Power + MoEFCC Secretaries); BEE = Administrator; Grid-India = Registry; CERC = trading regulator
  • Coverage: ~490 obligated entities in 7 sectors; thermal power excluded
  • Offsets: Voluntary projects by non-obligated entities

🧠 Mnemonic: "BGC" for the institutions

B → BEE administers

G → Grid-India keeps the registry

C → CERC regulates trading

  • ✘ Trading is regulated by CERC, not SEBI
  • ✘ Thermal power plants are not in the compliance mechanism
  • ✘ CCC (carbon, tCO₂e) ≠ ESCert (PAT, energy saved)
