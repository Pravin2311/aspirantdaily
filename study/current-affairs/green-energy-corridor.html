🔌 Green Energy Corridor

Renewable Energy Integration • Intra-State & Inter-State Transmission • Phase I, Phase II & Ladakh

High Yield GS-3 + Environment ⚡ Energy Security + Climate 📅 Last updated: Oct 2026
  • What is the Green Energy Corridor (GEC)? → Transmission lines and substations built to carry power from renewable energy (RE) sites to the grid and load centres. It builds transmission, not generation.
  • Two parts: an intra-state part (InSTS), run by MNRE and built by the states, and an inter-state part (ISTS), built by POWERGRID.
  • GEC Phase I (intra-state): 8 states, about 24 GW of RE, 9,700 ckm of lines and 22,600 MVA of substations; estimated cost ₹10,141.68 crore, with Central Financial Assistance (CFA) of ₹4,056.67 crore.
  • GEC Phase II (intra-state): approved by the CCEA on 6 January 2022; 7 states, about 20 GW of RE, about 10,750 ckm and 27,500 MVA; cost ₹12,031.33 crore, with CFA at 33% (₹3,970.34 crore); to be built over FY 2021-22 to 2025-26.
  • GEC Phase II – ISTS for Ladakh: approved on 18 October 2023 to evacuate 13 GW of RE from Pang, Ladakh; cost ₹20,773.70 crore, with CFA at 40%; target FY 2029-30.
  • UPSC Angle: RE integration, energy transition, Centre-State roles in the power sector, and how a transmission scheme is funded.

📌 GEC Phase I (Intra-State)

  • States (8): Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu.
  • Scope: 9,700 ckm of transmission lines and 22,600 MVA of substations to evacuate about 24 GW of RE.
  • Cost: ₹10,141.68 crore; CFA ₹4,056.67 crore.
  • Funding pattern: 40% grant from the Centre (originally from the National Clean Energy Fund), 40% soft loan (KfW, Germany) and 20% state equity.

📌 GEC Phase II (Intra-State)

  • Approval: Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA), 6 January 2022.
  • States (7): Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh.
  • Scope: about 10,750 ckm of lines and 27,500 MVA of substations (a 2023 release gives 10,753 ckm and 27,546 MVA), for about 20 GW of RE.
  • Cost: ₹12,031.33 crore, with CFA at 33% (₹3,970.34 crore). The CFA offsets intra-state transmission charges, which keeps power costs down for consumers.
  • Period: FY 2021-22 to 2025-26. The approval linked it to the target of 450 GW of installed RE capacity by 2030.

📌 GEC Phase II – Inter-State Transmission for Ladakh

  • Background: In his 15 August 2020 speech, the Prime Minister announced a 7.5 GW solar park in Ladakh. MNRE then planned 13 GW of RE with 12 GWh of battery storage (BESS) at Pang.
  • Scope: 713 km of lines, including 480 km of HVDC line, plus a 5 GW HVDC terminal at each end, at Pang (Ladakh) and Kaithal (Haryana). The line passes through Himachal Pradesh and Punjab.
  • Technology: Voltage Source Converter (VSC)-based HVDC, plus extra high voltage AC lines.
  • Implementing agency: POWERGRID, chosen because of the difficult terrain, harsh climate and defence sensitivities.
  • Cost and timeline: ₹20,773.70 crore; CFA at 40% (₹8,309.48 crore); target FY 2029-30.
  • Local links: to the existing Ladakh grid, and to the Leh-Alusteng-Srinagar line to supply Jammu & Kashmir.

📌 Who Does What

  • MNRE: runs the intra-state GEC scheme and releases the CFA.
  • States: their transmission utilities build and own the intra-state lines.
  • POWERGRID: builds the inter-state components, including the Ladakh project.

✅ Progress (Rajya Sabha reply, 9 March 2026)

  • GEC-I and GEC-II together cover 10 states: Rajasthan, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Kerala, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu.
  • The two phases are meant to evacuate 44 GW of RE; 26 GW had been integrated.
  • An intra-state GEC-III scheme for large-scale RE integration is under consideration.
  • RE capacity commissioned was about 263 GW as of January 2026.
  • Inter-regional transmission capacity is planned to rise from 120 GW (January 2026) to 168 GW by 2032.

✅ Phase-wise State Lists

  • Only in GEC-I: Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra.
  • Only in GEC-II: Kerala, Uttar Pradesh.
  • In both: Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu.
💡 Prelims Trap: GEC is a transmission scheme. It gives no capital subsidy to solar or wind developers. The CFA share is 40% in GEC-I and 33% in InSTS GEC-II; the 40% figure for GEC-II applies only to the Ladakh inter-state project.

🎯 Green Energy Corridor: Multi-Dimensional Analysis

🔹 Why Transmission Is the Bottleneck

  • Location mismatch: The best solar and wind sites (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Ladakh, the southern wind states) are far from the big load centres.
  • Timing mismatch: A solar park can be built in a year or two, but transmission lines take longer, mainly because of right-of-way and forest clearances. Without lines, generation is stranded.
  • Variability: Solar and wind output changes with weather and time of day, so the grid needs stronger links, storage and flexible resources.

🔹 How the Government Is Responding (PIB, March 2026)

  • Planning: GEC and the wider ISTS expansion are planned to integrate over 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 and over 600 GW by 2032.
  • Network growth: Under the National Electricity Plan (Volume-II, Transmission), the 220 kV-and-above network is projected to reach 6.48 lakh ckm, with 2,345 GVA of transformation capacity, by 2031-32.
  • Storage: Transmission for 35.6 GW of pumped storage projects has been planned up to 2031-32; about 47 GW of BESS is considered for integration by 2031-32; two Viability Gap Funding schemes support about 43.8 GWh of BESS.
  • Regulation: CERC's 2025 amendment to the GNA regulations introduced solar-hour and non-solar-hour connectivity, so the same line can serve solar by day and other sources at other times.

🔹 Federal Dimension

  • Electricity is in the Concurrent List (Entry 38, List III). The Centre funds part of the intra-state work through CFA, but states build and own those lines.
  • Why the CFA matters: Without it, states hosting RE parks would pass higher intra-state transmission charges on to their consumers, though much of the power may be used elsewhere.
  • Inter-state lines such as the Ladakh link fall under the Centre, which is why POWERGRID implements them.

🔹 Challenges & Way Forward

  • Right-of-way and clearances: Land compensation disputes and forest and wildlife clearances delay lines, as the extended GEC-I deadlines show.
  • Utilisation: Of the 44 GW the two intra-state phases were meant to evacuate, 26 GW was integrated by March 2026. Lines are only useful once the RE capacity they serve is built.
  • DISCOM finances: Weak distribution companies delay payments and power purchase agreements, which slows RE capacity addition.
  • Ecology: Overhead lines in desert and Himalayan habitats raise wildlife concerns. In Rajasthan and Gujarat, overhead lines in Great Indian Bustard habitat have been the subject of Supreme Court proceedings.

🔹 Mains Answer Framework

  1. Contextualize: The 500 GW non-fossil target for 2030 and the net-zero goal for 2070.
  2. Explain the scheme: GEC-I, InSTS GEC-II and the Ladakh ISTS project, with the funding pattern.
  3. Critically evaluate: Right-of-way delays, under-used capacity, DISCOM finances and ecological trade-offs.
  4. Way forward: Plan transmission ahead of generation, add storage, speed up clearances and take up GEC-III.

📌 Case 1: Ladakh 13 GW Project – HVDC in the Himalaya

  • Context: Ladakh has strong solar potential but is far from the main load centres and has a small local grid.
  • Intervention: A 713 km link, including 480 km of HVDC line, from Pang to Kaithal in Haryana, with 5 GW HVDC terminals at both ends.
  • Why HVDC: Long distances, difficult terrain and a large bulk transfer.
  • UPSC Link: Energy transition + Border-area development + Strategic infrastructure + Technology choice.

📌 Case 2: Funding Design – Why CFA Fell from 40% to 33%

  • GEC-I: 40% central grant, 40% soft loan from KfW and 20% state equity.
  • InSTS GEC-II: CFA cut to 33% of project cost; the rest is for the states to raise.
  • Ladakh ISTS: CFA back at 40%, reflecting the project's difficulty and strategic location.
  • UPSC Link: Cooperative federalism + Centre-State burden-sharing + External development finance.

Q1. With reference to the Green Energy Corridor (GEC) in India, consider the following statements:
1. The intra-state component is built by the states, while POWERGRID builds the inter-state component.
2. The Intra-State Transmission System GEC Phase-II covers seven states, including Kerala and Uttar Pradesh.
3. Central Financial Assistance under the Intra-State Transmission System GEC Phase-II is 40% of the project cost.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

✅ Answer: (a) 1 and 2 only

💡 Explanation: Statement 3 is incorrect: CFA under InSTS GEC-II is 33% (₹3,970.34 crore). The 40% rate applies to GEC-I and to the Ladakh inter-state project.

Q2. The Intra-State Transmission System – Green Energy Corridor Phase-II, approved in January 2022, has an estimated cost of about:

✅ Answer: (b) ₹12,031 crore

💡 Explanation: InSTS GEC-II costs ₹12,031.33 crore. ₹10,141.68 crore is the GEC-I intra-state cost, and ₹20,773.70 crore is the Ladakh inter-state project.

Q3. Consider the following pairs about the GEC Phase-II inter-state project for 13 GW of RE in Ladakh:
1. Implementing agency | POWERGRID
2. Point of integration with the National Grid | Kaithal, Haryana
3. Central Financial Assistance | 33% of project cost

How many pairs are correctly matched?

✅ Answer: (b) Only two

💡 Explanation: Pairs 1 and 2 are correct. The Ladakh project's CFA is 40% (₹8,309.48 crore of ₹20,773.70 crore); 33% is the intra-state GEC-II rate.

Q4. Which one of the following states is covered by the Intra-State GEC Phase-II but was NOT part of GEC Phase-I?

✅ Answer: (b) Kerala

💡 Explanation: Kerala and Uttar Pradesh joined in Phase-II. Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh were only in Phase-I.

Q5. According to the Government's March 2026 reply in Parliament, how much renewable energy capacity had been integrated under the intra-state GEC-I and GEC-II, out of the 44 GW planned?

✅ Answer: (b) About 26 GW

💡 Explanation: The Ministry of Power's Rajya Sabha reply of 9 March 2026 says the two phases are for evacuating 44 GW of RE, of which 26 GW is integrated.

🔁 Green Energy Corridor in 10 Seconds

  • GEC-I (intra-state): 8 states; ~24 GW; 9,700 ckm; ₹10,141.68 Cr; funded 40% central grant + 40% KfW loan + 20% state equity
  • InSTS GEC-II: CCEA 6 Jan 2022; 7 states; ~20 GW; ~10,750 ckm; ₹12,031.33 Cr; CFA 33%; FY22–FY26
  • GEC-II ISTS Ladakh: CCEA 18 Oct 2023; 13 GW; 713 km (480 km HVDC) Pang–Kaithal; ₹20,773.70 Cr; CFA 40%; POWERGRID; by FY 2029-30
  • Progress (Mar 2026): 26 GW of the 44 GW integrated; GEC-III under consideration
  • Roles: MNRE runs the intra-state scheme; states build intra-state lines; POWERGRID builds inter-state lines
  • Challenge: Right-of-way, clearances, DISCOM finances, wildlife impacts

🧠 Mnemonic: "GEC = 1-2-L"

1 → Phase I: 8 states, 24 GW, 40% grant (+ KfW loan)

2 → Phase II intra-state: 7 states, 20 GW, 33% CFA

L → Ladakh inter-state: 13 GW, HVDC to Kaithal, 40% CFA, POWERGRID

📌 Prelims Traps to Avoid

  • ✘ GEC is transmission-focused, not a generation subsidy (unlike the Solar Park scheme or PM Surya Ghar)
  • ✘ Intra-state lines are built by the states, not POWERGRID; POWERGRID builds the inter-state parts
  • ✘ CFA is 33% for InSTS GEC-II, not 40% and not 100%
  • ✘ InSTS GEC-II was approved in January 2022; the October 2023 approval is the separate Ladakh ISTS project
  • ✘ Kerala and Uttar Pradesh are in Phase-II only

🎯 Mains One-Liners

  • "Renewable capacity is only as useful as the transmission that carries it; GEC builds that link"
  • "Central Financial Assistance keeps intra-state transmission charges from falling on consumers in RE-host states"
  • "The Ladakh HVDC link shows transmission as strategic infrastructure, not just a utility asset"
  • "Way Forward: plan transmission ahead of generation, pair it with storage, and speed up right-of-way clearances"
