Types of Computer Network: PAN, LAN, MAN and WAN

What a network is • Nodes and packets • PAN • LAN and Ethernet • MAN • WAN and the Internet • Comparison table

SSC Banking Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

A computer network is two or more computers or computing devices connected so that they can share data and resources. A printer that several computers use, a shared folder on an office server, and a phone hotspot that your laptop joins are all examples. A small network might link a few machines in one room; the largest links billions of devices worldwide.

Each device on a network that can create, receive, store or send data is a node, sometimes called a host. Nodes include servers, desktops, laptops and phones, as well as networking devices such as switches, routers and modems. Data does not travel as one large block: it is split into small chunks called packets, which are carried across the network and reassembled at the far end. Links can be wired, using cables, or wireless, using radio waves through the air.

Networking began with ARPANET, a 1960s project of the US Department of Defence to connect research institutions. Its first message passed between the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) and the Stanford Research Institute (SRI). Independent networks joined over time, and the network of networks that grew from them is today's Internet.

Networks are grouped by the geographical area they cover and their data transfer rate. From smallest to largest, the four types are PAN, LAN, MAN and WAN. A useful rule: as the area grows, the number of connected devices grows, and the speed of the typical link falls.

A PAN links a few personal devices such as a phone, a laptop, a smartwatch or a printer, all within roughly 10 metres. It can be wired or wireless. A phone connected to a laptop by a USB cable forms a wired PAN. Two phones talking over Bluetooth form a wireless PAN (WPAN). A Bluetooth group of this kind is called a piconet.

A LAN connects devices in a limited area: a room, a floor, an office building, a laboratory, or a school or university campus. It can be extended up to about 1 km. Devices connect by Ethernet cables, fibre optics or Wi-Fi.

LANs give high data transfer rates over short distances, usually from 10 Mbps (classic Ethernet) to 1000 Mbps (Gigabit Ethernet). Mbps means megabits per second. Ethernet is the set of rules that decides how computers and other devices connect through cables in a LAN.

A LAN is comparatively secure, because only authorised users on the network can reach the shared computers and resources. Users can print on a shared printer and upload or download files and software to and from a local server.

A MAN is an extended form of LAN covering a city or town, often by joining several LANs. It can stretch to about 30 to 40 km. Speeds are still in Mbps but are considerably lower than on a LAN. The standard examples are a cable TV network and cable-based broadband Internet services in a city.

A WAN connects computers, LANs and MANs spread across different parts of a country, or across countries and continents. Large companies, banks, universities and government departments use WANs to connect offices in different locations. A WAN can be formed by joining one LAN to other LANs over wired or wireless media.

The Internet is the largest WAN. It connects billions of computers and smartphones and millions of LANs across the continents.

The ranges are guide figures used in textbooks, not fixed limits. A question is really asking which scale fits the situation it describes.

Scenario questions describe a situation and ask which network it is. Decide by the area covered, then check the example against the table.

  • Wireless earbuds paired with a phone: personal devices a few metres apart, linked by Bluetooth, so a WPAN.
  • Thirty computers and a printer in a school lab: one room or building, so a LAN.
  • A cable broadband operator serving one city: a town-sized area, so a MAN.
  • A railway reservation system linking stations across India: sites in many states, so a WAN.

If a question mixes scales, such as branch LANs joined into a national network, name the type for the part the question asks about.

  • ✘ Ordering the types by speed instead of area. The order by area is PAN < LAN < MAN < WAN.
  • ✘ Calling Wi-Fi a WAN because it reaches the Internet. Wi-Fi is a wireless LAN.
  • ✘ Picking LAN for offices in different cities. Distance decides: that needs a WAN.
  • ✘ Treating the Internet and the World Wide Web as the same. The Internet is the network; the web is the pages reached through it.

Q1. Which is the largest WAN? easy

✅ Answer: (c) The Internet

💡 Explanation: The Internet connects billions of devices and millions of LANs across every continent, so it is the largest Wide Area Network.

Q2. Two smartphones exchanging files over Bluetooth form a: easy

✅ Answer: (a) WPAN

💡 Explanation: Personal devices within about 10 metres form a personal area network. When the link is wireless, as with Bluetooth, it is called a wireless PAN or WPAN.

Q3. Up to what distance does the textbook say a LAN usually extends? easy

✅ Answer: (b) About 1 km

💡 Explanation: A LAN covers a room, floor, building or campus and can be extended up to about 1 km. 10 metres is a PAN and 30 to 40 km is a MAN.

Q4. A cable TV network across a city is an example of a: easy

✅ Answer: (d) MAN

💡 Explanation: A Metropolitan Area Network covers a city or town. Cable TV and cable broadband networks are the standard examples.

Q5. Two universities in different states want to share data over their own network. Which type do they need? medium

✅ Answer: (a) WAN

💡 Explanation: The two sites are far apart, in different states, so the link must be a Wide Area Network. A LAN or MAN cannot span that distance.

Q6. Gigabit Ethernet on a LAN supports speeds of about: medium

✅ Answer: (c) 1000 Mbps

💡 Explanation: LAN speeds usually range from 10 Mbps, called Ethernet, to 1000 Mbps, called Gigabit Ethernet. 1000 Mbps is 1 Gbps.

Is Wi-Fi a LAN or a WAN?

Wi-Fi is a wireless LAN. Its standard is IEEE 802.11, and a home or office Wi-Fi network covers a limited area like any LAN. When the router connects that LAN to the Internet, the traffic travels onward over WANs.

Is a bank's network a LAN, MAN or WAN?

Each branch usually has its own LAN, and the branches across the country are joined into a WAN. That is how you can reach your account from anywhere. The answer depends on which part of the network the question describes.
