Types of Soils in India: Alluvial, Black, Red and Yellow, Laterite, Arid and Forest Soils Compared

Alluvial: most widespread • Black (regur): cotton, holds moisture • Red: iron diffusion • Laterite: leaching, tea, coffee, cashew • Arid: kankar • Forest: hills

UPSC SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

NCERT classifies India's soils by the factors that formed them and by colour, thickness, texture, age and chemical properties. Soil forms very slowly: it takes millions of years to build up a few centimetres. Relief, parent rock, climate, vegetation and time all shape it.

  • Alluvial: the most widespread and important soil in India. It is a mix of sand, silt and clay. Near the break of slope it is coarse, as in the piedmont plains of the Duars, Chos and Terai. Because it is so fertile, alluvial regions are intensively cultivated and densely populated. In drier areas it is more alkaline.
  • Bangar vs Khadar: by age, Bangar is old alluvium with more kankar nodules. Khadar is new alluvium with finer particles, and it is more fertile.
  • Black: made of very fine clay, so it holds moisture well. It cracks deeply in hot weather, which lets air into the soil. It is sticky when wet, so farmers till it right after the first shower or in the pre-monsoon period.
  • Red and yellow: the colour comes from iron spread through crystalline and metamorphic rocks. The same soil looks yellow when hydrated.
  • Laterite: the name comes from the Latin later, meaning brick. It is humus-rich under forests but humus-poor under sparse vegetation, and it is prone to erosion.
  • Arid: calcium content increases with depth, so the lower layers hold kankar, which restricts water infiltration. In some areas the salt content is so high that common salt is obtained by evaporating the water.
  • Forest: loamy and silty on valley sides and coarse-grained on upper slopes.

Soil erosion is the removal of the soil cover and its washing down. Soil formation and erosion normally balance each other. Human activities such as deforestation, over-grazing, construction and mining can upset that balance, and wind, glaciers and water also cause erosion.

TypeWhat happens
Gully erosionRunning water cuts deep channels in clayey soil. The land becomes "bad land"; in the Chambal basin these are called ravines.
Sheet erosionWater flows as a sheet over a large slope and washes away the topsoil.
Wind erosionWind blows loose soil off flat or sloping land.

Conservation methods:

  • Contour ploughing: ploughing along contour lines slows water running down slopes. Ploughing up and down the slope does the opposite.
  • Terrace cultivation: steps cut into slopes. The western and central Himalayas have well-developed terrace farming.
  • Strip cropping: strips of grass between crops break the force of the wind.
  • Shelter belts: rows of trees. They have helped stabilise sand dunes and the desert in western India.

Q1. Regur is another name for: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Black soil

💡 Explanation: NCERT says black soils are also known as regur soils and as black cotton soil.

Q2. Which soil is the result of intense leaching due to heavy rain? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Laterite soil

💡 Explanation: Laterite develops in tropical climates with alternate wet and dry seasons, where heavy rain leaches nutrients out.

Q3. Compared with Bangar, Khadar soil is: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Newer, finer and more fertile

💡 Explanation: Khadar is new alluvium with more fine particles and more fertility. Bangar is old alluvium with more kankar nodules.

Q4. Black soil is generally poor in: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Phosphoric content

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime as rich nutrients in black soil, and says it is poor in phosphoric content.

Q5. In the Chambal basin, land cut up by gullies is called: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Ravines

💡 Explanation: Gully erosion makes bad land unfit for cultivation. NCERT says such land in the Chambal basin is called ravines.

How many types of soil are there in India?

NCERT's Class 10 geography describes six: alluvial, black, red and yellow, laterite, arid and forest soils. They differ by parent rock, climate, colour, texture and nutrients.

Which soil is best for cotton?

Black soil, also called regur or black cotton soil. Its fine clay holds moisture well and it is rich in lime, potash and magnesium, which suits cotton on the Deccan plateau.

Why is laterite soil poor in nutrients?

Heavy rain in a climate of alternate wet and dry seasons leaches nutrients out of it, leaving an acidic soil. With conservation and manuring it still supports tea, coffee and cashew.
