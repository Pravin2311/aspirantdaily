Pressure Belts and Planetary Winds: Equatorial Low to Polar High, Coriolis Force, the Three Cells, ENSO and Local Winds

Equatorial low (ITCZ) • Subtropical highs at 30° • Subpolar lows at 60° • Polar highs • Easterlies, westerlies, polar easterlies • Coriolis: right in NH, left in SH, zero at equator

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of a column of air over a unit area, from sea level to the top of the atmosphere. It is measured in millibars (mb) with a mercury or aneroid barometer. The average at sea level is 1,013.2 mb.

  • With height: in the lower atmosphere, pressure falls by about 1 mb for every 10 m of height. So climbing 1,000 m lowers pressure by roughly 100 mb: a station reading 1,000 mb at sea level would read about 900 mb at 1 km.
  • Why no strong upward winds? The vertical pressure gradient is much larger than the horizontal one, but gravity pulling the other way almost balances it.
  • Isobars are lines joining places of equal pressure, drawn after reducing readings to sea level. Closely spaced isobars mean a steep pressure gradient and strong wind.

That gives 7 belts in all: one equatorial low plus three belts in each hemisphere. The belts are not fixed. They swing with the sun's apparent movement, moving south in the northern hemisphere's winter and north in its summer. This shift is what drives seasonal winds such as the monsoon.

Wind blows from high pressure to low pressure. Near the ground it is shaped by three forces, with gravity acting downward as well.

  1. Pressure gradient force: the rate of change of pressure with distance. It is stronger where isobars are close together.
  2. Frictional force: slows the wind. It is greatest at the surface, reaches up to about 1–3 km, and is minimal over the sea.
  3. Coriolis force: caused by the earth's rotation and named after the French physicist who described it in 1844. It deflects winds to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. It is proportional to latitude: maximum at the poles, zero at the equator. Faster winds are deflected more.

Geostrophic wind: 2–3 km up there is no friction. If the isobars are straight, the pressure gradient force and the Coriolis force balance, and the wind blows parallel to the isobars.

Why no cyclones at the equator: with no Coriolis force, wind blows straight across the isobars into a low, which fills up instead of deepening.

The overall pattern of planetary winds is the general circulation of the atmosphere. NCERT says it depends on uneven heating by latitude, the pressure belts, their seasonal migration, the spread of land and sea, and the earth's rotation.

CellHow it worksSurface wind
Hadley cell (tropics)Air rises at the ITCZ to about 14 km, flows poleward and sinks near 30°, forming the subtropical highEasterlies, the trade winds, blowing back towards the equator
Ferrel cell (middle latitudes)Cold air from the poles sinks; warm air from the subtropical high risesWesterlies
Polar cellCold, dense air subsides at the polesPolar easterlies, blowing towards middle latitudes

Moving heat from low to high latitudes keeps this circulation going. It also drives the large, slow ocean currents, and the oceans in turn feed energy and water vapour back into the air.

Warm water from the central Pacific sometimes drifts towards South America and replaces the cool Peruvian current. This warm water off Peru is El Nino. It is linked to a see-saw of pressure between the central Pacific and Australia called the southern oscillation. Together they make up ENSO.

In strong ENSO years, the arid west coast of South America gets heavy rain, Australia and sometimes India suffer drought, and China sees floods. ENSO is monitored closely for long-range forecasting.

  • Seasonal winds: as heating and the pressure belts shift through the year, wind patterns change. The monsoons of southeast Asia are the most pronounced example.
  • Sea breeze and land breeze: by day the land heats faster, so low pressure forms over it and wind blows from sea to land. At night the land cools faster and the wind reverses, blowing from land to sea.
  • Valley breeze and mountain wind: by day heated slopes draw air up from the valley. At night cooled, dense air slides down into the valley.
  • Katabatic wind: cold air draining from high plateaus and ice fields into valleys.
  • Warm leeward wind: moist air drops its rain crossing a range, then warms as it descends the leeward side. This dry, warm air can melt snow quickly.
  • Subtropical high vs subpolar low: highs at 30°, lows at 60°.
  • Easterlies vs westerlies: easterlies (trade winds) blow in the tropics, westerlies in the middle latitudes.
  • Hadley vs Ferrel cell: the Hadley cell is tropical, the Ferrel cell mid-latitude.
  • Coriolis at equator vs poles: zero at the equator, maximum at the poles.
  • Sea breeze vs land breeze: the sea breeze is a daytime wind, the land breeze a night wind.
  • Cyclone direction: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern. Anticyclones are the reverse.

Q1. The subtropical high-pressure belts lie at about: easy

✅ Answer: (c) 30° N and S

💡 Explanation: NCERT places the subtropical highs along 30° N and 30° S, where air from the Hadley cell sinks.

Q2. The Coriolis force is: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Maximum at the poles and zero at the equator

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the Coriolis force is directly proportional to latitude, maximum at the poles and absent at the equator.

Q3. Surface winds blowing from the subtropical highs towards the subpolar lows are the: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Westerlies

💡 Explanation: In the middle latitudes the surface winds of the Ferrel cell are the westerlies.

Q4. A wind blowing parallel to straight isobars with no friction is called: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Geostrophic wind

💡 Explanation: When the pressure gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force, the wind blows parallel to the isobars as the geostrophic wind.

Q5. Why do tropical cyclones not form at the equator? medium

✅ Answer: (c) The Coriolis force is zero there

💡 Explanation: With no Coriolis force at the equator, wind blows straight into the low, which fills up instead of intensifying.

How many pressure belts of the earth are there?

Seven: one equatorial low, and in each hemisphere a subtropical high near 30°, a subpolar low near 60° and a polar high. They shift north and south with the apparent movement of the sun.

What are trade winds in NCERT's terms?

NCERT calls them the easterlies: surface winds blowing from the subtropical highs towards the equator, where they converge in the ITCZ. Exam books usually call them the trade winds.

Why does the wind blow around a low instead of straight into it?

The Coriolis force acts at right angles to the pressure gradient force, so the wind is deflected and circles the low: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern.
