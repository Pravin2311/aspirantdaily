Plate Tectonics: From Wegener's Continental Drift to Sea-Floor Spreading, Plate Boundaries and India's Journey

Wegener: Pangaea and Panthalassa • Holmes 1930s: convection currents • Hess 1961: sea-floor spreading • 1967: plate tectonics • 7 major plates • Divergent, convergent, transform

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

The coastlines on either side of the Atlantic look as if they once fitted together. Abraham Ortelius, a Dutch map maker, suggested this as early as 1596. The full argument came from Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist, as the continental drift theory.

  • All continents once formed one supercontinent, Pangaea ("all earth"), surrounded by a mega-ocean, Panthalassa ("all water").
  • About 200 million years ago, Pangaea split into Laurasia (north) and Gondwanaland (south). These later broke into today's continents.
  • Wegener said the drift was driven by the pole-fleeing force, linked to the earth's rotation and equatorial bulge, and the tidal force of the moon and sun. Most scholars found these forces totally inadequate, which is why the theory was set aside.

In the 1930s, Arthur Holmes suggested convection currents in the mantle, driven by heat from radioactive elements. This offered the missing force. After the Second World War, mapping of the ocean floor showed that it is not a flat plain. It has submerged mountain ranges, deep trenches near continental margins, and very active volcanoes along the mid-oceanic ridges.

The ocean floor has three divisions: continental margins (shelf, slope, rise and deep trenches), deep-sea basins (abyssal plains) and mid-ocean ridges, the longest mountain chain on earth. Four findings changed the picture:

  1. Volcanic eruptions are common along the mid-oceanic ridges and bring up huge amounts of lava.
  2. Rocks equidistant on either side of a ridge crest match in age, chemistry and magnetism. Those nearest the crest are youngest and have normal polarity, and age increases away from the crest.
  3. Ocean crust is nowhere more than 200 million years old, while some continental rocks are 3,200 million years old.
  4. Ocean-floor sediments are unexpectedly thin and nowhere older than 200 million years.

From these, Hess (1961) proposed sea-floor spreading. Constant eruptions at ridge crests rupture the ocean crust, and new lava wedges in and pushes the crust to either side. Because one ocean spreading does not shrink another, Hess argued that old floor sinks and is consumed at the oceanic trenches.

Earthquakes and volcanoes follow the same lines. They form a belt along the mid-oceanic ridges, where earthquake foci are shallow, and belts along the Alpine-Himalayan system and the Pacific rim, where they are deep-seated. The Pacific rim is called the "rim of fire" for its active volcanoes.

In 1967, McKenzie and Parker, and separately Morgan, brought these ideas together as plate tectonics. A tectonic (lithospheric) plate is a huge, irregular slab of solid rock that moves horizontally over the asthenosphere as a rigid unit. The lithosphere is the crust plus the top of the mantle: 5-100 km thick under the oceans and about 200 km under the continents.

The lithosphere is divided into seven major and some minor plates, bounded by young fold mountains, trenches and faults:

The key change from Wegener: it is the plate that moves, not the continent. All plates have moved in the past and will keep moving. Pangaea itself formed when continental masses on different plates converged. The driving force is the slow movement of hot, softened mantle below the plates, in convection cells fed by radioactive decay and residual heat.

Magnetic strips parallel to the ridges show how fast plates move. The Arctic Ridge is slowest, under 2.5 cm a year. The East Pacific Rise near Easter Island is fastest, over 15 cm a year.

The Indian plate includes Peninsular India and the Australian continental portion. Its northern boundary is the continent-continent convergence along the Himalayas, and its boundary with the Antarctic plate is a divergent oceanic ridge.

  • India was a large island off the Australian coast. The Tethys Sea separated it from Asia.
  • It began moving north about 200 million years ago, when Pangaea broke up. About 140 million years ago it lay as far south as 50°S.
  • About 60 million years ago, lava outpourings formed the Deccan Traps, while India was still near the equator.
  • It collided with Asia 40-50 million years ago, causing rapid uplift of the Himalayas. Scientists believe the process continues and the Himalayas are still rising.

Q1. Who proposed the sea-floor spreading hypothesis? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Harry Hess

💡 Explanation: Hess proposed sea-floor spreading in 1961. Wegener gave continental drift, Holmes convection currents in the 1930s, and Ortelius the 1596 idea of joined continents.

Q2. Wegener's mega-ocean surrounding Pangaea was called: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Panthalassa

💡 Explanation: Pangaea means all earth and Panthalassa all water. Laurasia and Gondwanaland were the two halves Pangaea split into.

Q3. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the best-known example of a: easy

✅ Answer: (d) Divergent boundary

💡 Explanation: At divergent boundaries plates pull apart and new crust is generated. NCERT names the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as the best-known example.

Q4. Which observation supported sea-floor spreading? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Ocean rocks get older away from the ridge crest

💡 Explanation: Rocks nearest the ridge crest are youngest and age increases away from it. Ocean crust is under 200 million years old and its sediments are unexpectedly thin.

Q5. According to NCERT, India collided with Asia about: medium

✅ Answer: (d) 40-50 million years ago

💡 Explanation: India began moving north about 200 million years ago and collided with Asia 40-50 million years ago, causing rapid uplift of the Himalayas.

What is the difference between continental drift and plate tectonics?

Wegener's continental drift said the continents themselves moved, driven by pole-fleeing and tidal forces. Plate tectonics says rigid lithospheric plates move, carrying continents, driven by mantle convection.

Why was Wegener's theory rejected at first?

His evidence was strong, but most scholars thought the pole-fleeing and tidal forces he proposed were totally inadequate to move continents. Holmes's convection currents later supplied a better force.

Is the Indian plate still moving?

Yes. NCERT says the Indian plate's northward movement is still continuing, and scientists believe the Himalayas are still rising today because of the collision with Asia.
