Tropical vs Temperate (Extra-Tropical) Cyclones: Formation, Structure, Regional Names, Air Masses, Fronts and Tornadoes

Tropical: over warm seas above 27 °C, east to west, no fronts, more destructive • Temperate: along the polar front, west to east, warm and cold fronts, larger area

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

NCERT calls temperate cyclones extra-tropical or middle-latitude cyclones. Both kinds are low-pressure systems with winds circling the centre: anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern. They differ in almost everything else.

Tropical cyclones are violent storms that form over tropical oceans and move to the coasts, bringing destructive winds, very heavy rain and storm surges. They have different names in different oceans:

RegionName
Indian OceanCyclone
AtlanticHurricane
Western Pacific and South China SeaTyphoon
Western AustraliaWilly-willy

Conditions for formation:

  1. A large sea surface warmer than 27 °C
  2. The Coriolis force, which is why they do not form right at the equator
  3. Little variation in wind speed with height
  4. An existing weak low-pressure area or low-level cyclonic circulation
  5. Divergence high above the system

Energy: condensation in the towering cumulonimbus clouds around the centre releases heat. A steady supply of moisture from the sea keeps strengthening the storm. When the storm reaches land, that supply is cut off and it dies out. The point where it crosses the coast is its landfall. Cyclones that cross 20° N generally recurve and are more destructive.

  • Eye: a calm centre with sinking air. The circulating system around it is 150–250 km across.
  • Eye wall: a ring of strong, spiralling rising air that reaches the tropopause. Winds peak here, up to 250 km/h, with torrential rain.
  • Rain bands: spiral out from the eye wall, carrying cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds to the outer region.
  • Size and speed over Indian seas: over the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea and the Indian Ocean, the storm is 600–1,200 km across and moves only 300–500 km a day.
  • Storm surge: sea water pushed ashore floods coastal lowlands.

An air mass is a large body of air with nearly uniform temperature and moisture. It takes on the character of the surface it sits over, its source region.

Air massSource region
Maritime tropical (mT)Warm tropical and subtropical oceans
Continental tropical (cT)Subtropical hot deserts
Maritime polar (mP)Cold high-latitude oceans
Continental polar (cP)Very cold snow-covered continents in high latitudes
Continental arctic (cA)Permanently ice-covered Arctic and Antarctica

Where two air masses meet is a front, and its formation is frontogenesis. Fronts occur in middle latitudes and bring sharp changes in temperature, clouds and rain.

  • Cold front: cold air advances into warm air.
  • Warm front: warm air advances towards cold air.
  • Stationary front: neither side moves.
  • Occluded front: an air mass is lifted completely off the ground.

Life of a temperate cyclone: it starts on a stationary polar front, with warm air to the south and cold air to the north in the northern hemisphere. When pressure drops along the front, warm air pushes north and cold air south, setting up an anticlockwise circulation. Warm air glides over cold air ahead of the warm front, bringing a sequence of clouds and rain. Behind it, the cold front lifts warm air sharply and builds cumulus clouds. The cold front moves faster, catches up with the warm front, the warm air is lifted, the front is occluded, and the cyclone dies.

These are short-lived, local and violent. A thunderstorm is a towering cumulonimbus cloud built by intense convection on hot, moist days. It brings thunder, lightning and heavy rain, hail if the cloud reaches freezing heights, or a dust storm if moisture is short.

From a severe thunderstorm, a spiralling wind may descend "like a trunk of an elephant", with very low pressure at its centre. This is a tornado. Tornadoes mostly occur in middle latitudes, and over the sea they are called water spouts.

  • Direction: tropical cyclones move east to west, temperate cyclones west to east.
  • Eye vs eye wall: the eye is calm with sinking air. The eye wall has the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
  • Cold vs warm front: at a cold front, cold air advances into warm air. At a warm front, warm air advances towards cold air.
  • Hurricane vs typhoon: both are tropical cyclones; hurricanes form in the Atlantic, typhoons in the western Pacific and South China Sea.
  • Tornado vs water spout: a water spout is a tornado over the sea.

Q1. Tropical cyclones in the Western Pacific are called: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Typhoons

💡 Explanation: NCERT: cyclones in the Indian Ocean, hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the Western Pacific and South China Sea, willy-willies in western Australia.

Q2. Which statement about extra-tropical cyclones is correct? medium

✅ Answer: (a) They have a clear frontal system and move west to east

💡 Explanation: Extra-tropical cyclones form along the polar front with warm and cold fronts and move west to east; tropical cyclones have no fronts and move east to west.

Q3. A favourable sea surface temperature for tropical cyclones is above: easy

✅ Answer: (d) 27 °C

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists a large sea surface with temperature higher than 27 °C as the first condition.

Q4. The strongest winds and heaviest rain in a tropical cyclone occur in the: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Eye wall

💡 Explanation: The eye is calm with sinking air. Around it, the eye wall has spiralling rising air, maximum winds up to 250 km/h and torrential rain.

Q5. When a cold front overtakes a warm front and lifts the warm air off the ground, the front is: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Occluded

💡 Explanation: NCERT: if an air mass is fully lifted above the surface, the front is occluded; the cyclone then dissipates.

What is the difference between tropical and temperate cyclones?

Tropical cyclones form only over warm seas, have no fronts, move east to west, are smaller and far more destructive. Temperate cyclones form along the polar front over land or sea, have warm and cold fronts, cover larger areas and move west to east.

Why do tropical cyclones weaken over land?

Their energy comes from condensation of moisture drawn from the warm sea. Once they cross the coast at landfall, that moisture supply is cut off and the storm dissipates.

Is a tornado the same as a cyclone?

No. A tornado is a short-lived, very small, violent spiral of wind that descends from a severe thunderstorm, mostly in middle latitudes. A cyclone is a large pressure system hundreds of kilometres across.
