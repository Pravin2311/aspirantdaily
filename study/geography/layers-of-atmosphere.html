Layers of the Atmosphere: Troposphere, Stratosphere, Mesosphere, Thermosphere and Exosphere

Composition of air • Five layers with heights • Tropopause and mesopause • Ozone layer • Ionosphere and radio waves • Lapse-rate sums

UPSC SSC State PSC Railway 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

The atmosphere is the envelope of air that wraps the earth. It is colourless and odourless, and we notice it only when it moves as wind, yet it is part of the planet's mass and holds the oxygen we breathe and the carbon dioxide plants use. Most of it sits very close to the ground: NCERT notes that 99 per cent of the total mass of the atmosphere lies within 32 km of the earth's surface.

Exam questions on this topic come in two kinds. Some ask what the air is made of and how that changes with height. Others ask about the layers themselves: their order, their heights, which way temperature moves inside each one, and which layer does what. This page covers both, in the order and with the numbers used in the NCERT Class XI textbook, the standard school reference for this topic.

NCERT divides the atmosphere into three components: gases, water vapour and dust particles. The mix is not the same at every height. Oxygen becomes almost negligible by about 120 km, and carbon dioxide and water vapour are found only up to about 90 km.

Gases that matter for climate

Carbon dioxide lets incoming sunlight through but blocks part of the heat the earth radiates back, absorbing some and sending some back down. That is why the textbook calls it largely responsible for the greenhouse effect. Its share of the air has been rising for decades, mainly from burning fossil fuels.

Ozone sits between about 10 and 50 km above the surface. It acts as a filter that absorbs ultra-violet rays from the sun before they reach the ground.

Water vapour

Water vapour is a variable gas. It thins out with height and from the equator towards the poles. In the warm, wet tropics it can be about four per cent of the air by volume; over cold or dry deserts and polar regions it can drop below one per cent. It absorbs part of the incoming sunshine and holds in the heat the earth gives off, acting like a blanket, and it affects whether air is stable or unstable.

Dust particles

Sea salt, fine soil, smoke and soot, ash, pollen and fragments of meteors all float in the air. They gather mostly in the lower layers, and more of them are found over subtropical and temperate regions, where dry winds lift them, than over equatorial and polar areas. Dust and salt particles also act as hygroscopic nuclei: water vapour condenses around them to form clouds.

Air density is greatest at the surface and falls with height. The layers, though, are defined by how temperature behaves, and NCERT names five of them from the ground up: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.

A quick way to hold the order is the pattern of temperature: down, steady, up-ish, down, up. It falls through the troposphere, holds steady at the tropopause, stops falling in the ozone-rich stratosphere, falls again in the mesosphere, and rises in the ionosphere.

The troposphere is the lowest layer and the one we live in. Its average height is 13 km, but it is not even: roughly 8 km near the poles and about 18 km at the equator. It is thickest over the equator because strong convection currents carry heat high into the sky there.

Almost everything that counts as weather happens here. The layer holds the dust particles and water vapour that make clouds and rain, and NCERT calls it the most important layer for biological activity. Temperature drops steadily as you climb, at about 1°C for every 165 m.

The tropopause is the boundary zone between the troposphere and the stratosphere. Its air is about −80°C over the equator and about −45°C over the poles. Temperature here stays nearly constant, which is why it is called a pause.

Above the tropopause, the stratosphere reaches up to 50 km. Its defining feature is the ozone layer, which absorbs ultra-violet radiation and shields life on the earth from that harmful form of energy. The steady fall in temperature seen in the troposphere does not continue here; NCERT says the fall begins 'once again' only in the next layer up.

The mesosphere lies above the stratosphere and extends to about 80 km. Here temperature drops again with height and reaches about −100°C at 80 km. The top of the mesosphere is called the mesopause.

Above the mesopause, between about 80 and 400 km, lies the ionosphere. It is named for the electrically charged particles, or ions, it contains. Those ions reflect radio waves sent from the earth back down. Temperature in this region rises with height.

NCERT's list of five layers calls this part of the column the thermosphere. The exosphere is the layer above it, and it is the highest. Very little is known about it; whatever it contains is extremely rarefied, and it fades gradually into outer space.

Although every layer influences us, NCERT points out that geographers are mostly concerned with the first two, because weather lives in one and the ozone shield in the other. For how the lower air then moves as pressure belts, winds and cyclones, see the climatology and oceanography notes, and for India's seasons see climate and monsoon of India.

Questions sometimes turn the troposphere's cooling rate into a sum. The rule is simple: divide the height gained in metres by 165 to get the number of degrees lost. It works out to just over 6°C per kilometre.

Worked example 1: a hill station

A town at the foot of a range is at 30°C. What is the temperature 3,300 m higher? 3,300 ÷ 165 = 20, so the air is 20°C cooler: 30 − 20 = 10°C.

Worked example 2: degrees per kilometre

One kilometre is 1,000 m, and 1,000 ÷ 165 ≈ 6.06°C. So a rough rule of 6°C per km gives answers close to NCERT's rate.

Worked example 3: finding the height

If the ground is at 35°C and a cloud base is at 2°C, how high is the cloud base? The drop is 33°C, and each degree takes 165 m: 33 × 165 = 5,445 m.

At the top of an 8 km polar troposphere the same rule gives a drop of about 48°C, so a surface reading of 25°C would fall below −20°C by the tropopause. Real values vary with season and weather; the rate is an average.

The difference in troposphere height between the equator and the poles is 18 − 8 = 10 km, a gap examiners like to test.

  • ✘ Putting the ozone layer in the troposphere. It is in the stratosphere; ozone as a gas is found between 10 and 50 km.
  • ✘ Saying the troposphere is thickest at the poles. It is thickest at the equator (about 18 km) and thinnest near the poles (about 8 km).
  • ✘ Mixing up the boundaries. The tropopause tops the troposphere; the mesopause tops the mesosphere.
  • ✘ Counting the ionosphere as a sixth layer. NCERT's count is five.
  • ✘ Confusing 32 km (99 per cent of the mass), 90 km (limit of carbon dioxide and water vapour) and 120 km (oxygen almost negligible).

Q1. According to NCERT, roughly how high does the troposphere extend at the equator? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 18 km

💡 Explanation: The troposphere is about 18 km thick at the equator and about 8 km near the poles, averaging 13 km. Strong convection carries heat higher over the equator. 50 km is the top of the stratosphere.

Q2. Which layer contains the ozone layer? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Stratosphere

💡 Explanation: The stratosphere, which extends from the tropopause up to 50 km, contains the ozone layer that absorbs ultra-violet radiation.

Q3. Radio waves sent from the earth are reflected back by the: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Ionosphere

💡 Explanation: The ionosphere, between 80 and 400 km, holds electrically charged particles (ions) that reflect radio waves back to the earth.

Q4. At what height does NCERT say oxygen becomes almost negligible? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 120 km

💡 Explanation: Oxygen is almost negligible at 120 km. Carbon dioxide and water vapour stop at 90 km, and 32 km is the height within which 99 per cent of the atmosphere's mass lies.

Q5. With the troposphere's rate of 1°C fall per 165 m, a place at 30°C at sea level would have what temperature 3.3 km above it? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 10°C

💡 Explanation: 3,300 m ÷ 165 m = 20 steps of 1°C, so the temperature falls by 20°C: 30 − 20 = 10°C.

Is the ionosphere a separate sixth layer?

No. NCERT counts five layers by temperature: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere. It describes the region from 80 to 400 km by its electrically charged particles as the ionosphere, and then calls the layer above it the exosphere, lying above the thermosphere. So answer 'five' when asked how many layers there are.

Why is the troposphere thicker at the equator than at the poles?

Strong convectional currents over the hot equator carry heat to great heights, so the layer reaches about 18 km there against about 8 km near the poles.

Why do geographers focus on the lowest two layers?

Weather and climate change happens in the troposphere, and the stratosphere holds the ozone layer that shields life from ultra-violet radiation. NCERT notes that geographers are mainly concerned with these first two layers.
