Three Methods of Measuring National Income: Value Added, Expenditure and Income Methods with Worked Examples

Product method: sum of value added • Expenditure method: C + I + G + X − M • Income method: W + P + In + R • All three give the same GDP

UPSC SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

NCERT starts with a simple circular flow. Households supply labour and other factor services to firms. Firms pay them wages, rent, interest and profit. Households spend that income on the goods firms produce. The same money keeps going round, so the economy's annual output can be measured at any of three points in the circle:

Where we measureMethodWhat we add upPhase
Value of final goods and services producedProduct, or value added, methodValue added by every firmProduction
Spending on final goods and servicesExpenditure methodC + I + G + X − MDisposition
Payments to factors of productionIncome methodW + P + In + RDistribution

Strictly, each method measures GDP. National income (NNP at factor cost) is then reached by adjusting GDP for net factor income from abroad, depreciation and net indirect taxes. See GDP vs GNP vs NNP for those steps.

The double-counting problem. NCERT imagines an economy of farmers and bakers. Farmers grow ₹100 of wheat using only labour and sell ₹50 of it to bakers. Bakers use up that wheat to make ₹200 of bread. Adding the two outputs gives 100 + 200 = ₹300, but that is wrong: the ₹50 of wheat is counted once as wheat and again inside the bread.

The fix. Raw materials that one firm buys from another and uses up completely are intermediate goods. Count only each firm's value added:

Value added = Value of output − Intermediate goods used

FarmerBaker
Total production100200
Intermediate goods used050
Value added100200 − 50 = 150

GDP = 100 + 150 = ₹250.

  • A firm's value added is shared among the four factors, labour, capital, entrepreneurship and land, as wages, interest, profits and rent.
  • Gross value added (GVA) includes depreciation, also called consumption of fixed capital. Net value added = GVA − depreciation.
  • Inventories, or stocks of unsold goods and materials, are treated as capital. A change in inventories during the year is therefore counted as investment, whether it was planned or unplanned.

Summing GVA across all firms gives GDP: GDP = GVA1 + GVA2 + … + GVAN.

This method looks at the demand side and adds only final expenditure, meaning spending not made for intermediate purposes. In the farmer–baker economy, the bakers' ₹50 of wheat is intermediate and is left out. Final spending is ₹200 on bread plus ₹50 on the wheat sold for final use, so GDP = 200 + 50 = ₹250, the same as before.

Final expenditure on domestic output comes from four sources:

  • C, consumption: mostly by households.
  • I, investment: spending by firms on capital goods. Unlike intermediate goods, investment goods stay with the firm, so they are counted.
  • G, government: includes both government consumption and investment.
  • X, exports: spending by foreigners on domestic goods.

Part of C, I and G goes on imports, which are not domestic production. So imports, M, are subtracted:

GDP = C + I + G + X − M

NCERT notes that of these five items, investment is the most unstable.

Firms' revenues from final sales are paid out to the factors of production. So GDP also equals the total of factor incomes:

GDP = W + P + In + R, that is, wages and salaries + profits + interest + rent.

Putting the three methods together, NCERT writes: Σ GVA = C + I + G + X − M = W + P + In + R. Here I includes both planned and unplanned investment, such as unplanned changes in inventories.

NCERT's second example has two firms. Firm A uses no raw material and grows cotton worth ₹50, which it sells to firm B. Firm B turns it into cloth and sells the cloth to consumers for ₹200. A pays ₹20 in wages and keeps ₹30 as profit. B pays ₹60 in wages and keeps ₹90 as profit.

MethodWorkingGDP
Value addedA: 50 − 0 = 50; B: 200 − 50 = 150₹200
ExpenditureFinal spending by consumers on cloth₹200
IncomeWages (20 + 60) + profits (30 + 90)₹200

The ₹50 of cotton never appears as final expenditure. It is an intermediate good inside the cloth.

NCERT gives three reasons why a higher GDP need not mean people are better off:

  1. Distribution: growth may go to a few. In NCERT's illustration, 100 people each earn ₹10, so GDP is ₹1,000. The next year, 90 people earn ₹9 and 10 earn ₹20, so GDP is 90 × 9 + 10 × 20 = ₹1010. GDP rose, yet 90 per cent of people are worse off.
  2. Non-monetary exchanges: unpaid domestic work and barter in the informal sector are generally not counted, so GDP is underestimated.
  3. Externalities: benefits or harms caused to others without payment or penalty, such as pollution from a refinery. They have no market, so GDP does not net them out.
  • Intermediate vs investment goods: wheat used up by a baker is intermediate and excluded. A new oven the baker buys is investment and included.
  • Value added vs output: GDP adds value added, not the total sales of every firm.
  • Exports vs imports: exports are added and imports are subtracted in the expenditure method.
  • Income method items: wages, profit, interest and rent are factor incomes. Pensions and scholarships are transfer payments, not payment for production, so they are not part of this sum.
  • Gross vs net value added: the difference is depreciation.

Q1. Adding the full output value of every firm in an economy leads to: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Double counting

💡 Explanation: Intermediate goods would be counted once in the seller's output and again inside the buyer's output. The value added method avoids this.

Q2. By the expenditure method, GDP equals: easy

✅ Answer: (b) C + I + G + X − M

💡 Explanation: The expenditure method adds consumption, investment, government spending and exports, then subtracts imports. W + P + In + R is the income method.

Q3. A firm buys flour worth ₹40 and sells bread worth ₹100. Its value added is: easy

✅ Answer: (d) ₹60

💡 Explanation: Value added = value of output − intermediate goods = 100 − 40 = ₹60.

Q4. Which is not included in GDP under the expenditure method? medium

✅ Answer: (a) Wheat bought by a baker to make bread

💡 Explanation: Wheat used up by a baker is an intermediate good, so it is not final expenditure. Machines, government investment and exports are final.

Q5. NCERT gives which reason why GDP may not reflect welfare? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Rising GDP may be concentrated in a few hands

💡 Explanation: NCERT gives three reasons: uneven distribution, non-monetary exchanges and externalities. Its example shows GDP rising while 90 per cent of people became worse off.

What are the methods of calculating national income?

There are three: the product or value added method, which sums value added by all firms; the expenditure method, which sums final spending; and the income method, which sums wages, profits, interest and rent.

Why do all three methods give the same answer?

Money moves in a circle. What buyers spend on final goods is the firms' revenue, and firms pay all of it out to factors of production as wages, profits, interest and rent, so spending, output and income are equal.

Is a change in inventories part of GDP?

Yes. NCERT treats inventories as capital, so a rise in a firm's stock of unsold goods counts as investment in that year, whether the rise was planned or unplanned.
