Land Reforms in India: Abolition of Intermediaries, Land Ceilings, Results and the Constitutional Shield

Intermediaries and land to the tiller • Land ceilings • 200 lakh tenants • Loopholes and evictions • Kerala and West Bengal • Article 31A • Article 31B and Ninth Schedule • State List entry 18

UPSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

After 1947, land reforms meant changes in the ownership of landholdings, carried out to bring equity to agriculture. NCERT's Class XI text "Indian Economic Development" groups them into two policies:

  • Abolition of intermediaries (zamindars, jagirdars and others), so that the tiller becomes the owner of the land.
  • Land ceilings, fixing the maximum land an individual can own, to reduce the concentration of ownership in a few hands.

The record was mixed. Abolishing intermediaries brought some 200 lakh tenants into direct contact with the government. Ceilings, however, were delayed and evaded. NCERT names Kerala and West Bengal as the States where land reforms succeeded.

This page combines the NCERT chapter "Indian Economy 1950-1990" (Reprint 2026-27) with the constitutional provisions that protected land reform laws: Articles 31A and 31B, the Ninth Schedule and entry 18 of the State List.

At independence, the land tenure system was dominated by intermediaries, "variously called zamindars, jagirdars etc.". They collected rent from the actual tillers but contributed nothing to improving the farm. Agricultural productivity was low, and India had to import food from the United States.

About 75 per cent of the population depended on agriculture at the time. Growth and equity in farming were therefore central to the five year plans, whose four goals NCERT lists as growth, modernisation, self-reliance and equity. Land reforms served mainly the goal of equity.

"Just a year after independence", steps were taken to abolish intermediaries and make the tillers owners of the land. The logic is the idea of land to the tiller:

  • An owner gains from higher output, so has a reason to invest in the land.
  • A tenant has little reason to improve land when the landowner takes most of the gain.

NCERT illustrates the point with Soviet collective farms. Farmers there did not own land and gained or lost nothing from the result. NCERT says they packed rotten fruit with fresh fruit, a loss that an owner would never accept.

NCERT adds a condition: ownership gives an incentive to invest "provided sufficient capital was made available". Ownership alone is not enough without credit.

A land ceiling fixes the maximum size of land an individual may own. The aim is to reduce the concentration of land ownership in a few hands.

The Constitution gives some protection to holdings within the ceiling. Under the second proviso to Article 31A(1), inserted by the Seventeenth Amendment (1964), the State may not acquire land under a person's personal cultivation that is within the ceiling limit applicable to them unless the law provides compensation at least equal to market value.

NCERT's verdict is that land reforms succeeded in Kerala and West Bengal, whose governments were committed to land to the tiller. Other States lacked that commitment, and "vast inequality in landholding continues to this day". Looking back over 1950-1990, NCERT credits land reforms with abolishing the zamindari system.

Note on names. The Constitution now uses "Keralam". The Kerala (Alteration of Name) Act, 2026 changed the name with effect from 25 August 2026, as recorded in the footnotes to Article 31A. NCERT's 2026-27 reprint still says Kerala.

Land reform laws took property from landowners, so they were open to challenge under the Fundamental Rights. Parliament answered with constitutional amendments.

The Ninth Schedule opens with the Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950. Land is a State subject under entry 18, and the land laws at the head of the Ninth Schedule, such as the Bihar Act, are State Acts. That fits NCERT's point that results depended on each State government's commitment.

The Supreme Court's later rulings on the Ninth Schedule are outside this page. For Article 31A in detail, see the right to property, and for the State List, see the Seventh Schedule.

  • The two main measures were abolition of intermediaries and land ceilings.
  • About 200 lakh tenants came into direct contact with the government.
  • Kerala (now Keralam) and West Bengal are NCERT's examples of success.
  • Article 31A and Article 31B with the Ninth Schedule came from the First Amendment, 1951.
  • Land is entry 18 of the State List.

Why did land reforms fail in many States?

NCERT cites loopholes, evictions by landowners claiming to be self-cultivators, court delays, and transfers to relatives, plus a lack of political commitment outside Kerala and West Bengal.

Which amendment created the Ninth Schedule protection?

The First Amendment, 1951 inserted Article 31B, with effect from 18 June 1951, and Article 31A with retrospective effect, to shield land reform laws.
