Fiscal Policy vs Monetary Policy: Who Runs Each, Their Tools, How They Work, and the Multipliers and Deficits Behind Them

Fiscal: government spending and taxes (budget) • Monetary: RBI controls money supply • Repo is RBI's main tool • G multiplier 1/(1−c) • Balanced budget multiplier = 1

UPSC SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Both policies try to keep output, employment and prices steady. They differ in who runs them and which levers they pull.

NCERT lists three objectives of the government budget:

  • Allocation: providing public goods such as national defence, roads and administration, which the market cannot supply.
  • Redistribution: using taxes and transfers to move income towards a distribution society considers fair.
  • Stabilisation: raising demand when it is too weak for full employment, or restraining it when it would cause inflation.

The stabilisation idea comes from Keynes, who argued that governments should use spending and taxes to steady output and employment. Doing so means running a surplus or a deficit rather than always balancing the budget.

Automatic vs discretionary: a proportional income tax is an automatic stabiliser, a shock absorber. When GDP rises, part of the extra income goes in tax, so spending rises less. In a recession, disposable income falls less sharply. Welfare transfers work the same way. A deliberate policy change to steady the economy is discretionary fiscal policy.

FRBM Act, 2003: it requires three statements with the budget: the Medium-term Fiscal Policy Statement, which sets three-year rolling targets, the Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement and the Macroeconomic Framework Statement.

Non-debt creating capital receipts are mainly recovery of loans and proceeds from selling PSUs. A large revenue deficit inside the fiscal deficit means much of the borrowing is going on consumption.

Let c be the marginal propensity to consume, the share of each extra rupee of income that is spent. NCERT's Example 5.1 uses c = 0.8.

  • Government expenditure multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − c) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. A rise of 100 in G raises income by 5 × 100 = 500.
  • Tax multiplier = −c ÷ (1 − c) = −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = -4. A rise of 100 in taxes cuts income by -400. It is negative and smaller in absolute value, because taxes act only through disposable income and consumption.
  • Balanced budget multiplier = 1. If G and T both rise by 100, income rises by 500 − 400 = 100, exactly the rise in spending.

Try it: with c = 0.75, the expenditure multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4, so a rise of 50 in G raises income by 200.

With a proportional income tax the multiplier becomes smaller, because part of each round of extra income goes in tax.

The RBI issues currency, banks for the government, holds the foreign exchange reserves, and is the lender of last resort, ready to lend to banks at all times. It controls money supply with two kinds of tool.

ToolWhat it isTo reduce money supply
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)Share of deposits banks must keep as cash reservesRaise CRR, so banks can lend less
Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)Reserves banks must keep in liquid formRaise SLR
Open market operationsRBI buys or sells government bonds; outright (permanent) or repo (with a resale agreement)Sell bonds, which drains reserves
Repo rateRate at which RBI lends money by buying securities with an agreed resale date and priceRaise the repo rate
Reverse repo rateRate at which RBI withdraws money by selling securities it agrees to buy backRaise the reverse repo rate
Bank rateRate at which RBI lends to commercial banksRaise it, so bank loans become costlier

NCERT notes that repo and reverse repo operations, at maturities such as overnight, 7-day and 14-day, have become the RBI's main tool of monetary policy. CRR, bank rate and open market operations are quantitative tools. Moral suasion and margin requirements are qualitative.

  • Budget vs RBI: taxes, spending, deficits and the FRBM Act are fiscal. CRR, SLR, repo, OMO and bank rate are monetary.
  • Repo vs reverse repo: repo injects money (RBI lends); reverse repo withdraws it (RBI borrows).
  • Buying vs selling bonds: RBI buying raises money supply; RBI selling reduces it.
  • Fiscal vs primary deficit: they differ by net interest liabilities.
  • Expenditure vs tax multiplier: 1/(1−c) is positive and larger; −c/(1−c) is negative and smaller. Together they give a balanced budget multiplier of 1.
  • Automatic vs discretionary: built-in stabilisers such as proportional taxes need no new decision. Discretionary policy is a deliberate change.

Q1. Which is a tool of fiscal policy rather than monetary policy? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Changes in income tax rates

💡 Explanation: Fiscal policy works through government spending and taxes. Repo rate, CRR and open market operations are RBI's monetary tools.

Q2. When the RBI buys government bonds in the open market, money supply: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Rises

💡 Explanation: RBI pays for the bonds, which adds to banks' reserves and increases money supply. Selling bonds does the opposite.

Q3. NCERT says the main tool of RBI's monetary policy now is: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Repo and reverse repo operations

💡 Explanation: NCERT states that repo and reverse repo operations at various maturities have become RBI's main monetary policy tool.

Q4. If the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, a rise of 50 in government spending raises equilibrium income by: medium

✅ Answer: (b) 200

💡 Explanation: The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4, so income rises by 4 × 50 = 200.

Q5. Fiscal deficit minus net interest liabilities equals: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Primary deficit

💡 Explanation: NCERT defines gross primary deficit as gross fiscal deficit minus net interest liabilities, to show present fiscal imbalance.

What is the difference between fiscal and monetary policy in short?

Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and taxes through the budget. Monetary policy is the RBI's control of money supply and interest rates through repo, CRR, SLR, bank rate and open market operations.

Who makes fiscal policy and monetary policy in India?

Fiscal policy is made by the government through the Union Budget, under the FRBM Act. Monetary policy is run by the Reserve Bank of India, which NCERT calls the country's monetary authority.

Which policy should fight inflation?

Both can. The RBI can raise policy rates or CRR or sell bonds to cut money supply, and the government can reduce spending or raise taxes to lower demand. NCERT calls reducing demand part of the budget's stabilisation function.
