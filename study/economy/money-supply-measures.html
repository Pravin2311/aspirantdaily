Money Supply in India: M1, M2, M3 and M4, High-Powered Money and the Money Multiplier

M1 = CU + DD • M2 • M3 = M1 + time deposits • M4 • Narrow vs broad • High-powered money • CRR and the multiplier • Fiat money and legal tender • 2024-25 figures

UPSC SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

Money supply is the total stock of money in circulation among the public at a point in time. The RBI publishes four measures, M1 to M4:

MeasureDefinitionType
M1Currency held by the public (CU) + net demand deposits with commercial banks (DD)Narrow money
M2M1 + savings deposits with Post Office savings banksNarrow money
M3M1 + net time deposits of commercial banksBroad money
M4M3 + total deposits with Post Office savings organisations, excluding National Savings CertificatesBroad money

The measures run in decreasing order of liquidity: M1 is the most liquid and M4 the least. M3 is the most commonly used measure and is also called aggregate monetary resources. Behind all four sits high-powered money, the base on which banks create credit.

This page follows the NCERT Class XII chapter "Money and Banking" (Reprint 2026-27) and the RBI figures it reproduces.

In India, currency notes are issued by the RBI, the monetary authority, while coins are issued by the Government of India. Balances in savings and current accounts also count as money because cheques drawn on them settle transactions. These are demand deposits, payable on demand. Fixed deposits have a fixed maturity and are called time deposits.

Two terms often appear in exam questions:

  • Fiat money. A note or coin has little value as paper or metal. Its value comes from the guarantee of the issuing authority, so notes and coins are fiat money, without intrinsic value like a gold coin.
  • Legal tender. Notes and coins cannot be refused by any citizen for settling a transaction. A cheque can be refused, so demand deposits are not legal tender.

Four details decide most questions:

  1. "Net" deposits. Only deposits of the public are counted. Interbank deposits, which one bank holds in another, are not money supply.
  2. M2 and M3 both build on M1. M2 adds Post Office savings bank deposits, while M3 adds bank time deposits. M3 is not M2 plus something.
  3. M4 builds on M3. It adds all Post Office savings deposits, but leaves out National Savings Certificates.
  4. Narrow vs broad. M1 and M2 are narrow money; M3 and M4 are broad money.

Money supply is a stock variable. It is measured at a point in time, unlike national income, which is a flow over a period.

The currency issued by the central bank can be held by the public or by commercial banks. NCERT calls this currency "high-powered money", "reserve money" or the "monetary base", because it is the basis for credit creation. A rupee of high-powered money held as bank reserves can support several rupees of deposits. That is why the central bank's control over this base gives it control over the wider money supply.

Banks must keep a share of deposits as cash reserves. This is the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR). Banks must also keep some reserves in liquid form, the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR). The rest can be lent, and loans come back into the system as new deposits.

Worked example, following NCERT. Take a CRR of 20 per cent and reserves of ₹100.

  • Reserves of ₹100 can support deposits of ₹100 ÷ 0.20 = ₹500.
  • The money multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.
  • Of the ₹500 in deposits, the banking system can lend ₹500 − ₹100 = ₹400.
  • If there is no currency with the public, M1 = 0 + 500 = ₹500.

Now raise the reserve ratio to 25 per cent. The multiplier falls to 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4, so ₹100 of reserves supports only ₹400 of deposits and ₹300 of loans. Banks would have to call back some loans, and money supply falls. The reserve requirement is therefore a limit on money creation.

The multiplier comes from summing an infinite geometric series. NCERT's appendix shows that a + ar + ar² + … equals a ÷ (1 − r) when 0 < r < 1. With a = 1 and r = 0.4, the sum is 1 ÷ 0.6.

NCERT divides the central bank's tools into two groups:

  • Quantitative: changing the CRR, the bank rate, and open market operations.
  • Qualitative: persuasion such as moral suasion and margin requirements, which encourage or discourage lending.

Open market operations are the purchase and sale of government bonds. When the RBI buys a bond it adds reserves and money supply rises; when it sells, both fall. Outright operations are permanent. Repo operations carry an agreement to resell at a set date and price, and NCERT notes that repo and reverse repo operations "have now become the main tool" of RBI monetary policy. Raising the bank rate makes borrowing dearer for banks and reduces money supply. For rates and instruments in detail, see fiscal vs monetary policy.

  • Coins are issued by the Government of India, not the RBI. The RBI issues notes.
  • M3 = M1 + time deposits, not M2 + time deposits.
  • M4 excludes National Savings Certificates.
  • Demand deposits are money, but they are not legal tender.
  • A higher CRR means a smaller multiplier.

Q1. M1 is defined as: easy

✅ Answer: (a) Currency with the public + net demand deposits

💡 Explanation: M1 = CU + DD, the currency held by the public plus net demand deposits with banks.

Q2. M3 equals: medium

✅ Answer: (b) M1 + net time deposits of commercial banks

💡 Explanation: M3 builds on M1, not M2, by adding net time deposits of commercial banks.

Q3. Which measure is also called aggregate monetary resources? easy

✅ Answer: (c) M3

💡 Explanation: NCERT calls M3 the most commonly used measure and aggregate monetary resources.

Q4. With a reserve ratio of 25 per cent, the money multiplier is: medium

✅ Answer: (b) 4

💡 Explanation: The multiplier is 1 divided by the reserve ratio, so 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.

Q5. Which of these is NOT legal tender? easy

✅ Answer: (c) A cheque on a savings account

💡 Explanation: Cheques can be refused, so demand deposits are money but not legal tender.

Who issues coins in India?

Coins are issued by the Government of India. Currency notes are issued by the Reserve Bank of India, which NCERT describes as India's monetary authority.

Is M2 part of M3?

No. Both build on M1. M2 adds Post Office savings bank deposits, while M3 adds net time deposits of commercial banks, so M3 is not M2 plus time deposits.
