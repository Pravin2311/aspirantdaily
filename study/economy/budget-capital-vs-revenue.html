Capital Receipts vs Revenue Receipts, and Capital vs Revenue Expenditure: Two Tests, Worked Examples and Budget 2026-27 Figures

Revenue receipts: tax and non-tax • Capital receipts: borrowing, loan recovery, disinvestment • Revenue vs capital expenditure • Grants to States • 11 items classified • 2026-27 figures

UPSC SSC Banking State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every rupee the Union government receives or spends falls into one of four boxes: revenue receipts, capital receipts, revenue expenditure or capital expenditure. Exam questions on this topic almost always give you an item, such as disinvestment proceeds, interest paid, a loan to a State or a dividend from a public sector bank, and ask which box it goes in. You do not need to memorise long lists. Two tests, both taken from the NCERT Class XII Introductory Macroeconomics chapter on the government budget, settle nearly every case.

  • For a receipt: does it create a liability or reduce the government's assets? If yes, it is a capital receipt. If neither, it is a revenue receipt.
  • For an expenditure: does it create a physical or financial asset, or reduce a liability? If yes, it is capital expenditure. If neither, it is revenue expenditure.

Everything else on this page is those two tests applied to real items, with the 2026-27 Budget figures to show how large each box is.

NCERT describes revenue receipts as receipts "that do not lead to a claim on the government", which is why they are called non-redeemable. Nothing has to be paid back and no asset is given up. They come in two kinds.

  • Tax revenue: direct taxes such as income tax and corporation tax, and indirect taxes such as GST, customs and excise. In the Union Budget this is shown net of the States' share, because a large part of the taxes the Centre collects is passed on to the States.
  • Non-tax revenue: interest the Centre earns on loans it has given, dividends and profits from public sector companies and the RBI, fees and charges for services, and grants received from foreign governments and international bodies.

Interest received is a revenue receipt. Interest paid is revenue expenditure. Questions often swap the two.

The NCERT test is that capital receipts are receipts that "create liability or reduce financial assets". Borrowing creates a liability, since the loan must be repaid with interest. Selling shares in a public sector company reduces the government's assets, since the future dividends from those shares are gone. The textbook adds that capital receipts can therefore be debt creating or non-debt creating.

The non-debt creating capital receipts matter for the deficit: the fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus revenue receipts and non-debt capital receipts. The full working is on fiscal, revenue and primary deficit.

NCERT defines revenue expenditure as "expenditure incurred for purposes other than the creation of physical or financial assets of the central government". It is the cost of running the government:

  • salaries, pensions and the running costs of ministries and departments
  • interest payments on the government's debt, the largest single item
  • subsidies on food, fertiliser and fuel
  • defence spending on pay, stores and day-to-day operations
  • grants to State governments and other bodies

The last item is the trap. Grants to the States count as revenue expenditure in the Centre's accounts even when the States use them to build roads or schools, because the asset belongs to the State, not to the Centre. NCERT makes this point directly ("even though some of the grants may be meant for creation of assets"). That is why the Budget separately reports grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets and an effective revenue deficit that subtracts them.

In NCERT's words, capital expenditure is spending that results "in creation of physical or financial assets or reduction in financial liabilities". It includes:

  • buying land, buildings, machinery and equipment, and building roads, railways and other infrastructure
  • investing in shares of companies
  • loans and advances by the Centre to States, Union Territories, public sector companies and others
  • in the textbook classification, repaying the principal of past loans, which reduces a liability

A loan given by the Centre is capital expenditure, because the Centre acquires a financial asset (the right to be repaid). When it is repaid later, the repayment is a capital receipt.

Q1. Proceeds from disinvestment of a public sector company are a: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Capital receipt

💡 Explanation: Selling shares reduces the government's assets, so it is a capital receipt (non-debt creating). It appears in the Budget under 'other receipts'.

Q2. Interest received by the Centre on loans it gave to States is a: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Non-tax revenue receipt

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists interest receipts on loans by the central government as the first item of non-tax revenue. Repayment of the loan itself would be a capital receipt.

Q3. A grant from the Centre to a State for building schools is, in the Centre's accounts: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Revenue expenditure

💡 Explanation: Grants to States are revenue expenditure even when meant for creating assets, because the asset belongs to the State. The Budget reports them separately as grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets.

Q4. Which of these is capital expenditure? easy

✅ Answer: (a) A loan given by the Centre to a public sector company

💡 Explanation: A loan given creates a financial asset for the Centre, so it is capital expenditure. The other three create no asset.

Q5. In the 2026-27 Budget Estimates, the largest part of capital receipts is: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Borrowings and other liabilities

💡 Explanation: Borrowings and other liabilities are ₹16,95,768 crore of ₹18,14,165 crore of capital receipts, about 93%. Non-tax revenue is a revenue receipt.

Is borrowing a capital receipt or a revenue receipt?

A capital receipt, because it creates a liability: the loan has to be repaid with interest. It is the debt-creating part of capital receipts.

What is effective capital expenditure?

Capital expenditure plus grants-in-aid for creation of capital assets. It counts the assets the Centre helps create through grants as well as those it builds itself. For 2026-27 BE it is ₹17,14,523 crore.
