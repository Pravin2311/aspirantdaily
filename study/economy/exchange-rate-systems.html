Exchange Rate Systems: Flexible, Fixed and Managed Floating, and Devaluation vs Depreciation

Flexible: depreciation and appreciation • Fixed: devaluation and revaluation • Managed floating • Demand and supply of dollars • Speculation, interest rates, income • PPP • Speculative attacks

UPSC SSC Banking 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If ₹50 buys one dollar, the rate is ₹50 per dollar. Countries set it in one of three ways:

SystemWho sets the rateRupee gets cheaperRupee gets dearer
Flexible (floating)Market demand and supplyDepreciationAppreciation
FixedThe governmentDevaluationRevaluation
Managed floating ("dirty floating")The market, with central bank interventionNo separate term in NCERT; the market moves the rate within limits the central bank tolerates

So depreciation and devaluation describe the same direction of change, a cheaper rupee. They differ in the cause: market forces under a flexible rate, government action under a fixed rate. This page follows the NCERT Class XII chapter "Open Economy Macroeconomics" (Reprint 2026-27).

Indian textbooks quote the rate as rupees per dollar. An increase in that number means the rupee has lost value. When the rate moves from ₹50 to ₹70 per dollar, you need more rupees for each dollar. The dollar has risen in value against the rupee, and the rupee has fallen. Most exam mistakes on this topic come from reading a rise in the number as a stronger rupee.

Under a flexible rate, the rate settles where the demand for and supply of foreign exchange meet.

buy goods and services from abroad (imports)foreigners buying Indian goods and services (exports)

A higher price of foreign exchange makes imports dearer, so demand for dollars falls. It also makes Indian goods cheaper for foreigners, which raises exports and may raise the supply of dollars. NCERT notes that whether supply actually rises depends on the elasticity of demand for exports and imports.

  • More imports. If demand for imports rises, the demand curve for dollars shifts right and the rupee depreciates. NCERT's example moves the rate from ₹50 to ₹70.
  • Speculation. If investors expect the pound to rise from ₹80 to ₹85 within a month, buying 1,000 pounds for ₹80,000 promises ₹85,000 later, a gain of ₹5,000. The extra demand for pounds raises the rate now, so the belief fulfils itself.
  • Interest rates. If equally safe bonds pay 8 per cent in country A and 10 per cent in country B, the 2-point differential pulls funds to B. A's currency depreciates and B's appreciates. A rise in home interest rates often leads to appreciation of the home currency, assuming no limits on buying foreign bonds.
  • Income. Faster growth at home raises spending on imports, which tends to depreciate the currency. If income also rises abroad, exports rise too, and the net effect depends on which grows faster.

Purchasing power parity (PPP) says that, without tariffs or quotas, exchange rates should adjust in the long run so the same product costs the same in every currency, apart from transport costs.

NCERT's shirt example. A shirt costs $8 in the US and ₹400 in India, so the rate should be ₹400 ÷ 8 = ₹50 per dollar. At ₹60, the US shirt would cost ₹480 and everyone would buy the Indian one. Now let Indian prices rise 20 per cent (₹400 → ₹480) and US prices rise 50 per cent ($8 → $12). The PPP rate becomes ₹480 ÷ 12 = ₹40 per dollar. The dollar has depreciated, because US prices rose faster.

The rule: over the long run, the currency of the country with higher inflation tends to fall.

Under a fixed system the government sets the rate. Suppose the market rate is ₹50 per dollar and the government, wanting to encourage exports, fixes ₹70. At ₹70, more dollars are supplied than demanded, so the RBI buys the excess dollars with rupees. The government can hold any rate this way, but it keeps accumulating foreign exchange while it does so.

If instead it fixes a rate below the market, demand for dollars exceeds supply. The government must sell dollars from past holdings, and if it cannot, a black market for dollars may appear.

  • Devaluation: government action raises the rate, making the domestic currency cheaper.
  • Revaluation: government action lowers the rate, making the domestic currency dearer.

A fixed rate depends on credibility. If people doubt that the reserves are enough, they expect a devaluation and buy foreign currency aggressively. When that forces the government to devalue, it is a speculative attack. NCERT notes that such attacks were seen before the Bretton Woods system collapsed.

Without any formal international agreement, the world has moved to managed floating. It mixes a flexible rate (the float) with a fixed rate (the management). Central banks buy and sell foreign currencies to moderate exchange rate movements when they judge it appropriate, so official reserve transactions are not zero. It is also called dirty floating.

For India's balance of payments, reserves and capital flows, see external sector and trade. For how the RBI's tools work, see money supply measures and fiscal vs monetary policy.

The rate in rupees per dollar is the nominal rate. The real exchange rate is the relative price of foreign goods in terms of domestic goods, so it adjusts the nominal rate for price levels in both countries. A higher real rate makes foreign goods relatively dearer, which lowers imports. When choosing between Indian and foreign goods, the real rate is the one that matters.

Q1. If the rupee-dollar rate moves from ₹50 to ₹70 under a flexible system, the rupee has: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Depreciated

💡 Explanation: More rupees per dollar means the rupee lost value through market forces, which is depreciation.

Q2. Devaluation refers to: easy

✅ Answer: (b) A government action making the currency cheaper under a fixed rate

💡 Explanation: Under a fixed system, a government action that raises the exchange rate is devaluation.

Q3. Managed floating is also called: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Dirty floating

💡 Explanation: NCERT describes managed floating as also called dirty floating, with central bank intervention.

Q4. A shirt costs $8 in the US and ₹400 in India. The PPP exchange rate is: medium

✅ Answer: (b) ₹50

💡 Explanation: PPP equates prices, so ₹400 ÷ $8 gives ₹50 per dollar.

Q5. A rise in domestic interest rates, with free capital flows, usually leads to: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Appreciation

💡 Explanation: Higher returns attract foreign funds, raising demand for the home currency, so it appreciates.

What is the difference between devaluation and depreciation?

Both make the domestic currency cheaper. Depreciation happens through market forces under a flexible rate, while devaluation is a deliberate government action under a fixed rate.

Which exchange rate system does the world follow now?

NCERT says the world has moved, without a formal international agreement, to managed floating, where central banks intervene to moderate exchange rate movements.
