Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule): Grounds, Merger Exception and Who Decides

52nd Amendment, 1985 • Paragraph 2 grounds • Paragraph 4 merger • Paragraph 6 Speaker • 91st Amendment, 2003 • Articles 102(2), 191(2)

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-31

Before 1985, a legislator could leave the party on whose ticket he was elected, join another and keep his seat. Frequent floor-crossing toppled governments. The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 answered this by adding the Tenth Schedule, with effect from 1 March 1985. Article 102(2) then says a person is disqualified from either House of Parliament "if he is so disqualified under the Tenth Schedule", and Article 191(2) does the same for State legislatures.

The Schedule has eight paragraphs. The ones that carry the exam questions are paragraph 2 (grounds), paragraph 4 (merger), paragraph 5 (presiding officers) and paragraph 6 (who decides).

Type of memberDisqualified ifParagraph
Elected on a party ticketVoluntarily gives up membership of the party2(1)(a)
Elected on a party ticketVotes or abstains contrary to the party's direction without prior permission, and the party does not condone it within 15 days2(1)(b)
Elected as an independentJoins any political party after the election2(2)
NominatedJoins a political party more than six months after taking his seat2(3)

The Explanation to paragraph 2(1) fixes which party a member belongs to: an elected member belongs to the party that set him up as a candidate. A nominated member who is already in a party on the date of nomination belongs to it; otherwise he belongs to the party he joins within the first six months.

Two points are often missed. First, the Schedule does not define "voluntarily given up his membership", so the presiding officer decides it on the facts of each case. Second, the whip ground in 2(1)(b) applies to votes and abstentions "in such House", so it is about conduct on the floor, not speeches outside.

A member is not disqualified under paragraph 2(1) when his original party merges with another party and he either joins the merged party or opts to function as a separate group. The protection is narrow. Under paragraph 4(2) a merger is deemed to have taken place "if, and only if, not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned have agreed to such merger".

Worked case. A party has 30 members in a State Assembly. 18 of them announce a merger with another party. Two-thirds of 30 is 20, so the merger condition is not met and the 18 can be disqualified for voluntarily giving up membership. Had 20 or more agreed, all of them would be protected.

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003, with effect from 1 January 2004, tightened the law in three ways:

  • Omitted paragraph 3, the exception that textbooks describe as protecting a "split" by one-third of a legislature party. Since 2004 only a two-thirds merger protects a group.
  • Article 75(1A): the Council of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, may not exceed 15 per cent of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha (Article 164(1A) does the same for States).
  • Article 75(1B): a member disqualified under paragraph 2 cannot be appointed a Minister until the end of what would have been his term, or until he is elected again, whichever is earlier.

Paragraph 5 protects the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, and their State counterparts. If, on election to the office, the person gives up party membership and does not rejoin any party while in office, there is no disqualification. Rejoining the old party after leaving office is also allowed.

Paragraph 6(1) refers every question of disqualification to the Chairman or Speaker of the House, "and his decision shall be final". When the question concerns the Chairman or Speaker himself, the House elects a member to decide it. Paragraph 6(2) treats these proceedings as proceedings in Parliament under Article 122, or in the State Legislature under Article 212.

Paragraph 7 barred courts from any matter connected with disqualification under the Schedule. The official edition notes that this paragraph was declared invalid, for want of ratification under the proviso to Article 368(2), by the majority opinion in Kihoto Hollohon v. Zachilhu.

Most defection questions are fact patterns. Work through them in this order:

  • What kind of member? Elected on a party ticket, elected as an independent, or nominated. The applicable ground depends on this.
  • What did the member do? Left the party, defied a whip on the floor, or joined a party. Match the act to paragraph 2(1)(a), 2(1)(b), 2(2) or 2(3).
  • Is there an exception? Check for a merger backed by two-thirds of the legislature party, or a presiding officer covered by paragraph 5. For a whip violation, check whether the party condoned it within fifteen days.
  • Who decides? The Chairman or Speaker of that House, under paragraph 6.
  • ✘ "A one-third split is still protected." Paragraph 3 was omitted in 2004.
  • ✘ "Independents have six months to join a party." That window is only for nominated members.
  • ✘ "The President decides defection cases." The Speaker or Chairman does; the President decides other disqualifications under Article 103.
  • ✘ "Courts can never examine defection decisions." Paragraph 7's bar was struck down.

For where the Tenth Schedule sits among the others, see the 12 Schedules of the Constitution; for the Houses themselves, the Parliament of India notes.

Q1. The Tenth Schedule was added to the Constitution by the: easy

✅ Answer: (c) 52nd Amendment, 1985

💡 Explanation: The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 added the Tenth Schedule with effect from 1 March 1985.

Q2. For a merger to protect members from disqualification, how many members of the legislature party must agree? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Two-thirds

💡 Explanation: Paragraph 4(2) requires 'not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned'.

Q3. An independent MP joins a political party a year after election. Under the Tenth Schedule he: medium

✅ Answer: (b) is disqualified

💡 Explanation: Paragraph 2(2) disqualifies a member elected otherwise than as a party candidate who joins any party after the election. The six-month window applies only to nominated members.

Q4. Who decides whether a member of the Lok Sabha is disqualified on the ground of defection? medium

✅ Answer: (c) The Speaker

💡 Explanation: Paragraph 6(1) refers the question to the Chairman or Speaker of the House. Disqualification under Article 102(1), by contrast, goes to the President on the Election Commission's opinion.

Q5. The 91st Amendment, 2003 changed the anti-defection law by: medium

✅ Answer: (b) omitting paragraph 3

💡 Explanation: The Ninety-first Amendment omitted paragraph 3 with effect from 1 January 2004. It also added Article 75(1A) and (1B) on the size of the ministry and disqualified defectors.

Can a court review the Speaker's decision on defection?

Paragraph 7, which barred courts from such matters, is noted in the official edition as declared invalid in Kihoto Hollohon v. Zachilhu (1992 judgment, reported AIR 1993 SC 412) for want of State ratification. With that bar gone, the Speaker's decision can be brought before the courts.

Does the anti-defection law apply to State legislatures?

Yes. Article 191(2) applies the Tenth Schedule to members of State Legislative Assemblies and Legislative Councils, just as Article 102(2) does for Parliament.
