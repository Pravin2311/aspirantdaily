Removal of Supreme Court and High Court Judges: Article 124(4), Grounds, Special Majority, Article 218 and the Ramaswami Motion

Security of tenure • Grounds • Article 124(4) step by step • The double majority • Article 124(5) • High Courts under Article 218 • Ramaswami 1991-93 • Exam traps

UPSC State PSC SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

NCERT's Class 11 text Indian Constitution at Work treats security of tenure as one of the main protections of judicial independence. Judges hold office until they reach the age of retirement, and only in exceptional cases may they be removed. Security of tenure lets judges function without fear or favour.

The difficulty is deliberate. NCERT says the Constitution makers believed a difficult removal procedure would give members of the judiciary security of office. Two more safeguards support it:

  • Parliament cannot discuss judges' conduct except while removal proceedings are under way (Article 121).
  • The executive plays the crucial role in appointment, but the power of removal lies with the legislature. NCERT says this split ensures both a balance of power and the independence of the judiciary.

Article 124(2)(b) says a Supreme Court judge "may be removed from his office in the manner provided in clause (4)". There is no other route.

Article 124(4) says a Supreme Court judge shall not be removed except by an order of the President, passed after an address by each House of Parliament. Read clause by clause, the conditions are:

  1. Ground: proved misbehaviour or incapacity. No other ground is allowed.
  2. Address by each House: both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha must pass an address praying for removal.
  3. Double majority in each House: a majority of the total membership of that House, and not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.
  4. Same session: the address must be presented to the President in the same session.
  5. Presidential order: the President then passes the order of removal.

NCERT sums it up: a motion containing the charges against the judge must be approved by special majority in both Houses. Unless Members of Parliament broadly agree, a judge cannot be removed.

Both tests must be passed in each House, and they work differently:

TestCounted againstWhat it blocks
Majority of total membershipThe whole House, including absent and abstaining membersRemoval by a thin, poorly attended House
Two-thirds of members present and votingOnly those who actually voteRemoval by a bare majority over a large opposition

The first test is the one abstention can defeat, as the Ramaswami case showed. For how each type of majority is counted, see types of majority in Parliament.

Article 124(5) lets Parliament "by law regulate" two things:

  • the procedure for presenting the address; and
  • the investigation and proof of the judge's misbehaviour or incapacity.

That is why a removal motion leads to an inquiry before any vote. The word "proved" in Article 124(4) means the charge has to be investigated and established first. It cannot simply be asserted in the House.

Article 218 says clauses (4) and (5) of Article 124 apply to a High Court as they apply to the Supreme Court, with references to the High Court substituted. So a High Court judge is removed by the same route: the same grounds, an address by both Houses of Parliament with the same double majority, and an order of the President. The State legislature has no role in the removal.

NCERT says only one case of removing a Supreme Court judge has come before Parliament so far:

  • 1991: the first-ever motion to remove a Supreme Court justice was signed by 108 Members of Parliament. Justice V. Ramaswami was accused of misappropriating funds while Chief Justice of the Punjab and Haryana High Court.
  • 1992: a year after proceedings began, an inquiry commission of Supreme Court judges found him "guilty of wilful and gross misuses of office".
  • The vote: the motion won two-thirds of the members present and voting. But the Congress party abstained, so it did not get one-half of the total strength of the House.
  • The result: Ramaswami survived the motion and was not removed.

The case shows the procedure working as designed, if not as its supporters wished: without a broad consensus, the total-membership test cannot be met.

On appointment, NCERT notes that the Supreme Court has established "the principle of collegiality" in recommending names. The Chief Justice recommends in consultation with the four senior-most judges, and the group's view carries greater weight. Removal is the other end of the cycle and belongs to Parliament. Keeping the two powers in different hands is part of the separation of powers design.

  • The ground for removing a judge is proved misbehaviour or incapacity. "Violation of the Constitution" is the ground for impeaching the President.
  • Each House needs both a majority of total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting.
  • The address must be presented in the same session.
  • High Court judges are removed by Parliament, not the State legislature (Article 218).
  • Retirement ages: 65 for the Supreme Court and 62 for High Courts.

Q1. On what grounds can a Supreme Court judge be removed under Article 124(4)? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Proved misbehaviour or incapacity

💡 Explanation: Article 124(4) allows removal only on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. 'Violation of the Constitution' is the ground for impeaching the President.

Q2. The removal address must be supported in each House by: medium

✅ Answer: (b) A majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting

💡 Explanation: Article 124(4) needs both a majority of the total membership and at least two-thirds of those present and voting, in each House, in the same session.

Q3. Which Article applies the Supreme Court's removal procedure to High Court judges? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Article 218

💡 Explanation: Article 218 applies clauses (4) and (5) of Article 124 to High Courts as they apply to the Supreme Court.

Q4. Who passes the final order removing a Supreme Court judge? easy

✅ Answer: (d) The President

💡 Explanation: Article 124(4) says removal is by an order of the President passed after both Houses present the address.

Q5. Under Article 124(5), Parliament may by law regulate: medium

✅ Answer: (a) The procedure for the address and the investigation and proof of misbehaviour or incapacity

💡 Explanation: Article 124(5) lets Parliament legislate the procedure for presenting the address and for investigating and proving misbehaviour or incapacity.

Q6. Why did the removal motion against Justice V. Ramaswami fail, according to NCERT? hard

✅ Answer: (b) It did not get a majority of the total strength of the House

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the motion got two-thirds of those present and voting, but with the Congress abstaining it lacked one-half of the total strength.

Q7. Until what age does a High Court judge hold office? easy

✅ Answer: (c) 62

💡 Explanation: Article 217(1) says a High Court judge holds office until the age of sixty-two years; a Supreme Court judge serves until 65 under Article 124(2).

Q8. Parliament may discuss the conduct of a judge only: medium

✅ Answer: (d) On a motion for an address seeking removal

💡 Explanation: Article 121 bars discussion of judges' conduct in Parliament except upon a motion for presenting an address praying for removal.

Is removal of a judge called impeachment?

The word is used loosely, but the Constitution speaks of removal by an order of the President after an address by each House under Article 124(4). Impeachment is the term the Constitution uses for the President under Article 61.

Has any Supreme Court judge been removed?

NCERT says only one removal case for a Supreme Court judge has come before Parliament, against Justice V. Ramaswami, and it failed because the motion lacked a majority of the total strength of the House.
