Ordinance-Making Power of the President Under Article 123: Conditions, Life and Limits

Article 123 • Article 85(1) six-month gap • Article 74 advice • Governor's ordinances, Article 213

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Parliament makes laws, but Parliament is not always sitting. Article 123 lets the President make a temporary law, called an ordinance, when Parliament is not in session and something cannot wait. While it lasts, an ordinance "shall have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament".

Because it is law made by the executive, the Constitution hedges it with conditions: when it can be made, how long it lives and what it may contain. Exam questions almost always test one of these three.

Article 123(1) allows an ordinance "at any time, except when both Houses of Parliament are in session". Read the words carefully:

  • If both Houses are sitting, no ordinance can be made.
  • If either House is not in session, the power is available, because then the two Houses cannot pass a Bill together.

The second condition is that the President "is satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action". The ordinance can contain whatever "the circumstances appear to him to require".

This satisfaction is not the President's personal judgment. Under Article 74(1), the President acts "in accordance with" the advice of the Council of Ministers. He may ask the Council to reconsider its advice once, but must then act on the advice given after reconsideration. In practice, an ordinance is a Cabinet decision that the President signs.

An ordinance is a bridge, not a replacement for legislation. Article 123(2)(a) says every ordinance:

  • must be laid before both Houses when Parliament reassembles; and
  • ceases to operate at the end of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament.

It can end sooner in two ways. If both Houses pass resolutions disapproving it within those six weeks, it ends when the second resolution is passed. And under Article 123(2)(b), the President may withdraw it at any time.

If the two Houses are summoned to reassemble on different dates, the Explanation to Article 123(2) says the six weeks run from the later date.

To keep the law alive beyond six weeks, Parliament must pass a Bill that replaces the ordinance.

The Constitution does not state a single maximum, but two provisions fix it in practice:

ProvisionWhat it says
Article 85(1)Six months must not pass between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next
Article 123(2)(a)The ordinance lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles

Add them together. An ordinance issued just after a session ends can live until Parliament next meets, at most about six months later, and then for six more weeks. That is why textbooks give the maximum life as six months and six weeks. It is a calculation from these two Articles, not a phrase in the Constitution.

Article 123(3) sets the limit on content: an ordinance is void "if and so far as" it makes a provision "which Parliament would not under this Constitution be competent to enact".

So the ordinance power is exactly as wide as Parliament's ordinary law-making power, and no wider:

  • It can deal with subjects on which Parliament can legislate.
  • It cannot deal with a State subject that Parliament could not legislate on at that time.
  • It is subject to Fundamental Rights in the same way as an Act of Parliament.
  • It cannot amend the Constitution, because an amendment needs the special procedure of Article 368, not an ordinary law.

The text of Article 123 has an empty clause (4). The footnote in the official edition explains that clause (4) was inserted by the Constitution (Thirty-eighth Amendment) Act, 1975, with retrospective effect, and omitted by the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, with effect from 20 June 1979.

For exams, the takeaway is the pattern: an Emergency-era amendment added a clause to Article 123, and the 44th Amendment, which reversed many Emergency changes, removed it.

Governors have a parallel power under Article 213. The structure is the same, with one important difference.

PointPresident (Art. 123)Governor (Art. 213)
WhenExcept when both Houses of Parliament are in sessionExcept when the Assembly is in session (or both Houses, where there is a Council)
ForceSame as an Act of ParliamentSame as an Act of the State Legislature assented to by the Governor
LifeSix weeks from reassembly of ParliamentSix weeks from reassembly of the Legislature
WithdrawalBy the President at any timeBy the Governor at any time
Prior instructionsNot requiredRequired from the President in three cases

The three cases in the proviso to Article 213(1) are those where a Bill with the same provisions would have needed the President's previous sanction, would have been reserved for the President, or would have been invalid without the President's assent. In those cases the Governor cannot act alone.

  • ✘ "An ordinance can be issued when Parliament is in session." Not when both Houses are in session; one House being out of session is enough.
  • ✘ "An ordinance lasts six months." It lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles; six months and six weeks is only the outer limit.
  • ✘ "An ordinance is weaker than an Act." While it lasts, it has the same force and effect as an Act.
  • ✘ "The President issues ordinances at his own discretion." He acts on ministerial advice under Article 74(1).

For the President's other legislative powers, see veto powers and Union and State executive.

Q1. The President can promulgate an ordinance under Article 123 when: easy

✅ Answer: (b) at least one House is not in session

💡 Explanation: Article 123(1) allows an ordinance 'except when both Houses of Parliament are in session'. If even one House is not sitting, the power is available.

Q2. An ordinance not replaced by an Act ceases to operate: easy

✅ Answer: (b) six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament

💡 Explanation: Article 123(2)(a): it ceases at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament, unless both Houses disapprove it earlier.

Q3. If the Lok Sabha reassembles on 1 July and the Rajya Sabha on 8 July, the six weeks are counted from: medium

✅ Answer: (b) 8 July

💡 Explanation: The Explanation to Article 123(2) says the six weeks are reckoned from the later of the two dates.

Q4. An ordinance on a subject in the State List (with no emergency or other enabling provision) would be: medium

✅ Answer: (c) void

💡 Explanation: Article 123(3): an ordinance is void to the extent it makes a provision Parliament could not enact.

Q5. Which statement about the Governor's ordinance power under Article 213 is correct? medium

✅ Answer: (b) In some cases the Governor needs instructions from the President

💡 Explanation: The proviso to Article 213(1) lists three cases in which the Governor cannot promulgate an ordinance without instructions from the President.

Does the President decide personally whether to issue an ordinance?

No. Under Article 74(1) the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. He may send the advice back once for reconsideration, but must then act on the advice given.

Can an ordinance amend the Constitution?

Article 123(3) makes an ordinance void to the extent Parliament could not enact the provision as an ordinary law. A constitutional amendment needs the separate Article 368 procedure, so an ordinance cannot do what only that procedure can.
