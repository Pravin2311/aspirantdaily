High Court Jurisdiction: Writs Under Article 226, Superintendence Under Article 227, and Articles 215, 228 to 231

Article 226 writs • 'any other purpose' • two-week interim rule • Article 227 superintendence • Armed Forces exclusion • Article 228 • 215 • 229 • 230 and 231

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

Chapter V of Part VI gives every High Court a set of powers that go beyond hearing appeals. Two of them, writs under Article 226 and superintendence under Article 227, appear in almost every polity paper. They are often confused, so this page puts them side by side with the related Articles.

Article 226(1) says that, notwithstanding Article 32, every High Court can issue to "any person or authority, including in appropriate cases, any Government" within its territories, directions, orders or writs, "including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari". It can do so "for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III and for any other purpose".

The phrase "for any other purpose" is the key difference from the Supreme Court. Under Article 32, the Supreme Court issues writs only to enforce Fundamental Rights. A High Court can also issue them to enforce legal and constitutional rights outside Part III, such as the right to property under Article 300A.

Other clauses of Article 226:

  • Cause of action (226(2)). A High Court may issue writs where the cause of action arises wholly or partly within its territory, even if the Government, authority or person is located elsewhere.
  • Interim orders (226(3)). If an interim order such as a stay was made without giving the other party copies of the petition and a hearing, that party can apply to vacate it. The High Court must decide within two weeks, or the interim order stands vacated.
  • No derogation (226(4)). This power does not reduce the Supreme Court's power under Article 32(2).

The five writs themselves are explained on five writs under Articles 32 and 226.

Article 227(1): "Every High Court shall have superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction."

Superintendence is a supervisory power over lower bodies as institutions, not a power that depends on a party coming to court to enforce a right. Clause (2) lists examples. The High Court may:

  1. (a) call for returns from the courts;
  2. (b) make general rules and prescribe forms regulating their practice and proceedings;
  3. (c) prescribe forms for books, entries and accounts kept by their officers.

Under clause (3), it may also settle tables of fees for sheriffs, clerks, officers, attorneys, advocates and pleaders. Rules, forms and tables under clauses (2) and (3) must be consistent with existing law and need the Governor's previous approval.

One exclusion (227(4)). No superintendence over any court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the Armed Forces.

History. Clause (1) was substituted by the 42nd Amendment, in force from 1 February 1977, and substituted again by the 44th Amendment, in force from 20 June 1979, giving the present text. A clause (5) inserted by the 42nd Amendment was omitted by the 44th.

If a High Court is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law about the interpretation of the Constitution, and that answering it is necessary to dispose of the case, it shall withdraw the case. It may then either:

  • (a) dispose of the case itself; or
  • (b) decide the question of law and return the case with a copy of its judgment. The lower court must then decide the case in conformity with that judgment.

This keeps constitutional interpretation out of the subordinate courts' hands.

  • Court of record (215). Every High Court has all the powers of a court of record, including the power to punish for contempt of itself. The Supreme Court has the same status under Article 129.
  • Staff (229). Officers and servants are appointed by the Chief Justice, or a Judge or officer the Chief Justice directs. The Governor may require consultation with the State Public Service Commission for some posts. Rules on salaries, allowances, leave or pensions need the Governor's approval. Administrative expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Union territories (230). Parliament may by law extend or exclude a High Court's jurisdiction over a Union territory. A State Legislature cannot increase, restrict or abolish that jurisdiction.
  • Common High Court (231). Parliament may by law establish a common High Court for two or more States, or for two or more States and a Union territory.

The Supreme Court's own jurisdictions are compared on Supreme Court jurisdiction, and judges' appointment and landmark cases on judiciary and landmark cases.

  • 226: writs to any person, authority or Government, for Part III rights "and for any other purpose"; cause-of-action reach; interim orders vacated if not decided in two weeks.
  • 227: superintendence over all courts and tribunals, except Armed Forces tribunals; rules need the Governor's approval.
  • 228: substantial constitutional question in a lower court: the High Court must withdraw the case.
  • 215: court of record with contempt power.
  • 230, 231: Parliament extends jurisdiction to Union territories and may create common High Courts.

Q1. Which phrase makes the writ jurisdiction of High Courts wider than that of the Supreme Court? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 'For any other purpose'

💡 Explanation: Article 226(1) allows writs for Part III rights 'and for any other purpose'. Article 32 is limited to Part III.

Q2. High Courts have NO superintendence over: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Courts and tribunals under Armed Forces law

💡 Explanation: Article 227(4) excludes courts and tribunals constituted under any law relating to the Armed Forces.

Q3. If an ex parte interim order is challenged under Article 226(3) and not decided in time, it stands vacated after: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Two weeks

💡 Explanation: The High Court must dispose of the application within two weeks, or the interim order stands vacated.

Q4. Rules made by a High Court under Article 227(2) require: hard

✅ Answer: (b) Previous approval of the Governor

💡 Explanation: The proviso to Article 227(3) requires the previous approval of the Governor.

Q5. A common High Court for two or more States is established by: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Parliament by law

💡 Explanation: Article 231(1) lets Parliament establish a common High Court by law.

What is the difference between Article 226 and Article 227?

Article 226 lets a High Court issue writs to any person, authority or Government; Article 227 gives it supervisory control over courts and tribunals in its territory.

Can a High Court enforce rights other than Fundamental Rights?

Yes. Article 226(1) allows writs for the enforcement of Part III rights and for any other purpose, which covers legal rights outside Part III.
