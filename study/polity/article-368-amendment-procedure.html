Article 368: The Three Ways the Constitution Can Be Changed

Initiation • Special majority • Ratification by half the States • President's assent • Changes outside Article 368 • Basic structure

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

The Indian Constitution is neither as hard to amend as the United States Constitution nor as easy as an ordinary law. It sorts its provisions into three groups, each with its own procedure:

  1. changes that can be made by an ordinary law passed by a simple majority, which the Constitution says are not amendments under Article 368;
  2. amendments under Article 368 that need a special majority in each House of Parliament;
  3. amendments under Article 368 that need the special majority and ratification by at least half of the States.

Questions usually test which group a provision belongs to, so the useful skill is to recognise the short list in groups 1 and 3. Everything else falls in group 2.

Clause (1) gives Parliament the power to amend "by way of addition, variation or repeal" any provision, in exercise of its constituent power. Clause (2) lays down the steps:

  • Initiation: only by introducing a Bill in either House of Parliament. State Legislatures cannot start an amendment, and no prior recommendation of the President is needed.
  • Special majority: the Bill must pass each House by a majority of that House's total membership and by at least two-thirds of the members present and voting. Both tests must be met in both Houses.
  • Ratification, where the proviso applies: the legislatures of at least one-half of the States must pass resolutions ratifying the amendment before it goes to the President. No time limit is set for ratification.
  • Assent: the President "shall give his assent", after which the Constitution stands amended. The President has no option to withhold assent or return the Bill.

Because each House must pass the Bill separately, a disagreement between the Houses cannot be settled at a joint sitting. If either House fails to muster the special majority, the Bill falls.

The proviso to clause (2) protects the federal structure. An amendment needs State ratification if it changes any of the following:

A memory aid: ratification is needed where a change would shift power between the Union and the States, alter how the States take part in choosing the President or sending members to Parliament, or touch the courts that decide Union–State disputes.

Several provisions can be changed by Parliament through an ordinary law, and the Constitution expressly says such laws are not amendments for the purposes of Article 368. The most tested are:

  • admitting or establishing new States, and forming new States or changing State boundaries and names (Articles 2 and 3, read with Article 4(2));
  • creating or abolishing a State Legislative Council (Article 169(3));
  • creating legislatures or councils of ministers for certain Union territories (Article 239A(2));
  • amending the Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas) and the Sixth Schedule (tribal areas of the North-East).

Other provisions, such as quorum, salaries of members, and rules about citizenship, are left by the Constitution to "Parliament by law". Changing them is ordinary law-making, not amendment.

Clause (3) says Article 13 does not apply to an amendment under Article 368. Article 13 voids laws that take away Fundamental Rights, so clause (3) makes clear that an amendment is not "law" in that sense. It was added after the Supreme Court in Golaknath (1967) held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights.

In Kesavananda Bharati (1973) the Court accepted that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but held that it cannot alter the Constitution's basic structure. Parliament responded with clauses (4) and (5), added by the 42nd Amendment (1976), which bar court challenges to amendments and declare that there is "no limitation whatever" on the amending power. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court struck those two clauses down as themselves violating the basic structure, because limited amending power is part of that structure. They remain in the printed text but are not in force.

  • ✘ "Amendments need a two-thirds majority of the total membership." The two-thirds is of those present and voting; the total-membership test is a simple majority.
  • ✘ "Fundamental Rights need State ratification." They need only the special majority.
  • ✘ "A joint sitting can pass an amendment." It cannot.

Q1. In which House can a Constitution Amendment Bill be introduced? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Either House of Parliament

💡 Explanation: Article 368(2) allows the Bill to be introduced in either House. Unlike a Money Bill, it is not limited to the Lok Sabha.

Q2. Which majority does Article 368 require in each House for an amendment? easy

✅ Answer: (c) A majority of the total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting

💡 Explanation: Both conditions apply together in each House: a majority of the House's total membership, and at least two-thirds of the members present and voting.

Q3. An amendment to which of these needs ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures? medium

✅ Answer: (d) The Lists in the Seventh Schedule

💡 Explanation: The proviso to Article 368(2) names the Seventh Schedule Lists. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles need only the special majority.

Q4. Creating a new State under Article 3 requires: medium

✅ Answer: (a) a law passed by a simple majority

💡 Explanation: Article 4(2) says such a law is not an amendment for the purposes of Article 368, so it is passed like an ordinary law.

Q5. Which case held that Parliament cannot use Article 368 to alter the basic structure of the Constitution? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Kesavananda Bharati (1973)

💡 Explanation: Kesavananda Bharati laid down the basic structure doctrine. Minerva Mills later applied it to strike down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368.

Is there a joint sitting if the two Houses disagree on an amendment Bill?

No. Article 368 requires the Bill to be passed by each House separately with the special majority, so the joint sitting procedure of Article 108, which is for ordinary Bills, does not apply. If either House fails to pass it, the Bill falls.

Can the President refuse assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill?

No. Since the 24th Amendment of 1971, Article 368(2) says the President shall give assent to a Bill passed by the required majorities and, where needed, ratified by the States. The President cannot withhold assent or return it.
