Secularism in the Indian Constitution: Meaning, Indian vs Western Model, Articles 25 to 30 and the Main Criticisms

Meaning • Western vs Indian model • Three differences • Articles 17, 25-30 • 42nd Amendment • Nehru vs Ataturk • Criticisms answered • Exam traps

UPSC State PSC SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

NCERT's Class 11 Political Theory text (Chapter 8, "Secularism") defines secularism first as a doctrine that opposes inter-religious domination, where members of one community are targeted because of their religion. It adds an equally important second side: opposition to intra-religious domination, such as the oppression of dalits or women within a religion.

Put positively, in NCERT's words, secularism "promotes freedom within religions, and equality between, as well as within, religions".

The word "SECULAR" entered the Preamble through the 42nd Amendment (1976), which changed "SOVEREIGN DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC" to "SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC". The secular arrangements in Part III were there from 1950.

NCERT notes that all secular states share one thing: they are neither theocratic nor do they establish a religion. Beyond that, the two models differ sharply.

NCERT gives a contrast from France. The French government banned religious markers such as turbans and veils in educational institutions. India has no such prohibition, because its ideal of secularism is different.

  1. Domination within and between religions: Indian secularism opposes, equally, all three of these:
    • the oppression of dalits and women within Hinduism;
    • discrimination against women within Indian Islam or Christianity;
    • threats a majority community might pose to minorities.
  2. Community rights: it protects the religious freedom of minority communities as well as individuals. Minorities have the right to exist and to maintain their own culture and educational institutions.
  3. State-supported reform: because it cares about intra-religious domination, Indian secularism allows the state to support religious reform. The Constitution bans untouchability, and laws have abolished child marriage and lifted the taboo on inter-caste marriage.

NCERT sums this up as principled state intervention. The state can engage with religion negatively, to oppose religious tyranny, as in the ban on untouchability. It can also engage positively, as when it lets minorities run educational institutions that may receive state aid. That is why NCERT says "equal respect for all religions" does not capture Indian secularism. Religiously sanctioned caste hierarchies, for example, get no respect from the secular state.

Article 25(2) is the constitutional basis of "principled distance". It lets the State reform religious practice without establishing any religion.

NCERT calls Nehru "the philosopher of Indian secularism". Asked by a student what secularism meant, he answered "equal protection by the State to all religions". His secular state was one that protects all religions but favours none, and adopts no state religion.

Nehru did not practise any religion or believe in God, yet secularism for him did not mean hostility to religion. That made him very different from Kemal Atatürk in Turkey, whose secularism suppressed religion. Nehru also opposed a complete separation of religion and state, holding that a secular state can interfere in religion to bring about social reform.

NCERT describes Atatürk's version, practised in Turkey in the first half of the twentieth century, as "active intervention in and suppression of" religion rather than principled distance. He set out to end the institution of Khalifa in public life and to modernise Turkey aggressively. NCERT asks students to compare it with Indian secularism, which leaves people free to keep their names, dress and language.

CriticismNCERT's response
Anti-religiousSecularism opposes institutionalised religious domination, which is not the same as being anti-religious. It protects religious identity by promoting religious freedom and equality
Western importSecularism has both western and non-western origins. In India, the peaceful coexistence of communities was central, and the idea of separation can be read differently by different societies
MinoritismMinority rights protect fundamental interests that a simple majority vote should not override. NCERT illustrates this with a train-compartment example of a smoker and an asthmatic
  • "Secular" was added to the Preamble in 1976 by the 42nd Amendment, but secular provisions such as Articles 25 to 30 were there from the start.
  • Indian secularism means principled distance, not a wall of separation. The State may intervene to reform religious practice under Article 25(2).
  • Article 28(1) bars religious instruction only in institutions wholly maintained by State funds.
  • Article 30 rights belong to both religious and linguistic minorities.

Q1. The word 'Secular' was added to the Preamble by the: easy

✅ Answer: (a) 42nd Amendment, 1976

💡 Explanation: The Preamble's footnote says the 42nd Amendment substituted 'Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic' for 'Sovereign Democratic Republic'.

Q2. Which phrase best describes the Indian model of secularism? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Principled distance and intervention

💡 Explanation: NCERT says Indian secularism allows principled state intervention, unlike the western model of mutual exclusion.

Q3. NCERT says Indian secularism opposes intra-religious domination. An example is: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Oppression of dalits within Hinduism

💡 Explanation: NCERT lists the oppression of dalits and women within Hinduism as intra-religious domination Indian secularism opposes.

Q4. No religious instruction can be given in institutions wholly maintained out of State funds under: medium

✅ Answer: (d) Article 28(1)

💡 Explanation: Article 28(1) bars religious instruction in any educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.

Q5. Article 30 gives the right to establish educational institutions to: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Religious and linguistic minorities

💡 Explanation: Article 30(1) covers all minorities, whether based on religion or language.

Q6. Who did NCERT call 'the philosopher of Indian secularism'? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Jawaharlal Nehru

💡 Explanation: NCERT's Secularism chapter calls Nehru the philosopher of Indian secularism and quotes his 'equal protection' definition.

Q7. Which Article lets the State make laws for social welfare and reform despite freedom of religion? hard

✅ Answer: (c) Article 25(2)

💡 Explanation: Article 25(2)(b) allows laws providing for social welfare and reform, including opening Hindu religious institutions to all.

Q8. NCERT contrasts Indian secularism with that of which leader, who suppressed religion in Turkey? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Kemal Ataturk

💡 Explanation: NCERT says Nehru's secularism was very different from Ataturk's in Turkey, which actively intervened to suppress religion.

How is Indian secularism different from western secularism?

The western model keeps state and religion mutually exclusive. Indian secularism keeps principled distance, opposes domination within religions too, and allows state-supported reform.

When was 'secular' added to the Constitution?

The 42nd Amendment of 1976 added 'Socialist Secular' to the Preamble, effective 3 January 1977. Secular provisions like Articles 25 to 30 existed from 1950.
