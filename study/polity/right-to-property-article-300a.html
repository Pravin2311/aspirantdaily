Right to Property: How the 44th Amendment Moved It From Part III to Article 300A

44th Amendment • Article 300A • Omitted 19(1)(f) and 31 • Article 32 vs 226 • Article 30(1A) • Article 31A market-value proviso • Exam traps

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

The right to property is the only right that has been moved out of Part III altogether. It was a Fundamental Right until the 44th Amendment (1978). That amendment, in force from 20 June 1979, did three things:

  1. omitted sub-clause (f) of Article 19(1), the property freedom among the Article 19 rights;
  2. omitted Article 31, "Compulsory acquisition of property", and the sub-heading "Right to Property" in Part III;
  3. inserted a new Chapter IV in Part XII, headed "Right to Property", containing a single Article, 300A.

Article 300A reads in full: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law."

Article 300A gives one guarantee: the executive cannot take property without a law that authorises it. Two consequences follow from the wording:

  • Law, not executive order. Deprivation must rest on a law made by a competent legislature. A bare executive instruction is not enough.
  • No express compensation clause. Unlike the omitted Article 31, Article 300A does not mention compensation or an "amount". The text itself does not require payment, though the law under which property is taken may provide for it.

The Article begins "No person", so on its wording the protection is not limited to citizens.

Article 32 gives a right to move the Supreme Court only "for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part", meaning Part III. Article 300A is in Part XII, so a person whose property is taken unlawfully cannot go directly to the Supreme Court under Article 32 for that alone. The usual routes are:

  • a writ petition to the High Court under Article 226, which covers "any other purpose" besides Fundamental Rights; or
  • an ordinary civil suit.

The two writ routes are compared on writs under Articles 32 and 226.

Removing the Fundamental Right did not remove every reference to property from Part III. Exams like to test the pieces that remain:

Article 31A(1)Laws acquiring estates, taking over management, amalgamating corporations and similar matters cannot be voided for violating Article 14 or 191st Amendment (1951); 44th Amendment replaced "article 14, article 19 or article 31" with "article 14 or article 19"

So compensation at market value is still a constitutional requirement in one narrow case: a cultivator's own land within the ceiling limit, under the second proviso to Article 31A(1). The general rule in Article 300A has no such requirement.

Article 31A is the provision that still does most of the work on land and corporate property inside Part III, so it is worth reading in more detail.

What it protects. Clause (1) lists five kinds of law that cannot be declared void on the ground that they are inconsistent with, or take away or abridge, the rights in Article 14 or Article 19:

  1. (a) acquisition by the State of any estate or rights in it, or the extinguishment or modification of such rights;
  2. (b) the State taking over the management of any property for a limited period, in the public interest or to secure proper management;
  3. (c) the amalgamation of two or more corporations;
  4. (d) the extinguishment or modification of rights of managing agents, directors or managers of corporations, or of shareholders' voting rights;
  5. (e) the extinguishment, modification or premature termination of mining leases and licences.

A condition for State laws. Under the first proviso, a State law gets this protection only if it was reserved for the President's consideration and received the President's assent.

What counts as an "estate". Clause (2)(a) gives the word a wide meaning. Besides its meaning in local land-tenure law, it includes any jagir, inam or muafi or similar grant, land held under ryotwari settlement, and land held or let for agriculture or ancillary purposes, including waste land, forest land, pasture and sites of buildings occupied by cultivators, agricultural labourers and village artisans.

  1. "The right to property was abolished." False. It became a constitutional right under Article 300A.
  2. "Article 300A is in Part III." False. It is in Part XII, Chapter IV.
  3. "The 42nd Amendment removed it." False. It was the 44th Amendment (1978).
  4. "Only citizens are protected." False. Article 300A says "No person".
  5. "Article 300A guarantees market-value compensation." False. Only the second proviso to Article 31A(1) mentions market value, for a narrow class of land.

The other changes made by the 44th Amendment are listed on important amendments. The six freedoms that remain in Article 19 are on Article 19, and Part XII's place in the text is on parts of the Constitution.

  • 44th Amendment (1978), in force 20-6-1979: omitted Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 and inserted Article 300A.
  • Article 300A: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law."
  • Location: Part XII, Chapter IV.
  • Remedy: Article 226 or a civil suit, not Article 32.
  • Market-value compensation: only under the second proviso to Article 31A(1), for land under personal cultivation within the ceiling.
  • Minority institutions: Article 30(1A) protects them when their property is acquired.

Q5. Article 300A protects: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Any person

Is the right to property a Fundamental Right today?

No. Since 20 June 1979 it is a constitutional right under Article 300A in Part XII, not a Fundamental Right in Part III.

Does Article 300A require compensation?

Its text does not mention compensation. Deprivation needs the authority of law; market-value compensation is required only in the narrow case under Article 31A.
