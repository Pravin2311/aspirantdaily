Article 356 and President's Rule: Grounds, Approval, Duration and Limits

Grounds • What the President takes over • Two-month approval • Six months to three years • Clause (5) • Judicial review

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

Article 356 is the Constitution's provision for a failure of constitutional machinery in a State. When the President is satisfied that a State's government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution, the Union can take over that government for a limited period. In everyday and exam language this is called President's Rule, and sometimes State Emergency, although the Constitution itself does not use the word "emergency" here.

The article sits in Part XVIII, the emergency chapter, next to the National Emergency of Article 352 and the Financial Emergency of Article 360. It is the one most often used in practice, and also the one the Supreme Court has hedged most tightly, so questions test both its text and its limits.

Clause (1) lets the President act on a report from the Governor "or otherwise". The Governor's report is the usual trigger but not a legal requirement; the President may rely on other material. The test is whether the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution.

A second route comes from Article 365. If a State fails to comply with directions given by the Union in the exercise of its executive power, the President may treat that failure as a situation in which the State's government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution. Article 365 does not create a separate kind of President's Rule; it supplies a ground for Article 356.

The President acts on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers, as in other matters, so in practice the decision is the Union Government's.

Under a Proclamation the President may:

  • assume all or any of the functions of the State Government and the powers of the Governor or any other State authority, except the Legislature;
  • declare that the powers of the State Legislature will be exercised by or under the authority of Parliament;
  • make incidental provisions, including suspending provisions of the Constitution relating to any State body or authority.

There is one firm exception. The proviso to clause (1) forbids the President to assume any power of a High Court or to suspend any provision relating to High Courts. The State judiciary carries on unaffected.

In practice the Governor administers the State on the President's behalf, usually with advisers. Under Article 357, Parliament may in turn confer the State Legislature's law-making power on the President, which is how State budgets and laws are passed during President's Rule.

Every Proclamation must be laid before both Houses of Parliament. It ceases after two months unless both Houses approve it by resolution within that time. If the Lok Sabha has been dissolved and only the Rajya Sabha approves, the Proclamation lapses 30 days after the reconstituted Lok Sabha first sits, unless the Lok Sabha also approves it.

Once approved, a Proclamation stays in force for six months from the date it was issued. Parliament can extend it by fresh resolutions, six months at a time, but it can never remain in force for more than three years. The original 1950 text measured the six months differently, from the second approving resolution, which is why older books sometimes differ.

Extending beyond one year is harder. Clause (5) allows a resolution to continue the Proclamation past one year only if both of these hold:

  • a Proclamation of Emergency (under Article 352) is in operation; and
  • the Election Commission certifies that the continuance is necessary because of difficulties in holding elections to the State Legislative Assembly.

The present clause (5) was put in by the Forty-fourth Amendment (1978). A special proviso allowed the Punjab Proclamation of 11 May 1987 to run for up to five years, which is why Punjab is the standard exception in questions on maximum duration.

For decades the use of Article 356 was criticised as a way to dismiss opposition-ruled State governments. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994 by a nine-judge bench, the Supreme Court set out limits that still govern its use:

  • Judicial review: a Proclamation can be challenged in court. The court asks whether there was relevant material and whether the power was used in bad faith, though it does not judge whether the material was sufficient.
  • Floor test: whether a government has lost its majority should be tested on the floor of the Assembly, not judged from the Governor's private assessment.
  • No dissolution before approval: the Assembly should be kept in suspended animation, not dissolved, until both Houses of Parliament approve the Proclamation.
  • Restoration: if the court strikes a Proclamation down, it can restore the dismissed government and revive the Assembly.
  • Last resort: the power is for a breakdown of constitutional machinery, not for a mere breakdown of administration or a political disagreement.

The judgment also held that secularism is a basic feature of the Constitution, and that a State government acting against it could face action under Article 356.

Q1. Within what period must both Houses of Parliament approve a Proclamation under Article 356? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Two months

💡 Explanation: Article 356(3) says the Proclamation ceases to operate at the end of two months unless both Houses approve it. One month is the limit for a National Emergency under Article 352.

Q2. What is the maximum period for which President's Rule can normally remain in force in a State? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Three years

💡 Explanation: Article 356(4) caps a Proclamation at three years in all, through six-monthly extensions. Beyond one year, clause (5) conditions must also be met.

Q3. Which two conditions must both hold for Parliament to extend President's Rule beyond one year? medium

✅ Answer: (d) A Proclamation of Emergency in operation and an Election Commission certificate

💡 Explanation: Clause (5) requires a Proclamation of Emergency to be in operation and the Election Commission to certify that Assembly elections cannot be held.

Q4. Under Article 356, which of these powers can the President NOT assume? medium

✅ Answer: (a) Powers of the High Court

💡 Explanation: The proviso to clause (1) forbids the President from assuming any power vested in a High Court or suspending provisions relating to High Courts.

Q5. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, the Supreme Court held that a State Assembly should ordinarily not be dissolved: hard

✅ Answer: (b) before Parliament approves the Proclamation

💡 Explanation: Bommai held that irreversible steps such as dissolution should wait until both Houses approve the Proclamation; until then the Assembly can only be kept in suspended animation.

Does Parliament need a special majority to approve President's Rule?

No special majority is written into Article 356, so approval is by an ordinary resolution of each House. This contrasts with a National Emergency under Article 352, where clause (6) requires an absolute majority of each House's membership and two-thirds of members present and voting.

Is President's Rule the same as a State Emergency?

In exam language, yes. The Constitution itself does not use the word emergency for Article 356; it speaks of a failure of constitutional machinery in a State. Textbooks call it President's Rule or State Emergency to distinguish it from a National Emergency under Article 352.
