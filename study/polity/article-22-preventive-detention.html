Article 22 Explained: Rights on Arrest, Preventive Detention Safeguards and the Advisory Board

22(1) grounds and lawyer • 22(2) 24-hour rule • 22(3) exclusions • 22(4) Advisory Board • 22(5)-(6) representation • 22(7) Parliament • 44th Amendment

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-05

Article 22 is the longest Fundamental Right on personal liberty. It is headed "Protection against arrest and detention in certain cases". It deals with two very different situations.

  • Punitive arrest (clauses 1 and 2). A person is arrested because they are suspected of having committed an offence. The aim is to bring them to trial.
  • Preventive detention (clauses 3 to 7). A person is detained to prevent them from doing something in the future. No offence needs to have been committed and there is no trial.

The two kinds get very different safeguards, and that contrast is what exams test. A person under preventive detention loses the clause (1) and (2) protections and gets a separate, weaker set under clauses (4) to (7).

Article 22(1) says no arrested person shall be detained in custody "without being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds for such arrest". Nor can they be denied the right to consult, and to be defended by, "a legal practitioner of his choice".

Article 22(2) adds the 24-hour rule. Every person arrested and detained in custody must be produced before the nearest magistrate within twenty-four hours of the arrest. The time needed for the journey from the place of arrest to the magistrate's court is excluded. No one can be kept in custody beyond that period without a magistrate's authority.

These are rights of "any person", not only citizens. Article 22 does not limit them to Indians.

Article 22(3) switches off clauses (1) and (2) for two groups:

  1. Enemy aliens: any person who for the time being is an enemy alien.
  2. Preventive detainees: any person arrested or detained under any law providing for preventive detention.

This is the hinge of the Article. Once a detention is made under a preventive detention law, the right to a lawyer of choice and the 24-hour magistrate rule no longer apply. The detainee relies instead on clauses (4) to (7).

The three-month limit and the Advisory Board (22(4)). No preventive detention law can authorise detention for longer than three months unless an Advisory Board reports, before the three months end, that there is in its opinion sufficient cause for the detention. The Board must consist of persons who are, or have been, or are qualified to be appointed as, High Court judges. Even with the Board's approval, detention cannot go beyond the maximum period Parliament prescribes under clause (7)(b).

There is a second route under 22(4)(b). Longer detention is allowed without the Board if the person is detained under a law made by Parliament under clause (7)(a) and (b).

Grounds and representation (22(5)). The detaining authority must, as soon as may be, communicate to the detainee "the grounds on which the order has been made". It must also give the detainee "the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order".

Public-interest exception (22(6)). The authority does not have to disclose facts that it considers to be against the public interest to disclose. The grounds must be given, but the underlying facts can be held back.

Parliament's role (22(7)). Parliament may by law prescribe:

  • (a) the circumstances and classes of cases in which a person may be detained for longer than three months without an Advisory Board's opinion;
  • (b) the maximum period of detention in any class of cases;
  • (c) the procedure an Advisory Board follows in its inquiry.

This is a favourite trick question. The 44th Amendment (1978) tried to tighten preventive detention. Its section 3 substitutes a new clause (4), but the official text marks that substitution "date yet to be notified". Until the government notifies a date, the original clause (4) stays in force.

So the correct answer to "the maximum detention without an Advisory Board report under Article 22 as in force" is three months, not two. An option that says two months describes the un-notified version.

Article 22 is not one of the rights protected during an emergency. Under Article 359(1), as amended by the 44th Amendment, the President may suspend the right to move courts to enforce Part III rights "except articles 20 and 21". So Article 22 can be included in such an order, while Articles 20 and 21 cannot. See National Emergency and Article 20.

  • 22(1): grounds of arrest and a lawyer of choice.
  • 22(2): before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours, excluding journey time.
  • 22(3): clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to enemy aliens or preventive detainees.
  • 22(4): no detention beyond three months without an Advisory Board report, within Parliament's maximum period.
  • 22(5) and (6): grounds communicated and a chance to make a representation, but facts can be withheld in the public interest.
  • 22(7): Parliament fixes when the Board can be skipped, the maximum period and the Board's procedure.
  • 44th Amendment: its new clause (4), with a two-month limit, has not been notified.

Q1. Under Article 22(2), an arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within: easy

✅ Answer: (b) 24 hours, excluding journey time

💡 Explanation: Article 22(2): twenty-four hours, excluding the time needed for the journey to the magistrate's court.

Q2. Under Article 22 as in force, the maximum detention without an Advisory Board report is: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Three months

💡 Explanation: Three months under 22(4). The 44th Amendment's two-month version has not been notified.

Q3. Clauses 22(1) and 22(2) do NOT apply to: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Enemy aliens and preventive detainees

💡 Explanation: Article 22(3) excludes enemy aliens and persons detained under preventive detention laws.

Q4. Which right does a preventive detainee have under Article 22(5)? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Opportunity to make a representation against the order

💡 Explanation: 22(5) requires the grounds to be communicated and the earliest opportunity of representation. 22(6) allows facts to be withheld in the public interest.

Q5. Who may prescribe the maximum period of preventive detention? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Parliament

💡 Explanation: Article 22(7)(b) lets Parliament prescribe by law the maximum period of detention for any class of cases.

What is the difference between punitive and preventive detention?

Punitive detention follows a suspected offence and leads to trial. Preventive detention aims to stop a future act, needs no offence and has no trial, so Article 22 gives it different safeguards.

Is the two-month Advisory Board limit in force?

No. The 44th Amendment's new clause (4) with two months is marked 'date yet to be notified', so the original three-month limit applies.
