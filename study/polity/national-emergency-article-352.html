National Emergency Under Article 352: Grounds, Approval, Duration and Effects

War, external aggression, armed rebellion • Written Cabinet advice • Special majority • Six-month renewals • Revocation • Articles 358 and 359

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

Article 352 lets the President proclaim an emergency when the security of India, or of any part of its territory, is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion. It is the most powerful of the three emergency provisions in Part XVIII: it lets the Union take over legislative and executive control from the States and can restrict Fundamental Rights.

Because the power was misused during the 1975–77 Emergency, the 44th Amendment (1978) rewrote much of Article 352. Most exam questions test those safeguards, so this page reads the article clause by clause as it stands today.

India has seen three National Emergencies: in 1962 (the war with China), in 1971 (the war with Pakistan) and in 1975, when an emergency on the ground of "internal disturbance" was declared while the 1971 one was still in force.

  • Grounds: war, external aggression or armed rebellion. "Armed rebellion" replaced the vaguer "internal disturbance" in 1978, so unrest without an armed rebellion is no longer enough.
  • Before the event: an Explanation allows a Proclamation before actual war, aggression or rebellion if the President is satisfied there is imminent danger of it.
  • Area: it may cover the whole of India or only a part.
  • Cabinet decision in writing: clause (3) bars the President from issuing or varying a Proclamation unless the decision of the Union Cabinet, the Prime Minister and Cabinet-rank ministers, has been communicated in writing. Advice from the Prime Minister alone is not enough.
  • More than one: clause (9) lets the President issue different Proclamations on different grounds, even while another is in force.

Every Proclamation must be laid before both Houses. It ceases after one month unless both Houses approve it in that time. If the Lok Sabha has been dissolved and only the Rajya Sabha approves, the Proclamation lapses 30 days after the new Lok Sabha first sits unless that House also approves it.

Once approved, it lasts six months from the second approving resolution and can be extended by further resolutions for six months at a time, with no overall limit.

Each approving or extending resolution needs a special majority in each House: a majority of the House's total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting.

Ending an emergency is deliberately easier than continuing one:

  • the President may revoke it at any time by a later Proclamation;
  • the President must revoke it if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving it. No special majority is required for this resolution;
  • if at least one-tenth of the Lok Sabha's total membership gives written notice of such a resolution, a special sitting must be held within 14 days. Notice goes to the Speaker if the House is in session, and to the President if it is not.
  • Executive: under Article 353 the Union may give directions to any State on how to exercise its executive power.
  • Legislative: Parliament may make laws on subjects in the State List (Article 250). Such laws lapse six months after the emergency ends.
  • Finance: the President may modify the distribution of revenues between the Union and the States (Article 354).
  • Term of the Lok Sabha: Parliament may extend it by law, one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the Proclamation ends (Article 83(2)). State Assemblies can be extended in the same way.

The States are not dissolved. Their governments continue, but they work under the Union's overriding authority. The Constitution becomes, in effect, unitary for the duration.

Both articles stop protecting a law as soon as the emergency or order ends, although things done in the meantime are not undone. The exclusion of Articles 20 and 21 from Article 359 was the 44th Amendment's answer to the ADM Jabalpur judgment of 1976, which had denied detainees habeas corpus during the Emergency; see the five writs page.

  • ✘ "Armed rebellion suspends Article 19 automatically." Article 358 covers only war and external aggression.
  • ✘ "An emergency can last at most three years." That limit is for President's Rule; see Article 356.
  • ✘ "Revocation needs a special majority." A simple Lok Sabha resolution of disapproval is enough.
  • ✘ "The Prime Minister's advice is enough." Clause (3) needs the Cabinet's decision in writing.

Q1. Which of these is NOT a ground for a National Emergency under Article 352 today? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Internal disturbance

💡 Explanation: The 44th Amendment replaced 'internal disturbance' with 'armed rebellion'. The current grounds are war, external aggression and armed rebellion.

Q2. Within what period must Parliament approve a Proclamation of National Emergency? easy

✅ Answer: (a) One month

💡 Explanation: Article 352(4) says the Proclamation ceases after one month unless both Houses approve it. President's Rule under Article 356 has two months.

Q3. How can the Lok Sabha end a National Emergency? medium

✅ Answer: (b) By a resolution of disapproval, which obliges the President to revoke it

💡 Explanation: Under Article 352(7) the President must revoke the Proclamation if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving it. No special majority is prescribed for this resolution.

Q4. Article 19 is suspended automatically under Article 358 only when the emergency is declared on the ground of: medium

✅ Answer: (d) war or external aggression

💡 Explanation: Article 358 refers only to war or external aggression. An emergency for armed rebellion does not suspend Article 19 automatically.

Q5. What share of Lok Sabha members can require a special sitting to consider disapproving an emergency? hard

✅ Answer: (c) One-tenth

💡 Explanation: Article 352(8): written notice by not less than one-tenth of the Lok Sabha's total membership requires a special sitting within fourteen days.

Is there a maximum duration for a National Emergency?

No. Unlike President's Rule, which is capped at three years, a National Emergency can continue indefinitely as long as both Houses keep approving it by special majority every six months. It ends when it is revoked or when an extension is not approved.

Can a National Emergency cover only part of India?

Yes. Clause (1) allows the Proclamation to cover the whole of India or any part of its territory, so an emergency can be limited to the region where the threat exists.
