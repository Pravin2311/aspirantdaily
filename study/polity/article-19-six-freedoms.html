Article 19: The Six Freedoms and the Grounds for Restricting Each

19(1)(a) to (g) • Grounds in clauses (2) to (6) • Property removed in 1978 • Co-operative societies added in 2011 • Suspension under Article 358

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-31

Article 19(1) gives every citizen six freedoms. They are the core of personal liberty in the Constitution, and each comes with its own list of grounds on which the State may restrict it.

Sub-clauseFreedomRestrictions allowed by
19(1)(a)Speech and expressionClause (2)
19(1)(b)To assemble peaceably and without armsClause (3)
19(1)(c)To form associations or unions or co-operative societiesClause (4)
19(1)(d)To move freely throughout the territory of IndiaClause (5)
19(1)(e)To reside and settle in any part of IndiaClause (5)
19(1)(g)To practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or businessClause (6)

The lettering jumps from (e) to (g). Sub-clause (f), the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, was omitted by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978, with effect from 20 June 1979.

No freedom here is absolute. Each clause lets the State make laws imposing reasonable restrictions, but only on the grounds that clause names. That is why matching freedoms to grounds is the most tested part of Article 19.

ClauseApplies toGrounds
19(2)Speech and expressionSovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence
19(3)AssemblySovereignty and integrity of India; public order
19(4)AssociationsSovereignty and integrity of India; public order; morality
19(5)Movement and residenceInterests of the general public; protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe
19(6)Profession, trade and businessInterests of the general public; also allows laws on professional or technical qualifications, and State monopolies in any trade or service
  • ✘ "Public interest is a ground for restricting speech." It is not in clause (2); it appears in clauses (5) and (6).
  • ✘ "Friendly relations with foreign States" applies to assembly. It is only in clause (2).

Questions often describe a law and ask which clause permits it. The method is the same each time: identify which freedom the law limits, then check whether its purpose is one of the grounds in that freedom's clause.

  • A district magistrate bans a procession because it may lead to riots. The freedom limited is assembly, 19(1)(b). Clause (3) allows restrictions in the interests of public order, so the ban can be valid if it is reasonable.
  • A law makes it an offence to publish false statements that damage a person's reputation. This limits speech, 19(1)(a). Defamation is a listed ground in clause (2).
  • A State requires anyone practising law to hold a recognised degree and enrol with the Bar Council. This limits the freedom of profession, 19(1)(g). Clause (6)(i) expressly allows laws on professional or technical qualifications.
  • Outsiders are barred from buying land in a Scheduled Tribe area. This limits residence and settlement, 19(1)(e). Clause (5) allows restrictions to protect the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
  • The State runs all bus services on a route itself, excluding private operators. This limits trade and business, 19(1)(g). Clause (6)(ii) allows the State or a State corporation to carry on any trade or service, to the exclusion of citizens.

The word reasonable does real work. A restriction must have a listed purpose and be proportionate to it. Courts strike down restrictions that are excessive or arbitrary even when the ground is on the list, which is why exam answers say a law "can be valid" rather than "is valid".

  • First Amendment, 1951: substituted the present clause (2), with retrospective effect.
  • 16th Amendment, 1963: inserted "the sovereignty and integrity of India" into clauses (2), (3) and (4).
  • 44th Amendment, 1978: omitted sub-clause (f), the right to property.
  • 97th Amendment, 2011: added "or co-operative societies" to sub-clause (c).

Under Article 358, while a Proclamation of Emergency is in force on the ground that India's security is threatened by war or external aggression, nothing in Article 19 restricts the State's power to make laws or take executive action. Such laws stop having effect, to the extent of the conflict, when the Proclamation ends.

The text now names only war or external aggression, so an emergency declared on the ground of armed rebellion does not suspend Article 19 automatically. For the emergency itself, see National Emergency under Article 352.

Article 19 begins "All citizens shall have the right". Foreign nationals cannot claim it, even though they can claim rights such as Article 14 and Article 21 that are given to "any person". Who counts as a citizen is covered in Articles 5 to 11. For the other Fundamental Rights, see the Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Duties notes, and for the remedies when a right is violated, the five writs under Articles 32 and 226.

Q1. How many freedoms does Article 19(1) guarantee today? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Six

💡 Explanation: Sub-clauses (a) to (g) originally gave seven. Sub-clause (f), on property, was omitted by the 44th Amendment, leaving six.

Q2. Article 19 rights are available to: easy

✅ Answer: (b) citizens only

💡 Explanation: Article 19(1) begins 'All citizens shall have the right'. Foreigners cannot claim it.

Q3. Which of these is NOT a ground for restricting freedom of speech under Article 19(2)? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Interests of the general public

💡 Explanation: 'Interests of the general public' is a ground in clauses (5) and (6), for movement, residence and trade. It is not in clause (2).

Q4. The words 'or co-operative societies' were added to Article 19(1)(c) by the: medium

✅ Answer: (d) 97th Amendment

💡 Explanation: The Constitution (Ninety-seventh Amendment) Act, 2011 inserted them, with effect from 8 February 2012.

Q5. Article 358 suspends Article 19 only when an emergency is declared on the ground of: hard

✅ Answer: (b) war or external aggression

💡 Explanation: Since the 44th Amendment, Article 358 operates only for an emergency declared on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion.

Is the right to property still a Fundamental Right?

No. Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 were removed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. Property is now protected by Article 300A: no person can be deprived of property except by authority of law. Because Article 300A sits outside Part III, it is a constitutional right but not a Fundamental Right, so it cannot be enforced through Article 32.

How is Article 19 different from Article 21?

Article 19 gives citizens six specific freedoms, each open to restriction only on the grounds its own clause lists. Article 21 protects the life and personal liberty of any person, citizen or not, against deprivation except according to procedure established by law. The two often apply together: a law restricting movement must satisfy both.
