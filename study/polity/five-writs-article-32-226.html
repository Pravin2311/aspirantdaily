The Five Writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto

Article 32 vs 226 • Habeas corpus • Mandamus • Prohibition • Certiorari • Quo warranto • Emergency suspension

UPSC SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-29

A writ is a formal order from a superior court commanding someone to do, or stop doing, something. The Constitution gives this power to two levels of court:

  • Article 32 lets any person move the Supreme Court to enforce the Fundamental Rights in Part III. Clause (2) empowers the Court to issue directions, orders or writs, "including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari".
  • Article 226 gives every High Court the same kinds of writs, to any person or authority within its territory, for enforcing Part III rights "and for any other purpose".

Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, so the remedy is guaranteed, not merely available. Clause (4) adds that it cannot be suspended except as the Constitution provides, which in practice means a Presidential order under Article 359 during an emergency.

So the High Courts' power is wider in purpose but narrower in territory. Clause (4) of Article 226 adds that it does not reduce the Supreme Court's power under Article 32. Clause (3) also protects people against one-sided interim orders: if an order is made without hearing a party, the High Court must decide that party's application to vacate it within two weeks of receiving it (or of the copy being served, if later), or the order stands vacated.

Habeas corpus: "you may have the body"

Habeas corpus orders a person or authority that is detaining someone to produce the detainee before the court and justify the detention. If the detention is unlawful, the court orders release. It protects personal liberty under Article 21.

  • Lies against: public authorities and private persons, such as someone confining a family member.
  • Does not lie: where the person is held under a lawful order of a competent court, or where the detention is lawful under the procedure established by law.
  • Example: in Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983), a man kept in prison for years after his acquittal was not only released but awarded compensation, an early use of writ jurisdiction to compensate for a rights violation.

Mandamus: "we command"

Mandamus commands a public authority to perform a public duty that the law requires of it and that it has failed or refused to perform.

  • Lies against: public officers, government departments, local bodies, tribunals and courts below.
  • Does not lie: against private individuals or bodies; for a duty that is discretionary rather than mandatory; to enforce a purely contractual obligation; or against the President or a State Governor personally, who under Article 361 are not answerable to courts for their official acts.

These two writs both control courts and tribunals that exceed their jurisdiction or ignore natural justice. The difference is timing.

  • Prohibition is preventive. A higher court issues it to a lower court or tribunal while proceedings are still pending, ordering it to stop acting beyond its jurisdiction. Once a final decision has been made, prohibition is too late.
  • Certiorari is corrective. It calls up the record of a decision already made and quashes it for lack or excess of jurisdiction, an error apparent on the face of the record, or a breach of natural justice.

Traditionally both lay only against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies, never against the legislature. Indian courts have since extended certiorari to administrative orders that affect people's rights, so it now reaches many executive decisions as well. Neither writ is available against a private body.

A simple test: if the lower body has not yet decided, ask for prohibition; if it has decided, ask for certiorari. A petitioner can ask for both when the proceedings are partly complete.

Quo warranto, "by what authority", asks a person holding a public office to show the legal authority for holding it. If the appointment is invalid, the court can declare the office vacant.

  • Lies against: the holder of a substantive public office of a permanent character, created by the Constitution or by statute.
  • Does not lie: for private offices, or for offices held at the pleasure of the appointing authority.
  • Who may apply: any person, even one not personally harmed, because the public has an interest in lawful appointments. This is an exception to the usual rule that only an aggrieved person may seek a writ.

During the 1975–77 Emergency, the Supreme Court in ADM Jabalpur (1976) held that detainees could not seek habeas corpus while enforcement of Article 21 was suspended. The 44th Amendment then ensured that enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended under Article 359, and in K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) the Court expressly overruled ADM Jabalpur.

Q1. Which writ is used to quash an order already passed by a lower court or tribunal acting without jurisdiction? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Certiorari

💡 Explanation: Certiorari is corrective: it removes and quashes a decision already made. Prohibition is preventive and stops proceedings while they are still pending.

Q2. A writ of mandamus cannot be issued against: medium

✅ Answer: (a) the President of India for an official act

💡 Explanation: Article 361 protects the President and Governors from being answerable to courts for their official acts, so mandamus does not lie against them personally.

Q3. Which writ can be sought by any member of the public, even one not personally affected? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Quo warranto

💡 Explanation: Quo warranto tests a person's right to hold a public office. Because the public has an interest in lawful appointments, the usual personal-injury requirement is relaxed.

Q4. Why is the writ jurisdiction of High Courts said to be wider than that of the Supreme Court? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Article 226 also allows writs 'for any other purpose', not only for Fundamental Rights

💡 Explanation: Article 32 covers only Part III rights. Article 226 covers Part III rights and any other legal right, so its scope is wider, though its territorial reach is narrower.

Q5. During an emergency, the President cannot suspend the right to move a court for enforcing which Articles? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Articles 20 and 21

💡 Explanation: Article 359(1) allows the President to suspend enforcement of Part III rights by order, except the rights in Articles 20 and 21.

Is Article 32 itself a Fundamental Right?

Yes. Article 32 sits in Part III and its first clause guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights. That is why a person can go directly to the Supreme Court for a Part III violation without first approaching a High Court.

Can a writ be issued against a private company?

Usually not, because writs are public-law remedies against the State and its authorities. Habeas corpus is an exception and can be issued against a private person holding someone unlawfully. Courts have also issued writs to private bodies that perform public functions.
