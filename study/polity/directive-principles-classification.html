Classification of Directive Principles: Socialist, Gandhian and Liberal-Intellectual

Article 37: not enforceable • Socialist: 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47 • Gandhian: 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48 • Liberal: 44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-31

Part IV, Articles 36 to 51, lists the Directive Principles of State Policy: goals the State must keep in mind when making laws. Under Article 37 they are not enforceable by any court, yet they are "fundamental in the governance of the country", and the State has a duty to apply them in making laws.

The Constitution does not group them. Textbooks sort them by the ideology behind each one into three groups: socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual. Exams use this classification, so learn which Article sits where. A few Articles are placed differently by different authors, and those are flagged below.

These aim at economic and social justice and reducing inequality.

Articles 43 and 47 appear in two groups because each has two parts: the living wage and public health parts are socialist, while cottage industries and prohibition are Gandhian.

The classification is often asked together with a comparison of Part IV and Part III. The core difference is enforceability: a Fundamental Right can be taken to court, a Directive Principle cannot. But the two are designed to work together, and several Directive Principles have since been turned into enforceable rights or constitutional structures.

PointFundamental Rights (Part III)Directive Principles (Part IV)
Enforceable in courtYes, through Articles 32 and 226No, by Article 37
NatureLimits on what the State may doGoals the State must work towards
AimPolitical democracy and individual libertySocial and economic democracy
Addressed toMostly the State, sometimes private personsThe State, when making laws and policy

Three examples show how a directive can become something more concrete:

  • Article 40, village panchayats, was given constitutional shape by the 73rd Amendment, 1992, which inserted Part IX on Panchayats and the Eleventh Schedule.
  • Article 45, education: the 86th Amendment, 2002 inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen a Fundamental Right, and rewrote Article 45 to cover children under six.
  • Article 43B, co-operatives, came with the 97th Amendment, 2011, which also added co-operative societies to the freedom of association in Article 19(1)(c).

Q1. Which Article says the Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Article 37

💡 Explanation: Article 37 says they are not enforceable by any court but are fundamental in the governance of the country.

Q2. Organisation of village panchayats (Article 40) is usually classified as a: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Gandhian principle

💡 Explanation: Village self-government reflects Gandhi's ideas, so textbooks place Article 40 among the Gandhian principles.

Q3. Which Directive Principle was added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Article 48A: protection of environment

💡 Explanation: The 42nd Amendment inserted Articles 39A, 43A and 48A. Article 43B came in 2011; 44 and 50 are original.

Q4. 'Equal pay for equal work for both men and women' is found in: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Article 39(d)

💡 Explanation: Article 39(d) directs State policy towards equal pay for equal work for both men and women.

Q5. The present Article 45, on early childhood care and education until age six, was substituted by the: hard

✅ Answer: (c) 86th Amendment

💡 Explanation: The 86th Amendment Act, 2002 substituted Article 45, alongside inserting Article 21A on free education for ages 6 to 14.

Is the three-way classification in the Constitution?

No. The Constitution lists Articles 36 to 51 without grouping them. Socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual is the classification textbooks use, and some Articles, such as 43, 47 and 48, are placed in more than one group by different authors.

How are Directive Principles different from Fundamental Duties?

Directive Principles, in Part IV, are addressed to the State and tell it what goals to pursue in making laws. Fundamental Duties, in Part IVA (Article 51A), are addressed to citizens, such as the duty to respect the National Flag or to promote harmony. Both are outside Part III, so neither can be enforced through Article 32 the way a Fundamental Right can.

If Directive Principles cannot be enforced, why do they matter?

Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance and a duty of the State when making laws. Courts use them to interpret laws and to judge whether a restriction on a Fundamental Right is reasonable, and many laws, from land reform to free legal aid, were passed to implement them.
