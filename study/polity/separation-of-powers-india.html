Separation of Powers in India: Limited Separation, Constitutional Articles, Checks and Balances, and Judicial Independence

Limited separation • Why fragment power • Articles 50, 53, 79, 121, 122, 361 • Parliament's four checks • Judicial independence • Judicial review • Clashes • Exam traps

UPSC State PSC SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

NCERT's Class 11 text Indian Constitution at Work describes the Indian Constitution as based on "a delicate principle of limited separation of powers and checks and balances". Each organ of government has a clear area of functioning:

  • Parliament is supreme in making laws and amending the Constitution.
  • The executive is supreme in implementing them.
  • The judiciary is supreme in settling disputes and deciding whether laws accord with the Constitution.

The word limited matters. India does not keep the three organs in watertight compartments. In a parliamentary system, NCERT notes, the executive and legislature are interdependent: the legislature controls the executive and is in turn controlled by it.

NCERT's first chapter, "Constitution: Why and How", explains the logic. Constitutions are often subverted not by the people but by small groups who wish to enhance their own power. A well-crafted constitution fragments power so that no single institution acquires a monopoly.

The Indian Constitution, NCERT says, "horizontally fragments power" across the Legislature, Executive and Judiciary, and even across independent bodies like the Election Commission. If one institution tries to subvert the Constitution, the others can check it. NCERT credits this "intelligent system of checks and balances" with the Constitution's success.

Article 50 sits among the Directive Principles. It is a duty the State must work towards, not an enforceable right.

NCERT's Legislature chapter says Parliament ensures executive accountability at every stage: policy-making, implementation, and after implementation. It does so through four devices.

DeviceHow it works (NCERT)
Deliberation and discussionDebates on bills and general discussions. In Question Hour, held every day of a session, ministers answer searching questions
Approval or refusal of lawsA bill becomes law only with Parliament's approval. Even a majority government must bargain, especially without a Rajya Sabha majority
Financial controlThe legislature controls the purse strings: it can refuse funds, discuss why money is needed, and probe misuse using CAG and Public Accounts Committee reports
No-confidence motionThe most powerful weapon. After 1989 several governments were forced to resign after losing the support of coalition partners

NCERT also lists the limits. These checks need adequate time, interested members and willingness to compromise, yet sessions and debate time have declined. With a disciplined majority, the power to dismiss the government can be "fictional rather than real".

NCERT defines judicial independence in three parts:

  • the executive and legislature must not restrain the judiciary from doing justice;
  • they should not interfere with its decisions;
  • judges must be able to act without fear or favour.

It is protected in four ways:

  1. Tenure: judges have a fixed tenure until retirement, with a very difficult removal procedure.
  2. Money: their salaries and allowances are not subject to the legislature's approval.
  3. Contempt: courts can punish contempt, which protects judges from unfair criticism.
  4. Silence in Parliament: Parliament cannot discuss judges' conduct except during removal proceedings.

Independence is not arbitrariness. NCERT says the judiciary remains accountable to the Constitution, to democratic traditions and to the people.

NCERT calls judicial review perhaps the most important power of the Supreme Court. It is the power of the Supreme Court or a High Court to examine whether a law is constitutional and to declare an inconsistent law unconstitutional and inapplicable.

The term appears nowhere in the Constitution. NCERT explains that the power is implied by two things: India's written constitution, and the Court's power to strike down laws that go against fundamental rights. The Court has also brought areas once thought beyond review, such as the powers of the President and Governor, under judicial scrutiny.

NCERT notes that despite the clear division of power, conflict between Parliament and the judiciary has been a recurring theme. The main flashpoints it lists are:

  • the scope of the right to property;
  • how far Parliament can curtail fundamental rights;
  • the scope of Parliament's amending power;
  • the appointment of judges;
  • judges' salaries and allowances.

The amending-power dispute led to the basic structure doctrine.

  • India has a limited separation of powers. Strict separation, as in a presidential system, does not apply, because ministers sit in Parliament.
  • Article 50 is a Directive Principle about separating the judiciary from the executive. It does not cover the legislature.
  • The President is part of Parliament under Article 79 and also holds the Union's executive power under Article 53.
  • "Judicial review" is not written in the Constitution. It is implied.

Q1. Which Article directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Article 50

💡 Explanation: Article 50, a Directive Principle, says the State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services.

Q2. NCERT describes the Indian Constitution as based on: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Limited separation of powers with checks and balances

💡 Explanation: NCERT's Judiciary chapter says the Constitution rests on a delicate principle of limited separation of powers and checks and balances.

Q3. Parliament cannot discuss the conduct of a Supreme Court judge except on a removal motion under: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Article 121

💡 Explanation: Article 121 bars discussion in Parliament on judges' conduct except upon a motion for an address seeking removal.

Q4. Which Article bars courts from questioning Parliament's proceedings for irregularity of procedure? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Article 122

💡 Explanation: Article 122(1) says the validity of proceedings in Parliament shall not be questioned on grounds of alleged irregularity of procedure.

Q5. NCERT calls which device the most powerful weapon for executive accountability? medium

✅ Answer: (a) No-confidence motion

💡 Explanation: NCERT's Legislature chapter calls the no-confidence motion the most powerful weapon to ensure executive accountability.

Q6. The term 'judicial review' in the Indian Constitution is: hard

✅ Answer: (b) Not mentioned anywhere

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the term judicial review is nowhere mentioned in the Constitution; the power is implied.

Q7. Under Article 79, Parliament consists of: medium

✅ Answer: (c) The President and two Houses

💡 Explanation: Article 79 says Parliament shall consist of the President and two Houses, the Council of States and the House of the People.

Q8. Which Article protects the President from being answerable to any court for official acts? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Article 361

💡 Explanation: Article 361(1) says the President and Governors are not answerable to any court for exercising the powers and duties of office.

Does India follow strict separation of powers?

No. NCERT calls it a limited separation with checks and balances; ministers sit in Parliament and the legislature and executive are interdependent in the parliamentary system.

How does the judiciary check the other organs?

Through judicial review: courts can declare a law unconstitutional and inapplicable if it is inconsistent with the Constitution, a power NCERT says is implied rather than written.
