Judicial Review, Judicial Activism and Public Interest Litigation: Constitutional Basis, Early PIL Cases, Impact and Criticism

Judicial review defined • Articles 13, 32, 226 • Two grounds of review • Activism • PIL from 1979 • Early cases • Impact • Criticism • Exam traps

UPSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

Exams often pair these two terms. They are related, but they are not the same thing. Judicial review is a power that flows from the Constitution. Judicial activism is a description of a role the courts took up, mainly after 1979, through Public Interest Litigation.

PointJudicial reviewJudicial activism
What it isThe power to test a law against the Constitution and declare an inconsistent law unconstitutional and inapplicableA proactive approach in which courts go beyond cases brought by the aggrieved and direct the other organs
BasisImplied by the written Constitution, Article 13 and the writ powers under Articles 32 and 226Court practice, chiefly PIL (also called Social Action Litigation)
Who triggers itA party challenging a law or actionOften public-spirited citizens, groups or even newspaper reports and letters
Main riskConflict with Parliament over the scope of amendmentOverburdened courts and blurred lines between the organs

NCERT's Class 11 text Indian Constitution at Work calls judicial review perhaps the most important power of the Supreme Court. It defines it as the power of the Supreme Court, or the High Courts, to examine the constitutionality of any law. If the Court finds a law inconsistent with the Constitution, the law is declared unconstitutional and inapplicable.

The term itself appears nowhere in the Constitution. NCERT explains that the power is implied by two facts:

  • India has a written Constitution; and
  • the Supreme Court can strike down a law that goes against fundamental rights.

NCERT says the Constitution gives the Supreme Court two ways to remedy a violation of rights. It can restore rights by issuing writs such as habeas corpus and mandamus under Article 32, a power the High Courts also have under Article 226. It can also declare the offending law unconstitutional and non-operational under Article 13. The second route is judicial review.

ArticleWhat the text saysRole in review
13(2)The State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges Part III rights; a law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contraventionThe core basis for striking down laws
32(1)The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings to enforce Part III rights is guaranteedMakes the remedy itself a fundamental right
32(4)This right shall not be suspended except as the Constitution providesProtects the remedy
226(1)Every High Court can issue writs to enforce Part III rights "and for any other purpose"Wider writ reach than Article 32

NCERT says these two provisions together make the Supreme Court the protector of citizens' fundamental rights on one hand and the interpreter of the Constitution on the other. For the five writs in detail, see writs under Articles 32 and 226.

NCERT notes that review is not limited to rights. The Court can review a law on either of two grounds:

  1. Fundamental rights: the law abridges a Part III right.
  2. Federal distribution of powers: for example, if the Centre makes a law that some States say belongs to the State List, they can go to the Supreme Court, which can declare it unconstitutional.

The review power extends to laws passed by State legislatures too. NCERT concludes that the writ powers and the review power together make the judiciary very powerful.

In the normal course of law, NCERT explains, a person could approach the courts only if he or she had been personally aggrieved. That concept changed around 1979, when the Court heard a case filed not by the aggrieved persons but by others on their behalf. Because such cases involved an issue of public interest, they came to be called public interest litigations.

NCERT calls PIL, or Social Action Litigation (SAL), the chief instrument through which judicial activism has flourished in India, and "the most important vehicle of judicial activism". The judiciary, traditionally confined to responding to cases brought before it, began taking up cases merely on the basis of newspaper reports and postal complaints. That is why the term judicial activism became the popular description of its role.

CaseYearHow it began (NCERT)
Hussainara Khatoon vs. Bihar1979Newspaper reports showed many undertrial prisoners in Bihar had spent long years in jail, longer than the sentence for their alleged offence. An advocate filed a petition
Sunil Batra vs. Delhi Administration1980A Tihar inmate sent a scribbled note on torture of prisoners to Justice Krishna Iyer, who had it converted into a petition
Bandhua Mukti Morcha vs. Union of India1984Justice Bhagwati wrote that the problems of the poor need a different judicial approach from the adversarial procedure

NCERT adds that the Court later abandoned the practice of treating letters as petitions. Through PIL the courts also took up the blinding of jail inmates by police, inhuman working conditions in stone quarries and the sexual exploitation of children.

NCERT lists several effects:

  • Wider rights: clean air, unpolluted water and decent living came to be treated as rights for the entire society.
  • Access for the poor: courts allowed public-spirited citizens, social organisations and lawyers to file petitions on behalf of the needy and the deprived.
  • A democratised judiciary: groups, not just individuals, gained access to the courts.
  • Executive accountability: the Court directed the CBI to investigate politicians and bureaucrats in cases such as the hawala case.
  • Cleaner elections: candidates must file affidavits on assets, income and educational qualifications.

Review also widened. Areas once thought beyond it, such as the powers of the President and Governor, were brought under the courts' purview. And in the 1973 Kesavananda Bharati case the Court held that nobody, not even Parliament through amendment, can violate the basic structure.

NCERT also sets out a negative side. A large number of PILs has overburdened the courts. Activism has blurred the line between the judiciary and the other two organs, because the courts have taken up matters, such as reducing pollution, investigating corruption or electoral reform, that belong to the administration under legislative supervision. Some feel this makes the balance among the organs very delicate, and that activism "may be creating strains" on the principle that each organ respects the others' powers. See separation of powers in India.

NCERT also notes that PIL is spreading abroad: South Africa's constitution has put public interest litigation in its bill of rights.

  • "Judicial review" is not written in the Constitution. It is implied.
  • Review covers both fundamental-rights violations and breaches of the federal division of powers, and it applies to State laws.
  • Article 32 is itself a fundamental right. Article 226 lets High Courts issue writs "for any other purpose" as well.
  • Hussainara Khatoon (1979) concerned Bihar undertrials. Sunil Batra (1980) began with a prisoner's note from Tihar.
  • PIL is also called Social Action Litigation (SAL).

How is judicial review different from judicial activism?

Judicial review is a power: courts test a law against the Constitution and declare an inconsistent law unconstitutional. Judicial activism describes a proactive role, such as hearing cases on newspaper reports or letters and directing the executive, mainly through PIL.

Who can file a PIL?

NCERT explains that since around 1979 courts have let public-spirited citizens, social organisations and lawyers file petitions on behalf of the needy and deprived, instead of only the personally aggrieved person.
