Articles 1 to 4 Explained: Union of States, Territory of India and How Parliament Forms New States

Article 1 Union of States • Article 2 new States • Article 3 powers and proviso • Explanations I and II • Article 4 and Article 368

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-06

Part I of the Constitution is only four Articles long, but it settles three questions every exam asks about: what India is called, what its territory consists of, and who can redraw the map. The answer to the last question is Parliament, by an ordinary law, with the States consulted but not able to block the change.

Article 1(1) gives the country two official names in one sentence: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The word chosen is "Union", not "federation". Exam questions often ask what that word signals. The Constitution itself does not explain the choice, but the rest of Part I shows its practical effect: the States do not own their borders, and Parliament can change them.

Article 1(2) says the States and their territories are as specified in the First Schedule. This clause was substituted by the 7th Amendment (1956), the amendment that came with the reorganisation of States.

Article 1(3) separates two ideas that students often mix up:

  • The Union of States means the States alone.
  • The territory of India is wider. It comprises (a) the territories of the States, (b) the Union territories specified in the First Schedule, and (c) "such other territories as may be acquired".

So a Union territory is part of the territory of India but is not one of the States that make up the Union. Clause (3)(c) means any territory India later acquires becomes part of its territory automatically, without a constitutional amendment.

Article 2 is short: "Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit."

  • Admit refers to bringing in an area that is not already part of India as a State.
  • Establish refers to setting up a State that did not exist before.

Article 2 is about areas coming into the Union. Article 3 is about rearranging the States already inside it. Exams test this split: if the question concerns splitting or merging existing States, the answer is Article 3, not Article 2.

The old Article 2A, which associated Sikkim with the Union, was inserted by the 35th Amendment (1974) and omitted by the 36th Amendment (1975) with effect from 26 April 1975.

Under Article 3, Parliament may by law:

  1. (a) form a new State by separating territory from any State, by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
  2. (b) increase the area of any State;
  3. (c) diminish the area of any State;
  4. (d) alter the boundaries of any State;
  5. (e) alter the name of any State.

Explanation II, added by the 18th Amendment (1966), confirms that clause (a) includes forming a new State or Union territory by uniting part of a State or Union territory to another. Explanation I says that in clauses (a) to (e), "State" includes a Union territory. So Parliament can also reshape Union territories under Article 3.

The proviso, as substituted by the 5th Amendment (1955), adds two procedural conditions. A Bill under Article 3 cannot be introduced in either House of Parliament unless:

  1. Presidential recommendation: the President has recommended it; and
  2. Reference to the State Legislature: where the Bill affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President has referred it to that State's Legislature for its views, within a period the President specifies or extends, and that period has expired.

Three points follow directly from the text:

  • The State Legislature is asked only "for expressing its views". The text gives those views no binding force, so Parliament may go ahead even if the State objects.
  • Parliament only has to wait until the period expires. It does not need a reply.
  • Explanation I says that in the proviso, "State" does not include a Union territory. A Union territory's legislature, where one exists, has no right to be consulted under this proviso.

Creating a State changes the First Schedule, which lists the States, and the Fourth Schedule, which allocates Rajya Sabha seats. Normally a change to the text of the Constitution needs an amendment under Article 368. Article 4 removes that need.

  • Article 4(1): any law under Article 2 or 3 shall contain provisions to amend the First and Fourth Schedules as needed, and may contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions, including representation in Parliament and in the affected State Legislatures.
  • Article 4(2): "No such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368."

The practical result is that a reorganisation law passes like any ordinary law. Neither the special majority of Article 368(2) nor ratification by half the States is required. The amendment routes are compared on Article 368 amendment procedure, and the majorities on types of majority in Parliament.

  1. "A new State needs a constitutional amendment." False. Article 4(2) says the law is not an amendment for Article 368.
  2. "The State's consent is required." False. The proviso asks only for views and requires only that the period expire.
  3. "The Bill can be introduced by any member." False as a complete rule. It cannot be introduced without the President's recommendation.
  4. "Union territories must be consulted." False. In the proviso, "State" excludes Union territories.
  5. "Union of States and territory of India mean the same." False. The territory of India also includes Union territories and acquired territories.

How Article 3 affects the federal balance is discussed on federal and unitary features. The Schedules it amends are listed on schedules of the Constitution, and Part I's place in the whole text is on parts of the Constitution.

  • Article 1: India, that is Bharat, is a Union of States. The territory of India covers States, Union territories and acquired territories.
  • Article 2: Parliament admits or establishes new States.
  • Article 3: Parliament forms, enlarges, shrinks, re-borders or renames States.
  • Proviso: President's recommendation first, then reference to the affected State Legislature for views that do not bind.
  • Article 4: the law amends the First and Fourth Schedules and is not an Article 368 amendment.

Q1. Which Article says 'India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States'? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Article 1

💡 Explanation: Article 1(1) gives both names and describes India as a Union of States.

Q2. Forming a new State by separating territory from an existing State is done under: easy

✅ Answer: (c) Article 3

💡 Explanation: Article 3(a) covers formation by separation or union; Article 2 deals with admitting or establishing new States from outside.

Q3. Under the Article 3 proviso, the views of the affected State Legislature are: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Sought but not binding

💡 Explanation: The Bill is referred for expressing views within a period; Parliament may proceed once the period expires.

Q4. A law creating a new State under Article 3 is: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Not deemed an amendment under Article 368

💡 Explanation: Article 4(2) says such a law is not deemed an amendment for Article 368, so ordinary procedure applies.

Q5. In the proviso to Article 3, the word 'State': hard

✅ Answer: (b) Excludes Union territories

💡 Explanation: Explanation I: in clauses (a) to (e) State includes a Union territory, but not in the proviso.

Does Parliament need the consent of a State to change its boundaries?

No. The proviso to Article 3 only requires that the President refer the Bill to the State Legislature for its views and that the time allowed expire.

Why is creating a new State not a constitutional amendment?

Article 4(2) declares that a law under Article 2 or 3 is not deemed an amendment for Article 368, even though it changes the First and Fourth Schedules.
