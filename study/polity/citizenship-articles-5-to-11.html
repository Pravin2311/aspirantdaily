Citizenship Under Articles 5 to 11: Who Became a Citizen in 1950

Article 5 domicile tests • 19 July 1948 cut-off • 1 March 1947 • Persons of Indian origin • Voluntary foreign citizenship • Article 11

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-10-31

Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11, answers one question: who was a citizen of India on the day the Constitution began? It does not set out how anyone becomes or stops being a citizen after that. Those rules were left to Parliament, which is why Article 11 is the Article in this Part that still shapes citizenship law today.

Partition is the background. Millions had crossed between India and Pakistan, so Articles 6 and 7 deal with migrants in each direction, and Article 8 covers people of Indian origin living abroad.

7Migrants to PakistanNot a citizen if the person migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947, unless he returned under a permit for resettlement or permanent return.
8Persons of Indian origin abroadCitizen if registered by India's diplomatic or consular representative in the country of residence.
9Those who took foreign citizenshipNo citizenship under Articles 5, 6 or 8 for anyone who voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State.
10All the above citizensThey continue to be citizens, subject to any law made by Parliament.
11ParliamentNothing in Part II limits Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all other matters relating to citizenship.

Two dates appear in this Part, and exams like to swap them:

  • 19 July 1948 (Article 6): a migrant from Pakistan who came before this date only needed to have been ordinarily resident in India since migrating. One who came on or after it had to be registered as a citizen by an officer of the Government of the Dominion of India, after at least six months' residence.
  • 1 March 1947 (Article 7): a person who moved from India to Pakistan after this date lost the claim to Indian citizenship, unless he came back under a permit for resettlement or permanent return. Such a returnee is treated as a migrant after 19 July 1948 for Article 6.
  • ✘ "Article 6 used 15 August 1947 as the cut-off." The dates in the text are 19 July 1948 and 1 March 1947.
  • ✘ "Article 5 required ten years' residence." It required five.

Exam questions on Part II often describe a person and ask whether he or she was a citizen at the commencement. Work through the Articles in order: Article 5 first, then the special cases in Articles 6 to 8, then the bar in Article 9.

  • Born in Madras, living there in 1950. Domiciled in India and born in India, so a citizen under Article 5(a). No other Article is needed.
  • Born in Nepal to a mother born in Bombay, domiciled in India since childhood. Domicile plus a parent born in India satisfies Article 5(b), even though the person was born abroad.
  • Born in Lahore in 1920, moved to Delhi in January 1948 and stayed. Born in India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935, and migrated before 19 July 1948, so a citizen under Article 6 because he has been ordinarily resident in India since migrating. Had he arrived in, say, October 1948, he would also have needed to be registered.
  • Moved from Delhi to Karachi in May 1947 and stayed. Migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947, so not a citizen under Article 7, unless he later returned under a permit for resettlement or permanent return.

Two habits avoid most mistakes. First, check domicile before applying Article 5: birth alone was not enough. Second, Article 9 overrides Articles 5, 6 and 8 for anyone who had voluntarily taken another country's citizenship.

Citizenship is not only a question of history. Several provisions of the Constitution are available only to citizens, so whether a person is a citizen decides which protections he or she can claim.

Article 11 keeps Parliament's hands free: it can make any provision about acquiring and terminating citizenship. Parliament used this power to pass the Citizenship Act, 1955, which governs how citizenship is acquired and lost after the commencement.

Part II gives India a single citizenship: there is no separate State citizenship. That is one of the unitary features discussed in federal and unitary features of the Constitution. Several Fundamental Rights, including the six freedoms of Article 19, are available only to citizens, which is why this Part matters in practice. See also the Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Duties notes.

Q1. Article 5 deals with citizenship: easy

✅ Answer: (b) at the commencement of the Constitution

💡 Explanation: Article 5 decides who was a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution, using domicile plus birth, parentage or five years' ordinary residence.

Q2. Which Article empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship by law? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Article 11

💡 Explanation: Article 11 says nothing in Part II limits Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all other matters relating to citizenship.

Q3. For a migrant from Pakistan under Article 6, which cut-off date decided whether registration was needed? medium

✅ Answer: (c) 19 July 1948

💡 Explanation: Those who migrated before 19 July 1948 needed only ordinary residence since migration; those who came on or after it had to be registered.

Q4. Under Article 5(c), the period of ordinary residence required was at least: medium

✅ Answer: (c) five years

💡 Explanation: Article 5(c) required ordinary residence in India for not less than five years immediately before the commencement, along with domicile.

Q5. A person of Indian origin living abroad could become a citizen under Article 8 by: hard

✅ Answer: (b) registration with India's diplomatic or consular representative

💡 Explanation: Article 8 required registration by the Indian diplomatic or consular representative in the country of residence.

Does Part II say how a person becomes a citizen today?

No. Articles 5 to 8 only decide who was a citizen when the Constitution began. Acquisition and loss of citizenship after that are left to Parliament under Article 11, which acted through the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Which citizenship Articles came into force before 26 January 1950?

Articles 5 to 9. Article 394 brought them into force 'at once', that is on adoption on 26 November 1949, together with a few other provisions, so that the question of who was a citizen was settled before the rest of the Constitution began on 26 January 1950. Articles 10 and 11 began with the rest.
