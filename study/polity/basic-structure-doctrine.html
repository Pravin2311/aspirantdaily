Basic Structure Doctrine: From Shankari Prasad to Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills, What It Protects and Why

The amendment conflict • Kesavananda 1973 • What the ruling did • 42nd Amendment and Minerva Mills • Basic features list • Case timeline • Exam traps

UPSC State PSC SSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

The basic structure doctrine says that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368, but no amendment can destroy the Constitution's basic structure. The Supreme Court first laid this down in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973).

The Constitution itself never mentions a basic structure. NCERT's Class 11 text (Indian Constitution at Work, Chapter 9) is direct: the theory "has emerged from judicial interpretation". In effect the judiciary has amended the Constitution without any formal amendment.

NCERT traces the doctrine to a long dispute between Parliament and the courts. Soon after 1950, Parliament wanted to restrict the right to property so that land reforms could go ahead. The Court held that fundamental rights could not be restricted that way. When Parliament tried amending the Constitution instead, the Court said that even through an amendment a fundamental right cannot be abridged.

Between 1967 and 1973 the conflict became very serious. NCERT lists the laws at the centre of it:

  • land reform laws;
  • laws on preventive detention;
  • reservations in jobs;
  • the acquisition of private property, and the compensation paid for it.

The core question was simple. The government said Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution. The Court said Parliament could not make an amendment that violated Fundamental Rights.

Parliament responded with the 24th Amendment (1971), which added two clauses:

  • Article 13(4): nothing in Article 13 applies to an amendment made under Article 368.
  • Article 368(3): the same rule, stated from Article 368's side.

Together they meant a constitutional amendment would no longer count as "law" that could be struck down for violating fundamental rights.

NCERT's Judiciary chapter sums up three outcomes of the ruling:

  1. There is a basic structure of the Constitution, and nobody, not even Parliament through an amendment, can violate it.
  2. The right to property, the disputed issue, was not part of the basic structure, so it could be suitably abridged.
  3. The Court reserved to itself the right to decide what is part of the basic structure.

The case also struck down part of Article 31C. That part was a clause barring courts from questioning whether a law actually gave effect to the Directive Principles. The Constitution's own footnote records this, citing Kesavananda Bharati vs. State of Kerala, AIR 1973 SC 1461.

Chapter 9 adds what the ruling means for amendments in practice:

  • it sets specific limits on Parliament's power to amend;
  • it still allows Parliament to amend any and all parts of the Constitution within that limit;
  • it makes the judiciary the final authority on whether an amendment violates the basic structure.

The doctrine balances rigidity and flexibility. Certain core parts cannot be changed, which keeps the Constitution rigid there, while everything else can be amended.

NCERT calls the 38th, 39th and 42nd Amendments, passed during the 1975–77 Emergency, the most controversial. The 42nd Amendment (1976) was "an attempt to override the ruling of the Supreme Court given in the Kesavananda case". It changed the Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles, and added two clauses to Article 368:

  • 368(4): no amendment could be "called in question in any court on any ground".
  • 368(5): there would be "no limitation whatever" on Parliament's constituent power.

After the 1977 elections, the 43rd and 44th Amendments cancelled most of the changes made by the 38th, 39th and 42nd. The Court then repeated its Kesavananda stand in Minerva Mills (1980). The Constitution's footnotes record that Minerva Mills declared invalid both of these:

  • the section adding clauses (4) and (5) to Article 368;
  • the 42nd Amendment's widening of Article 31C to cover all the Directive Principles.

The 44th Amendment (1978) also removed the right to property from the Fundamental Rights and made it a legal right under Article 300A.

NCERT explains that the Court read the Constitution by its spirit rather than its letter. It saw the basic structure as "something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all".

Over time, governments, Parliament and political parties accepted the idea. When a commission to review the working of the Constitution was set up in 2000, the outcome showed how far that acceptance went:

  • the commission was chaired by retired Chief Justice Venkatachaliah;
  • it stuck to the theory of basic structure;
  • it suggested nothing that would endanger it.

What does the basic structure rule mean?

It is the rule, laid down in Kesavananda Bharati (1973), that Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution but cannot violate its basic structure, which the courts identify.

Is the basic structure defined in the Constitution?

No. NCERT stresses that the Constitution never mentions it; the Supreme Court developed it and has listed features case by case since 1973.
