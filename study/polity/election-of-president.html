Election of the President of India: Electoral College and How to Calculate the Value of a Vote

Articles 54 and 55 • 1971 census freeze • Single transferable vote • Disputes, Article 71

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

India's President is not elected directly by the people. Article 54 creates an electoral college made up of:

  • the elected members of both Houses of Parliament; and
  • the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States.

The Explanation to Article 54, added by the 70th Amendment, says that "State" here includes the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union territory of Puducherry. So MLAs of Delhi and Puducherry vote.

The word "elected" keeps others out. Nominated members of Parliament do not vote, and neither do members of State Legislative Councils, because Article 54 names only Legislative Assemblies.

Article 55 does not give every voter one vote. It aims for two things, stated in Article 55(1) and (2):

  • Uniformity among States. An MLA who represents more people should carry more weight.
  • Parity between the States and the Union. All MPs together should carry about the same weight as all MLAs together.

The population used is not the latest census. Under the Explanation to Article 55, "population" means the 1971 census until the figures of the first census taken after 2026 are published. The year 2026 was put in by the 84th Amendment, replacing 2000.

Article 55(2)(a) and (b) give the rule. In plain steps:

  • Divide the State's population by the number of elected MLAs.
  • Count how many whole thousands are in the result. That is the vote value.
  • If what is left over is 500 or more, add one.

As a formula: value of an MLA's vote = (State population ÷ elected MLAs) ÷ 1,000, rounded to the nearest whole number, with exactly 500 rounding up.

Worked example (invented figures). State A has a 1971 population of 4,000,000 and 60 elected MLAs.

  • 4,000,000 ÷ 60 = 66,666.67
  • Whole thousands: 66. Remainder: 666.67, which is at least 500.
  • Value of each MLA's vote = 66 + 1 = 67.
  • Total for State A = 67 × 60 = 4,020.

State B has a population of 2,500,000 and 40 MLAs. 2,500,000 ÷ 40 = 62,500. That is 62 thousands with a remainder of exactly 500, which is "not less than five hundred", so the value is 63. Total for State B = 63 × 40 = 2,520.

Article 55(2)(c) gives the rule for MPs. In plain steps:

  • Add up the total votes of all MLAs of all States.
  • Divide by the total number of elected members of both Houses of Parliament.
  • Round: a fraction exceeding one-half counts as one; any other fraction is dropped.

Worked example (continued). Suppose the country has only States A and B, and 31 elected MPs.

  • Total MLA votes = 4,020 + 2,520 = 6,540
  • 6,540 ÷ 31 = 210.97
  • The fraction 0.97 exceeds one-half, so the value of each MP's vote is 211.
  • Total for all MPs = 211 × 31 = 6,541, almost equal to the MLAs' 6,540. That is the parity Article 55 aims for.

Note the different rounding rules. For MLAs, a remainder of exactly 500 rounds up. For MPs, a fraction of exactly one-half is dropped, because it does not "exceed" one-half.

Article 55(3) says the election is held "in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote" and that voting "shall be by secret ballot".

In outline, each voter ranks the candidates in order of preference. A candidate needs more than half of the total value of valid votes to win. If no one reaches that on first preferences, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and those ballots pass to the next preference shown. This repeats until someone crosses the mark. The detailed counting rules are in the law Parliament has made under Article 71(3), not in the Constitution.

Q1. Which of these members vote in the election of the President? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Elected members of the Delhi Legislative Assembly

💡 Explanation: Article 54 includes only elected members of both Houses of Parliament and of State Legislative Assemblies, and its Explanation counts the NCT of Delhi and Puducherry as States.

Q2. A State has a population of 4,000,000 and 60 elected MLAs. What is the value of each MLA's vote? medium

✅ Answer: (b) 67

💡 Explanation: 4,000,000 ÷ 60 = 66,666.67. That has 66 multiples of 1,000 and a remainder of 666.67, which is at least 500, so the value is 66 + 1 = 67.

Q3. Which census is used for the President's election until figures of the first census after 2026 are published? easy

✅ Answer: (b) 1971

💡 Explanation: The proviso to the Explanation in Article 55 says the reference is to the 1971 census until then.

Q4. In the MP-vote calculation, a quotient of 210.5 is taken as: hard

✅ Answer: (a) 210

💡 Explanation: Article 55(2)(c) counts 'fractions exceeding one-half' as one and disregards other fractions. Exactly one-half does not exceed one-half, so 210.5 becomes 210.

Q5. A President dies in office. The election to fill the vacancy must be held within: easy

✅ Answer: (c) six months

💡 Explanation: Article 62(2): as soon as possible and in no case later than six months, and the new President gets a full five-year term.

Why do MLAs from different States have different vote values?

Article 55(2)(a) divides each State's population by its number of elected MLAs. States where each MLA represents more people get a higher vote value, which is what 'uniformity in the scale of representation' means.

Why does the total value of MPs' votes roughly equal that of all MLAs?

Article 55(2)(c) sets each MP's vote by dividing the total MLA votes by the number of elected MPs. This is the 'parity between the States as a whole and the Union' the Article aims for.
