Attorney-General (Article 76), Advocate-General (Article 165) and the Solicitor General Compared

Article 76 • Article 165 • Articles 88 and 177 • Article 105(4) • Article 139A • Solicitor General not in the Constitution

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-05

Exam questions on India's law officers usually mix up three titles: the Attorney-General for India, the Solicitor General of India and the Advocate-General of a State. Only two of them are created by the Constitution. The Attorney-General is in Article 76 (Part V, the Union executive) and the Advocate-General is in Article 165 (Part VI, the State executive). The Constitution never mentions a Solicitor General. It is a law office of the Government of India with no constitutional basis.

The rest of this page goes through each row against the text of the Constitution.

Article 76 has four clauses, and each one is a likely exam statement.

  1. Appointment (76(1)). "The President shall appoint a person who is qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court to be Attorney-General for India."
  2. Duties (76(2)). The Attorney-General advises the Government of India on legal matters referred by the President, performs other duties of a legal character assigned by the President, and discharges the functions given by the Constitution or any other law.
  3. Right of audience (76(3)). In the performance of duties, the Attorney-General "shall have right of audience in all courts in the territory of India". That covers every court, not only the Supreme Court.
  4. Tenure and pay (76(4)). The Attorney-General holds office "during the pleasure of the President" and receives such remuneration as the President may determine.

Notice what Article 76 does not say. It sets no fixed term, no retirement age and no removal procedure. Because the office is held at the President's pleasure, and the President acts on ministerial advice under Article 74, the Attorney-General can be replaced whenever the government chooses. This is very different from a Supreme Court judge, whose removal needs a special address by both Houses under Article 124(4).

Article 76 borrows its qualification from Article 124(3), which sets out who can be appointed a Supreme Court judge. The person must be a citizen of India and must satisfy one of three conditions:

  • have been a judge of a High Court (or of two or more High Courts in succession) for at least five years; or
  • have been an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more in succession) for at least ten years; or
  • be, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.

The Advocate-General's qualification is the High Court test in Article 217(2): a citizen of India who has held a judicial office in India for at least ten years, or has been an advocate of a High Court for at least ten years. There is no "distinguished jurist" route for the Advocate-General.

The age limits that apply to judges do not appear in Articles 76 or 165. Those Articles borrow only the qualifications, so the age limits do not apply to the law officers.

The Attorney-General is not a member of Parliament, yet Article 88 gives the office a place in it. Every Minister and the Attorney-General "shall have the right to speak in, and otherwise to take part in the proceedings of, either House, any joint sitting of the Houses, and any committee of Parliament of which he may be named a member". The same Article then adds that this does not entitle them to vote.

Article 105(4) completes the picture. The freedom of speech and the immunity from court proceedings that MPs enjoy under Article 105(1) to (3) also apply to persons who have a constitutional right to speak in a House of Parliament. So the Attorney-General gets parliamentary privileges while taking part, even without a vote.

A common exam statement reads: "The Attorney-General can take part in the proceedings of both Houses but cannot vote." That statement is correct. A statement that the Attorney-General is a member of the Cabinet is wrong. The Constitution places the office beside the Council of Ministers in Part V, not within it.

Article 165 is the State-level copy of Article 76, with three clauses instead of four.

  • 165(1): the Governor of each State appoints a person qualified to be a High Court judge.
  • 165(2): the Advocate-General advises the State Government on legal matters referred or assigned by the Governor, and discharges the functions given by the Constitution or any other law.
  • 165(3): the office is held during the pleasure of the Governor, with remuneration as the Governor may determine.

Article 165 has no clause matching 76(3), so the Constitution does not give the Advocate-General a right of audience in all courts in India. Article 177 gives the Advocate-General the same kind of legislative role the Attorney-General has under Article 88. The Advocate-General may speak in and take part in the proceedings of the Legislative Assembly (and the Legislative Council, where a State has one) and in any committee of which they are named a member, but "shall not, by virtue of this article, be entitled to vote".

Article 139A gives the Attorney-General a role in moving cases. When cases involving the same or substantially the same questions of law are pending before the Supreme Court and one or more High Courts, or before two or more High Courts, the Supreme Court may withdraw them and decide them itself. It can do so on its own motion, on an application by a party, or "on an application made by the Attorney-General of India", if it is satisfied that the questions are substantial questions of general importance.

Beyond Articles 76, 88, 105(4) and 139A, the Attorney-General's work comes from referrals by the President under Article 76(2) and from other laws. The President's advisory reference to the Supreme Court is a separate power under Article 143 and is covered on Supreme Court jurisdiction.

A search of the official text of the Constitution finds no mention of a Solicitor General. The office exists in practice as a law officer of the Government of India, but because it has no constitutional basis, no Article governs its appointment, qualifications, tenure or pay. In exams this produces a reliable trap: any option that says the Solicitor General is appointed "under Article 76" or is a "constitutional post" is wrong.

A few points to keep apart:

  • The Attorney-General is the only law officer named in Part V of the Constitution.
  • The Advocate-General is the only law officer named in Part VI.
  • Articles 88 and 177 name only the Attorney-General and the Advocate-General. So only these two have a constitutional right to take part in legislative proceedings.

For the full list of constitutional and statutory offices and bodies, see constitutional and statutory bodies. The executive context for both offices is on the Union and State executive.

  • Attorney-General: Article 76, Part V. Appointed by the President. Qualified to be a Supreme Court judge. Holds office at the President's pleasure.
  • Right of audience: in all courts in the territory of India (76(3)).
  • In Parliament: may speak and take part in both Houses, joint sittings and committees, but may not vote (Article 88). Has the same privileges as MPs while doing so (Article 105(4)).
  • Advocate-General: Article 165, Part VI. Appointed by the Governor. Qualified to be a High Court judge. Speaks in the State Legislature without a vote (Article 177).
  • Solicitor General: not in the Constitution.
  • Fixed term: none, for either constitutional office.

Q1. The Attorney-General for India is appointed under: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Article 76

💡 Explanation: Article 76. Article 165 is the Advocate-General of a State and Article 148 the CAG.

Q2. Which statement about the Attorney-General is correct? easy

✅ Answer: (c) Can take part in Parliament but cannot vote

💡 Explanation: Article 88 gives the right to speak and take part in either House and committees, but no vote. The office has no fixed term.

Q3. The Attorney-General must be qualified to be appointed as: easy

✅ Answer: (a) A judge of the Supreme Court

💡 Explanation: Article 76(1). The Advocate-General must be qualified to be a High Court judge.

Q4. Which law officer is NOT mentioned in the Constitution? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Solicitor General of India

💡 Explanation: The official text has no reference to a Solicitor General. The other two are in Articles 76 and 165.

Q5. Who has the right of audience in all courts in the territory of India? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Attorney-General

💡 Explanation: Article 76(3) gives this right to the Attorney-General in the performance of the office's duties.

What is the term of the Attorney-General of India?

The Constitution fixes no term. Under Article 76(4) the Attorney-General holds office during the pleasure of the President.

Is the Solicitor General a constitutional post?

No. Of the three law officers, the Constitution mentions only the Attorney-General (Article 76) and the Advocate-General of a State (Article 165).
