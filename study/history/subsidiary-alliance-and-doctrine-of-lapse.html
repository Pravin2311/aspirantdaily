Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse: How Each Worked, Who Used It, the States Taken Under Each, and How 1858 Ended Them

Residents after 1764 • Subsidiary alliance: no own army, pay for Company troops • Awadh 1801, Hyderabad, Mysore 1799 • Paramountcy under Lord Hastings • Doctrine of Lapse under Dalhousie: Satara to Jhansi • Awadh 1856 • 1858 reversal

Between 1757 and 1857 the East India Company rarely attacked a territory head-on. NCERT explains that it used political, economic and diplomatic methods to extend its influence first, and annexed a kingdom only later. Two of those methods are the subsidiary alliance and the doctrine of lapse. Both are favourite exam topics because the two are easy to mix up.

The first step came after the Battle of Buxar in 1764, when the Company began posting Residents in Indian states. A Resident was a political or commercial agent who served the Company's interests. Through Residents, Company officials interfered in the internal affairs of states: who would succeed to the throne and who would hold office. The subsidiary alliance went a step further.

Under a subsidiary alliance, an Indian ruler:

  • was not allowed an independent army;
  • was protected by the Company instead;
  • had to pay for the "subsidiary forces" the Company kept for that protection;
  • lost part of his territory as a penalty if he failed to pay.

NCERT's examples come from the time when Richard Wellesley was Governor-General (1798-1805). In 1801 the Nawab of Awadh had to hand over more than half his territory because he could not pay for the subsidiary forces, and Hyderabad was forced to cede territory on similar grounds.

Mysore shows the other route. The Company fought four wars against Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan. In the last, in 1799, Tipu was killed defending his capital Shrirangapatnam. Mysore was handed back to the old ruling family, the Wodeyars, and a subsidiary alliance was imposed on it.

The alliance left the ruler on his throne but hollow: no army of his own, a heavy bill to pay, and a Company force inside his state.

From the early nineteenth century the Company became openly aggressive. Under Lord Hastings (Governor-General 1813-1823) it adopted the policy of paramountcy. It claimed that its authority was supreme, greater than that of any Indian state, and that it was justified in annexing, or threatening to annex, any kingdom to protect its interests.

This did not go unchallenged. When the British tried to annex the small state of Kitoor in present-day Karnataka, Rani Channamma took up arms. She was arrested in 1824 and died in prison in 1829; Rayanna, a chowkidar of Sangoli, carried on the fight until he was hanged in 1830.

The last wave of annexations came under Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856. His Doctrine of Lapse declared that if an Indian ruler died without a male heir, the kingdom would "lapse", that is, become Company territory.

To remember the order, take the initials S, S, U, N, J: Satara and Sambalpur first, then Udaipur, Nagpur and finally Jhansi, the most famous case. The five annexations fall within six years, 1848 to 1854.

In practice adopted sons were not accepted as heirs. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi asked the Company to recognise her adopted son after her husband's death, and Nana Saheb, the adopted son of Peshwa Baji Rao II, asked for his father's pension. The Company refused both, and both later led the Revolt of 1857.

Awadh is the trap. It was taken over in 1856, but not by lapse. The British said they were "obliged by duty" to free its people from the Nawab's "misgovernment". The humiliating removal of the Nawab drove many people of Awadh into the great revolt of 1857.

Taken together, these methods worked. NCERT notes that by 1857 the Company ruled directly over about 63 per cent of the territory and 78 per cent of the population of the subcontinent, and influenced the rest indirectly.

The Revolt of 1857 ended this policy. From 1858 the rulers were promised that their territory would never be annexed and that they could pass their kingdoms to heirs, including adopted sons, as long as they accepted the British Queen as Sovereign Paramount. For what came before, see the Battles of Plassey and Buxar; for what followed, see the Revolt of 1857.

Q1. Under a subsidiary alliance, an Indian ruler: easy

✅ Answer: (b) Could not keep an independent army and paid for Company troops

💡 Explanation: The ruler gave up his independent armed forces, was protected by the Company, and had to pay for the subsidiary forces stationed for that protection.

Q2. Which Governor-General devised the Doctrine of Lapse? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Lord Dalhousie

💡 Explanation: Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856, devised the doctrine and annexed several states with it.

Q3. Which of these states was NOT annexed under the Doctrine of Lapse, according to NCERT's list? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Awadh

💡 Explanation: Awadh was taken over in 1856 on the ground of 'misgovernment', not lapse. Satara, Sambalpur, Udaipur, Nagpur and Jhansi were annexed by lapse.

Q4. The Nawab of Awadh had to give over half his territory to the Company in 1801 because: medium

✅ Answer: (a) He failed to pay for the subsidiary forces

💡 Explanation: Under Wellesley, Awadh failed to pay for the subsidiary forces, so the Company took over half its territory as a penalty.

Q5. The policy of 'paramountcy' was begun under: medium

✅ Answer: (b) Lord Hastings

💡 Explanation: Under Lord Hastings (1813-1823) the Company claimed supreme authority over Indian states and the right to annex any of them.

What is the main difference between the subsidiary alliance and the Doctrine of Lapse?

The subsidiary alliance kept a ruler on the throne but took away his army and made him pay for Company troops, with territory taken if he defaulted. The Doctrine of Lapse took the whole kingdom when a ruler died without a natural male heir.

Was Awadh annexed under the Doctrine of Lapse?

No. Awadh came under a subsidiary alliance in 1801 and was annexed in 1856 on the claim that the Nawab misgoverned it. This is a common exam trap.

When did the Doctrine of Lapse end?

After the Revolt of 1857. From 1858 rulers were assured that their territory would never be annexed and that adopted sons could inherit, though they had to accept the British Queen as Sovereign Paramount.
