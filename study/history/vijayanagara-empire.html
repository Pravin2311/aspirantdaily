Vijayanagara Empire: The Four Dynasties, Krishnadeva Raya, the Amara-Nayaka System and Hampi

Founded 1336 by Harihara and Bukka • Sangama → Saluva → Tuluva → Aravidu • Krishnadeva Raya 1509-29, Amuktamalyada • Talikota 1565 • Hampi rediscovered 1800

UPSC SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Vijayanagara, "city of victory", was the name of both a city and an empire. Founded in the fourteenth century, the empire at its height ran from the river Krishna in the north to the far south of the peninsula. The city was sacked in 1565 and abandoned. People in the Krishna-Tungabhadra doab remembered the ruins as Hampi, after the local mother goddess Pampadevi.

The rulers called themselves rayas. Popular tradition named three rival powers by their strength: the Gajapatis of Orissa were lords of elephants, the Deccan Sultans ashvapati (lords of horses) and the rayas narapati (lords of men).

Power was always contested. Members of the ruling lineage and military commanders both made claims, which is how the Saluvas, themselves commanders, came to power.

Krishnadeva Raya of the Tuluva dynasty is the most famous ruler of Vijayanagara. NCERT describes his reign as one of expansion and consolidation:

  • 1512: took the Raichur doab, the land between the Tungabhadra and the Krishna.
  • 1514: subdued the rulers of Orissa.
  • 1520: inflicted severe defeats on the Sultan of Bijapur.
  • Building: built fine temples and added impressive gopurams to many south Indian temples. He built the hall in front of the main shrine of the Virupaksha temple to mark his accession.
  • Nagalapuram: a suburban township near the capital, named after his mother.
  • Writing: the Amuktamalyada, a work on statecraft in Telugu. In it he advises the king to improve harbours and to keep merchants who import elephants and good horses loyal, so the animals never reach his enemies.
  • Diplomacy: he backed some claimants to power in the Sultanates and was proud of the title "establisher of the Yavana kingdom".

Despite constant military readiness, NCERT says the kingdom flourished in unparalleled peace and prosperity under him. After his death in 1529, rebellious nayakas (military chiefs) troubled his successors.

Warfare depended on cavalry, so rival kingdoms needed horses imported from Arabia and Central Asia. Arab traders controlled this trade at first, with local horse merchants called kudirai chettis. From 1498 the Portuguese arrived on the west coast, and their muskets made them important players.

Relations with the Deccan Sultanates were not always hostile. The Sultan of Bijapur even helped settle succession disputes after Krishnadeva Raya died. But Rama Raya, the chief minister, tried to play one Sultan against another. The Sultans combined against him, and in 1565 his army was routed at Rakshasi-Tangadi (also called Talikota) by the armies of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar and Golconda. They sacked the city, and it was totally abandoned within a few years.

The nayakas were military chiefs who controlled forts and had armed followers. They usually spoke Telugu or Kannada. Many accepted Vijayanagara's authority but often rebelled.

The amara-nayakas were military commanders given territories to govern by the raya:

  • They collected taxes and dues from peasants, craftspeople and traders.
  • They kept part of the revenue for themselves and to maintain a fixed contingent of horses and elephants. This gave the kings the fighting force that brought the whole southern peninsula under their control.
  • Some revenue went to temples and irrigation works.
  • They sent tribute every year and appeared at court with gifts. Kings sometimes transferred them to assert control.

In the seventeenth century many nayakas set up independent kingdoms, which hastened the empire's collapse. NCERT names Chandragiri, Madurai, Ikkeri, Thanjavur and Mysore as centres of nayaka power.

  • Fortifications: Abdur Razzaq, an ambassador from Persia, counted seven lines of forts. They enclosed farmland and forests as well as the city. The walls used no mortar; wedge-shaped stone blocks held them in place.
  • Water: the region is one of the most arid in the peninsula. The Kamalapuram tank (early fifteenth century) fed fields and the royal centre. The Hiriya canal drew water from the Tungabhadra.
  • Royal centre: in the south-west of the settlement, it held over 60 temples, the mahanavami dibba (a platform about 40 feet high on a base of about 11,000 sq ft), the Lotus Mahal and the Hazara Rama temple, which has Ramayana panels and was probably for the royal family only.
  • Sacred centre: on the rocky northern bank of the Tungabhadra. The kings ruled on behalf of the god Virupaksha, a form of Shiva, and signed orders "Shri Virupaksha". They also used the title "Hindu Suratrana" (Hindu Sultan).
  • Temples: huge raya gopurams (royal gateways) that dwarfed the central towers, plus mandapas and pillared corridors. The Vitthala temple honours a form of Vishnu worshipped in Maharashtra and has a shrine shaped like a chariot.

Rediscovery: Colonel Colin Mackenzie of the East India Company brought the ruins to light in 1800 and made the first survey map. Epigraphists were collecting inscriptions by 1836, and photographers recorded the monuments from 1856. Hampi was recognised as a site of national importance in 1976, and NCERT's timeline lists its declaration as a UNESCO World Heritage site (1986).

Who founded the Vijayanagara Empire?

Two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, in 1336, according to tradition and inscriptions. Their line is known as the Sangama dynasty, which ruled until 1485.

Why is Krishnadeva Raya famous?

He was the best-known Tuluva ruler (1509-29). He took the Raichur doab, subdued Orissa, defeated Bijapur, added gopurams to temples and wrote the Amuktamalyada in Telugu.

What was the amara-nayaka system?

Military commanders received territories from the raya, collected taxes there and kept horses and elephants for his army. They sent annual tribute, but many later became independent rulers.
