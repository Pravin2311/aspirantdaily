Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari Systems: Who Introduced Each, Where, Who Paid the Revenue, and Why Each Ran Into Trouble

Diwani 1765 and the 1770 famine • Permanent Settlement 1793: zamindars, fixed revenue • Mahalwari 1822: village headman, revised demand • Ryotwari: direct with the ryot • Why all three hurt cultivators

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

On 12 August 1765 the Mughal emperor appointed the East India Company Diwan of Bengal. As Diwan, it became the chief financial administrator of the territory and now had to organise land revenue, the main source of income in the countryside.

At first the Company behaved like a trader. It wanted as much revenue as possible and cheap cotton and silk, but set up no regular system of assessment. Artisans deserted villages because they were forced to sell to the Company at low prices, and peasants could not pay the dues demanded. Then in 1770 a terrible famine killed ten million people in Bengal, about one-third of the population.

Company officials concluded that agriculture had to improve if revenue was to be secure. After two decades of debate they chose the first of three systems. Exam questions on the permanent settlement, ryotwari and mahalwari systems almost always ask the same four things: where, when, who introduced it, and who paid.

The Permanent Settlement was introduced in 1793, when Cornwallis was Governor-General of Bengal. Under it:

  • rajas and taluqdars were recognised as zamindars;
  • zamindars collected rent from the peasants and paid revenue to the Company;
  • the revenue was fixed permanently and was never to be raised.

The idea was that a fixed demand would give the Company a steady income and encourage zamindars to invest in the land, since any extra output would be theirs.

It did not work out. The fixed revenue was so high that zamindars struggled to pay, and those who failed lost their zamindaris, many of which were auctioned. Later, when prices and cultivation rose, zamindars earned more but the Company gained nothing, because it could not raise a permanently fixed demand. Zamindars still did not invest; they simply let out land to tenants. For the cultivator, rent was high and his right to the land insecure. He borrowed from moneylenders to pay, and was evicted when he could not.

By the early nineteenth century officials wanted a system whose demand could be raised. In the North Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency, most of which is now Uttar Pradesh, Holt Mackenzie devised a new system that came into effect in 1822.

Mackenzie saw the village as an important institution of north Indian society. Collectors went from village to village, inspecting and measuring fields and recording the rights of different groups. The estimated revenue of each plot was added up to give the revenue each village, or mahal, had to pay. Two features set it apart:

  • the demand was to be revised periodically, not fixed for ever;
  • the village headman, not a zamindar, collected the revenue and paid it to the Company.

In revenue records a mahal is a revenue estate, which may be one village or a group of villages.

In the south the Company also moved away from the Permanent Settlement. The ryotwari (or ryotwar) system was first tried on a small scale by Captain Alexander Read in areas taken after the wars with Tipu Sultan. It was then developed by Thomas Munro and gradually extended over south India.

Read and Munro argued that the south had no traditional zamindars, so the settlement had to be made directly with the cultivators, the ryots who had tilled the land for generations. Each ryot's fields were surveyed separately before the revenue was assessed. Munro believed the British should act as paternal father figures protecting the ryots.

The hopes did not last. Officials, eager to raise income, set the demand too high. Peasants could not pay, ryots fled the countryside, and villages were deserted in many regions.

Revenue was not the only thing the British wanted from the land. NCERT notes that they also pushed cultivators to grow crops Europe needed: jute in Bengal, tea in Assam, sugarcane in the United Provinces, wheat in Punjab, cotton in Maharashtra and Punjab, and rice in Madras, besides opium and indigo.

A quick way to remember who paid: Permanent → landlord, Mahal → village, Ryot → cultivator. The Diwani that started it all is covered under the Battles of Plassey and Buxar. Peasant anger over revenue demands was one of the causes of the Revolt of 1857.

Q1. Under the Permanent Settlement of 1793, who paid the land revenue to the Company? easy

✅ Answer: (b) The zamindars

💡 Explanation: Rajas and taluqdars were recognised as zamindars. They collected rent from peasants and paid a permanently fixed revenue to the Company.

Q2. Who devised the mahalwari system, which came into effect in 1822? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Holt Mackenzie

💡 Explanation: Holt Mackenzie devised it for the North Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency, most of which is now Uttar Pradesh.

Q3. In which system was the settlement made directly with the cultivators? easy

✅ Answer: (a) Ryotwari

💡 Explanation: Read and Munro argued that the south had no traditional zamindars, so the ryotwari settlement was made directly with the ryots after surveying their fields.

Q4. Why did the Company gain nothing when prices and cultivation rose in Bengal in the early nineteenth century? medium

✅ Answer: (b) The revenue demand had been fixed permanently

💡 Explanation: Under the Permanent Settlement the demand could never be raised, so the extra income went to the zamindars, not the Company.

Q5. Which of these is true of the mahalwari system but NOT of the Permanent Settlement? medium

✅ Answer: (c) The demand was to be revised periodically

💡 Explanation: The mahal's demand was revised periodically and collected by the village headman. The Permanent Settlement fixed the demand for ever and used zamindars.

Is the Permanent Settlement the same as the zamindari system?

In exam usage, yes. The Permanent Settlement recognised rajas and taluqdars as zamindars who collected rent and paid a fixed revenue, which is why it is often called the zamindari system.

What does 'mahal' mean?

In British revenue records a mahal is a revenue estate, which may be a village or a group of villages. The mahalwari system assessed and collected revenue mahal by mahal.

Did any of the three systems help the peasants?

Not as intended. NCERT says the Permanent Settlement left cultivators with high rent and insecure rights, and under the later systems the demand was set so high that ryots fled and villages were deserted.
