Quit India Movement 1942: The War, the Cripps Mission, 'Do or Die', the Underground Resistance and the Parallel Governments

War breaks out, September 1939 • Ministries resign, October 1939 • Individual satyagraha 1940-41 • Cripps Mission, spring 1942 • Quit India, August 1942 • 'Do or die' • Satara prati sarkar • Over 90,000 arrested by end-1943

UPSC SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

The Government of India Act of 1935 promised some form of representative government, and the Congress won the provincial elections of 1937 and formed ministries in most provinces. Two years later, in September 1939, the Second World War broke out.

Gandhi and Nehru had been strongly critical of Hitler and the Nazis. They offered Congress support for the war effort if Britain promised to grant India independence once the war ended. The offer was refused, and the Congress ministries resigned in October 1939. Through 1940 and 1941 the Congress ran a series of individual satyagrahas to press the British to promise freedom after the war.

Meanwhile, in March 1940, the Muslim League passed a resolution demanding a measure of autonomy for the Muslim-majority areas of the subcontinent. NCERT's Class XII book says politics had become a three-way struggle between the Congress, the League and the British.

Britain had an all-party government. Its Labour members were sympathetic to Indian aims, but the Conservative Prime Minister, Winston Churchill, was a diehard imperialist. In the spring of 1942 Churchill was persuaded to send one of his ministers, Sir Stafford Cripps, to India to seek a compromise with Gandhi and the Congress.

The talks broke down over defence. The Congress insisted that if it was to help the British defend India from the Axis powers, the Viceroy must first appoint an Indian as the Defence Member of his Executive Council. Britain would not agree, and the mission failed.

After the failure of the Cripps Mission, Gandhi decided to launch his third major movement against British rule (after Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience). The "Quit India" campaign began in August 1942, in the middle of the war.

The British must quit India immediately, Gandhi told them. To the people he said "do or die" in your effort to fight the British, but you must fight non-violently.

Gandhi and the other leaders were jailed at once, but the movement spread anyway. It especially drew in peasants and the youth, many of whom left their studies and went to jail. Demonstrators clashed with the police everywhere, and communications and symbols of state authority were attacked across the country.

With the senior leaders in prison, younger activists organised strikes and acts of sabotage all over India. Socialist members of the Congress were particularly active in the underground resistance, among them Jayaprakash Narayan.

In several districts, such as Satara in the west and Medinipur in the east, "independent" governments were proclaimed.

NCERT ties Satara's movement to the non-Brahman movement in Maharashtra, which had opposed caste and landlordism since the late nineteenth century and had linked up with the national movement by the 1930s.

The first British response was severe repression. By the end of 1943, over 90,000 people were arrested and around 1,000 killed in police firing; in many areas orders were given to machine-gun crowds from airplanes. It took the British more than a year to suppress the rebellion.

Local accounts show the cost. On 25 August 1942, nineteen people died in police firing at Paparandi in Nabarangpur, Orissa; more than a thousand were jailed in Koraput district, and the tribal leader Veer Lakhan Nayak was hanged.

  • A mass movement: NCERT's Class XII book calls Quit India genuinely a mass movement that drew in hundreds of thousands of ordinary Indians.
  • The League's rise: while the Congress leaders were in jail, Jinnah and the League expanded their influence and began to make a mark in Punjab and Sind, where they had scarcely been present.
  • Release: Gandhi was freed in June 1944, with the end of the war in sight, and held talks with Jinnah to bridge the gap between the Congress and the League.

Alongside, Subhas Chandra Bose, who had secretly left his Calcutta home in January 1941, raised the Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj) from Singapore. Its 1944 attempt to enter India through Imphal and Kohima failed. The INA members were imprisoned and tried, and people from all walks of life joined the protests against the INA trials.

What followed was the road to freedom. In 1945 a Labour government came to power in Britain and committed itself to granting independence to India, and the Viceroy, Lord Wavell, brought the Congress and the League together for a series of talks. Quit India was the last of Gandhi's three great mass campaigns before independence in 1947. For the earlier campaigns, see Civil Disobedience and the Dandi March and modern India and the freedom struggle.

✅ Answer: (a) Britain refused to promise independence after the war in return for support

💡 Explanation: The Congress offered support for the war if Britain promised independence once the war ended; the offer was refused and the ministries resigned in protest.

What was the slogan of the Quit India Movement?

Gandhi told the British to quit India immediately and told the people to 'do or die' in their effort to fight the British, but to fight non-violently.

Who led the movement once Gandhi was jailed?

Gandhi and other leaders were jailed at once. Younger activists organised strikes and sabotage, and socialist Congress members such as Jayaprakash Narayan were especially active underground.

Did the Quit India Movement succeed?

It was crushed: it took the British more than a year, with over 90,000 arrests by the end of 1943. NCERT's Class VIII book still says the rebellion ultimately brought the Raj to its knees.
