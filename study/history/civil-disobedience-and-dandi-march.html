Civil Disobedience Movement and the Dandi March: Background, the Salt Satyagraha, Its Spread and the Gandhi-Irwin Pact

Simon Commission 1928 • Purna Swaraj, Lahore, December 1929 • Eleven demands, 31 January 1930 • Sabarmati to Dandi, 12 March to 6 April • 78 volunteers • Gandhi-Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931 • Second Round Table • Poona Pact 1932

UPSC SSC Railway State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

Two forces shaped Indian politics in the late 1920s, NCERT says. The first was the world economic depression: agricultural prices began to fall from 1926 and collapsed after 1930, so peasants could not sell their harvests or pay revenue. By 1930 the countryside was in turmoil.

The second was the Simon Commission, set up by the new Tory government in Britain under Sir John Simon to review the constitutional system. It did not have a single Indian member. When it arrived in 1928, it was greeted with the slogan "Go back Simon", and both the Congress and the Muslim League joined the protests. Lala Lajpat Rai was assaulted by the police during one such demonstration and died of his injuries. In the same year Vallabhbhai Patel led the successful Bardoli Satyagraha against higher land revenue.

In October 1929 the viceroy, Lord Irwin, made a vague offer of "dominion status" at some unspecified time and a Round Table Conference. It did not satisfy the Congress. In December 1929 the Lahore Congress, under Jawaharlal Nehru, formalised the demand for Purna Swaraj, or complete independence, and declared 26 January 1930 Independence Day. The celebrations drew little attention, so Gandhi looked for a way to tie the idea of freedom to everyday issues.

On 31 January 1930 Gandhi sent Irwin a letter with eleven demands, some of general interest and some for particular classes, from industrialists to peasants, so that everyone could identify with the campaign. The most stirring was the demand to abolish the salt tax.

Salt was eaten by rich and poor alike. People were forbidden to make it even for home use and had to buy it at a high price. Gandhi said the tax and the government's monopoly over salt production revealed the most oppressive face of British rule. In writing quoted by NCERT's Class XII book he called the monopoly a "fourfold curse", noting that the government even destroyed salt it could not sell profitably.

The letter was in effect an ultimatum: if the demands were not met by 11 March, the Congress would launch civil disobedience. Irwin would not negotiate.

At every stop Gandhi explained what he meant by swaraj and urged people to defy the British peacefully. Parallel salt marches took place in other parts of the country. The Dandi march 1930 began the Civil Disobedience Movement.

The new movement went further than Non-Cooperation. In 1921-22 people had refused to cooperate; now they were asked to break colonial laws as well. Thousands made salt and demonstrated at government salt factories. Foreign cloth was boycotted and liquor shops picketed. Peasants refused revenue and chaukidari taxes, village officials resigned, and forest people entered Reserved Forests to collect wood and graze cattle.

The government arrested Congress leaders one by one. When Abdul Ghaffar Khan was arrested in April 1930, crowds in Peshawar faced armoured cars and police firing. A month later, after Gandhi's own arrest, workers in Sholapur attacked police posts, municipal buildings, courts and railway stations. Repression followed and about 100,000 people were arrested.

  • Rich peasants such as the Patidars of Gujarat and the Jats of UP joined against high revenue, but many stayed away in 1932 because revenue rates were not revised.
  • Business groups, organised in FICCI (1927) under leaders such as Purshottamdas Thakurdas and G. D. Birla, gave money and refused to trade in imported goods at first.
  • Women joined marches, made salt, picketed shops and went to jail in large numbers.
  • Industrial workers mostly stayed aloof except in the Nagpur region, and the Congress did not adopt their demands.

Gandhi called off the movement and signed a pact with Irwin on 5 March 1931. Under the Gandhi-Irwin Pact he agreed to attend a Round Table Conference in London (the Congress had boycotted the first), and the government agreed to release political prisoners.

Gandhi went to London in December 1931 for the second conference, but the talks broke down. Back home he found a new round of repression: Ghaffar Khan and Nehru were in jail and the Congress was declared illegal. He relaunched the movement in 1932; it went on for over a year but lost momentum by 1934.

At the second conference Dr B.R. Ambedkar, who had founded the Depressed Classes Association in 1930, demanded separate electorates for dalits. When the British conceded, Gandhi began a fast unto death. The result was the Poona Pact of September 1932: reserved seats for the Depressed Classes, elected by the general electorate.

✅ Answer: (d) Release of political prisoners

Q4. Which arrest in April 1930 led to demonstrations in Peshawar? medium

✅ Answer: (a) Abdul Ghaffar Khan

💡 Explanation: Angry crowds faced armoured cars and police firing in Peshawar after Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a devout disciple of Gandhi, was arrested.

Q5. How did the Poona Pact of 1932 settle representation for the Depressed Classes? medium

✅ Answer: (b) Reserved seats, elected by the general electorate

💡 Explanation: After Gandhi's fast, Ambedkar accepted reserved seats in the provincial and central councils, filled by the general electorate rather than a separate one.

Why did Gandhi choose salt?

Salt was eaten by rich and poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food. The state monopoly over making and selling it was deeply unpopular, so a protest against it could unite all classes.

How was Civil Disobedience different from Non-Cooperation?

In 1921-22 people were asked to refuse cooperation with the British. In 1930 they were asked to break colonial laws as well, starting with the salt law.

Did all groups join the movement?

No. Industrial workers mostly stayed aloof except in the Nagpur region, dalit participation was limited, and large sections of Muslims, alienated from the Congress after the mid-1920s, did not respond to the call.
