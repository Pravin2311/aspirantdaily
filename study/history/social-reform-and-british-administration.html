📖 Social Reforms & British Administration

Reform Movements • Reformers • Governors-General & Viceroys • Land Revenue Systems • Education Policy

High Yield History 📅 Last updated: Oct 2026
  • The 19th century saw a wave of socio-religious reform movements that attacked sati, child marriage, caste discrimination and the ban on widow remarriage, and spread modern education. Many historians see them as the intellectual roots of Indian nationalism.
  • In parallel, the British built a centralised administration through a line of Governors-General (and, after 1858, Viceroys), each tied in exams to specific laws and events.
  • This page pairs both topics because questions often combine them — for example, Raja Ram Mohan Roy's campaign and Lord William Bentinck's 1829 ban on sati.

Reformers and the Laws They Inspired

  • Sati abolished — Bengal Regulation XVII of 1829, under Bentinck, after Ram Mohan Roy's campaign.
  • Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856 — after the campaign of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar; drafted under Dalhousie, passed under Canning.
  • Native Marriage Act, 1872 — civil marriage, promoted by Keshab Chandra Sen, who split from the original Samaj in 1866.
  • Age of Consent Act, 1891 — after campaigns by Behramji Malabari.
  • Sarda Act, 1929 (Child Marriage Restraint Act) — fixed minimum marriage ages at 14 for girls and 18 for boys.
  • Savitribai Phule and Jyotiba Phule opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848.

Education Policy

  • Charter Act, 1813 — set aside ₹1 lakh a year for education, the first such commitment.
  • Macaulay's Minute (1835) — backed English-medium education; adopted by Bentinck. It aimed at a class "Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste".
  • Wood's Despatch (1854) — the "Magna Carta of English education in India"; led to the universities of 1857.
  • Hunter Commission (1882) — primary and secondary education; Raleigh Commission (1902) — led to the Universities Act, 1904; Sadler Commission (1917) — Calcutta University.

Q1. Satyarth Prakash was written by:

✅ Answer: (d) Swami Dayananda Saraswati

💡 Explanation: Dayananda Saraswati, founder of the Arya Samaj (1875), wrote Satyarth Prakash.

Q2. The Subsidiary Alliance system was introduced by:

✅ Answer: (a) Lord Wellesley

💡 Explanation: Wellesley introduced it in 1798; Hyderabad was the first state to accept it.

Q3. The Ryotwari system was associated with:

✅ Answer: (b) Thomas Munro

💡 Explanation: Thomas Munro and Alexander Read introduced Ryotwari in Madras; Holt Mackenzie is linked with Mahalwari.

Q4. Which Viceroy is known as the father of local self-government in India?

✅ Answer: (c) Lord Ripon

💡 Explanation: Ripon's resolution of 1882 laid the basis for local self-government.

Q5. Who founded the Satyashodhak Samaj in 1873?

✅ Answer: (d) Jyotiba Phule

💡 Explanation: Jyotiba Phule founded it in Pune to fight caste discrimination; he also wrote Gulamgiri.

📌 Prelims Traps to Avoid

  • ✘ Bentinck was the first Governor-General of India; Warren Hastings was the first of Bengal.
  • ✘ The Theosophical Society was founded in New York, not in India.
  • ✘ The Widow Remarriage Act was passed under Canning (July 1856), though drafted under Dalhousie.

Who was the first Governor-General of India?

Lord William Bentinck became the first Governor-General of India under the Charter Act of 1833. Warren Hastings had earlier been the first Governor-General of Bengal under the Regulating Act of 1773.

What is the difference between the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems?

Under the Permanent Settlement (1793, Bengal) zamindars paid a revenue fixed forever. Under Ryotwari (Madras, Bombay) individual cultivators paid the state directly. Under Mahalwari (North-Western Provinces, Punjab) the village community as a whole was responsible for revenue.
