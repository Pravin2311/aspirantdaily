Sufi Movement in India: Silsilas, Khanqahs, the Chishti Saints and Their Dargahs

Silsila = chain of master and disciple • Chishti: from Chisht, Afghanistan • Qadiri: after Abd'ul Qadir Jilani • Suhrawardi: Delhi Sultans • Naqshbandi: Mughals

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

In the early centuries of Islam, some religious-minded people turned to asceticism and mysticism in protest against the growing materialism of the Caliphate. These were the sufis. They criticised the theologians' dogmatic reading of the Qur'an and the sunna (traditions of the Prophet). They sought salvation through intense devotion and love for God and by following the example of the Prophet Muhammad. They read the Qur'an through personal experience.

"Sufism" is an English word coined in the nineteenth century. Islamic texts call it tasawwuf. Scholars derive the term from suf (wool, for the coarse woollen clothes sufis wore), from safa (purity), or from suffa, the platform outside the Prophet's mosque where close followers gathered.

By the eleventh century Sufism had become a well-developed movement with its own literature. Its institutions use a set of terms that exam questions test directly:

New members swore an oath of allegiance, wore a patched garment and shaved their heads. Outside the khanqahs, radical mystics such as the Qalandars, Madaris, Malangs and Haidaris lived as celibate mendicants and ignored rituals. Because they defied the shari'a they were called be-shari'a, as against the ba-shari'a sufis who followed it.

Of the sufi groups that came to India in the late twelfth century, the Chishtis were the most influential. They adapted to the local environment and took on features of Indian devotional traditions. The order is named after the town of Chisht in central Afghanistan.

  • Khwaja Muinuddin's dargah at Ajmer is the most revered Chishti shrine. Its earliest textual mentions date to the fourteenth century. Akbar visited it fourteen times, until 1580, gave a huge cauldron (degh) in 1568 to cook for pilgrims, and built a mosque in its compound.
  • Nizamuddin Auliya's khanqah stood on the Yamuna at Ghiyaspur, then outside Delhi. It had small rooms and a big hall, the jama'at khana. Soldiers, slaves, singers, merchants, poets, Hindu jogis and qalandars all came. Visitors included Amir Hasan Sijzi, Amir Khusrau and the historian Ziyauddin Barani. His disciples called him sultan-ul-mashaikh, "Sultan amongst shaikhs".
  • Local practices: bowing before the shaikh, offering water to visitors, shaving the heads of initiates and yogic exercises all show local traditions being absorbed.
  • Shaikh Salim Chishti, a descendant of Baba Farid, has his dargah at Fatehpur Sikri, Akbar's capital. It symbolised the bond between the Chishtis and the Mughal state.

Sama' was central to the Chishtis. Amir Khusrau (1253-1325), poet, musician and disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya, introduced the qaul (Arabic, "saying"), a hymn sung at the opening or close of a qawwali. Sufi poetry in Persian, Hindavi or Urdu followed it. Qawwals at Nizamuddin Auliya's shrine still begin with the qaul.

  • Chishtis in Delhi spoke Hindavi, the language of the people.
  • Baba Farid's verses in the local language are in the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat tells the love of Padmini and Ratansen, king of Chittor, as an allegory of the soul's journey to God.
  • Around Bijapur, Chishti sufis of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries composed short poems in Dakhani, a variant of Urdu, that women sang while grinding grain or spinning.

Rulers needed the sufis. The Sultans set up charitable trusts (auqaf) for hospices and granted tax-free land (inam). Because most of their subjects were not Muslims, the Sultans resisted the ulama's demand to make the shari'a state law, and sought legitimacy from sufis instead. Kings often wanted their tombs near sufi shrines. There was friction too: both sides expected rituals such as prostration and kissing of the feet.

Sources: historians use four kinds of sufi texts. These are manuals such as Hujwiri's Kashf-ul-Mahjub; malfuzat (conversations), such as the Fawa'id-al-Fu'ad of Nizamuddin Auliya, compiled by Amir Hasan Sijzi; maktubat (letters); and tazkiras (biographies). The fourteenth-century Siyar-ul-Auliya of Mir Khwurd Kirmani was the first tazkira written in India.

What is the difference between a khanqah and a dargah?

A khanqah is the hospice where a living shaikh taught and his disciples lived. A dargah, meaning court, is the shaikh's tomb-shrine after his death, the focus of pilgrimage or ziyarat.

Which was the most influential Sufi order in India?

The Chishti order. NCERT says the Chishtis, who came in the late twelfth century, adapted to local conditions and adopted features of Indian devotional traditions, such as sama' and local languages.

What is urs?

The death anniversary of a sufi saint, when pilgrims gather at his dargah. The word also means marriage, signifying the union of the saint's soul with God.
