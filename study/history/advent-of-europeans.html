Advent of Europeans in India: Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes and French, Their Arrival, Factories and Rivalry

Order of arrival • 1498 sea route • 1600 charter • Hugli 1651 to Kalikata • Trade with arms • Standard dates table • Wandiwash 1760

UPSC SSC Railways State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-07

The standard exam sequence is Portuguese → Dutch → English → Danes → French. NCERT's Class 8 history text (Our Pasts III, Chapter 2, "From Trade to Territory") sets out the core of this story.

  • The Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama discovered the sea route to India in 1498.
  • By the time the first English ships reached the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese were already established on India's western coast, with their base at Goa.
  • By the early seventeenth century the Dutch were exploring trade in the Indian Ocean, and soon the French traders arrived.

NCERT's Class 12 text (Theme Seven, on Vijayanagara) adds the Portuguese side of the story. From 1498 the Portuguese arrived on the west coast and tried to set up trading and military stations. Their superior military technology, especially muskets, made them important players in the region's politics.

Charter of 1600: the Company got a charter from Queen Elizabeth I giving it the sole right to trade with the East. No other English trading group could compete with it. NCERT explains why this mattered: mercantile companies of the time made profit mainly by excluding competition, so that they could buy cheap and sell dear.

The race for the same goods: all the European companies wanted the same things:

  • fine cotton and silk;
  • pepper, cloves, cardamom and cinnamon.

Competition pushed up purchase prices and cut profits, so each company tried to eliminate its rivals. Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries they sank each other's ships, blockaded routes and fortified their trading posts. As NCERT puts it, trade was carried on with arms.

NCERT's sequence for Bengal:

YearEvent
1651First English factory set up on the banks of the river Hugli. The Company's traders were known as "factors".
1696The Company began building a fort around the settlement.
Two years later (1698)Zamindari rights over three villages, one of them Kalikata, which grew into Calcutta (Kolkata).
—Aurangzeb's farman granted the Company the right to trade duty free.

A factory here was not a manufacturing unit. It had a warehouse where goods for export were stored, and offices where Company officials sat.

The farman's duty-free right applied only to the Company. Company officials doing private trade on the side were still expected to pay duty, but they refused. This caused Bengal a huge loss of revenue and led to protests from Nawab Murshid Quli Khan. That friction ran all the way to Plassey in 1757.

Trade brought writers as well as merchants. NCERT's Class 12 chapter on travellers (Theme Five) traces the sequence.

Portuguese writers: after the Portuguese arrived around 1500, several wrote detailed accounts of Indian social customs and religious practices. The Jesuit Roberto Nobili even translated Indian texts into European languages. The best known is Duarte Barbosa, who described trade and society in south India.

After 1600: growing numbers of Dutch, English and French travellers came to India.

  • The French jeweller Jean-Baptiste Tavernier visited at least six times. He was especially interested in trading conditions.
  • The Italian doctor Manucci never returned to Europe and settled in India.
  • The Frenchman François Bernier spent twelve years in India, from 1656 to 1668. He was physician to Prince Dara Shukoh, Shah Jahan's eldest son.

Their accounts are among the main European sources for the period in which the trading companies were gaining ground.

The last serious European rival to the English was France. Their struggle in south India ran through three Carnatic Wars in the mid-eighteenth century. The French governor Dupleix tried to build influence by backing rival claimants to local thrones. The English answered in kind, with Robert Clive rising to prominence in these conflicts.

English victory at Wandiwash (1760) ended French ambitions of empire in India. Pondicherry stayed French, but only as a trading enclave. NCERT's Class 8 chapter notes that Tipu Sultan of Mysore developed a close relationship with the French in India and modernised his army with their help.

  • The English charter (1600) predates the Dutch VOC (1602), but the Dutch became active in Indian waters early. The arrival order in India is usually given as Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes, French.
  • Vasco da Gama discovered the sea route in 1498. Goa came later.
  • Hugli (1651) was the first English factory in Bengal, not the first in India. Exam guides list Surat as the first major English factory.
  • A "factory" was a warehouse-cum-office, not a manufacturing unit.

What was the order of arrival of Europeans in India?

The usual exam answer is Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danes and French. NCERT confirms the Portuguese came first, with Vasco da Gama's sea route in 1498.

Why did European companies fight each other in India?

NCERT explains they all wanted the same goods, so competition raised prices and cut profits; companies sank rivals' ships, blockaded routes and fortified trading posts.
