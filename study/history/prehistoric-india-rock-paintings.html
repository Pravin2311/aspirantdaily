Prehistoric Rock Paintings of India: Bhimbetka's Periods, Colours and Themes, Lakhudiyar and the Neolithic Sites of the South

First Indian find 1867-68 (Carlleyle) • Bhimbetka: 45 km south of Bhopal, about 800 shelters, 500 painted, found by V.S. Wakankar in 1957-58 • Haematite red, chalcedony green • Lakhudiyar • Kupgallu, Piklihal, Tekkalkota

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-01

Prehistory is the distant past before paper, language or writing, when no books or documents existed. Scholars have rebuilt it from excavated tools, pottery, bones and cave drawings. Painting and drawing were among the oldest ways people expressed themselves, and cave walls were their canvas.

The early prehistoric period is called the Old Stone Age or Palaeolithic Age. It is not known whether Lower Palaeolithic people made any art. By the Upper Palaeolithic, artistic activity flourished around the world, and India's earliest paintings come from this time. Their subjects were human figures, human activities, geometric designs and animal symbols.

Discovery: the archaeologist Archibold Carlleyle made the first discovery of rock paintings in India in 1867-68. That was 12 years before the famous cave of Altamira in Spain was found. Cockburn, Anderson, Mitra and Ghosh later found many more sites.

Lakhudiyar in detail: it lies about 20 km along the Almora-Barechina road. Humans are drawn as stick figures. The main animals are a long-snouted animal, a fox and a many-legged lizard, alongside wavy lines, rectangles filled with designs, and groups of dots. The paintings overlap in layers: black first, then red ochre, and white last.

The Vindhya hills were ideal for Stone Age people. They are full of Palaeolithic and Mesolithic remains, along with forests, wild plants, fruits and streams. Bhimbetka's themes range from daily life to sacred and royal images: hunting, dancing, music, horse and elephant riders, animal fights, honey collection, body decoration and household scenes.

Period I: Upper Palaeolithic. These are linear drawings in green and dark red of huge animals, such as bison, elephants, tigers, rhinos and boars, alongside stick-like humans. A few are wash paintings, but most are filled with geometric patterns. The green figures are dancers and the red ones are hunters.

Period II: Mesolithic. This period has the largest number of paintings. They are smaller, and hunting scenes predominate: groups hunting with barbed spears, pointed sticks, bows and arrows, traps and snares. The hunters wear simple clothes and ornaments, sometimes with elaborate head-dresses or masks. Animals are many: elephant, bison, tiger, boar, deer, antelope, leopard, rhinoceros, fish, frog, lizard, squirrel and birds. Animals were painted naturalistically, but humans in a stylised way.

Mesolithic paintings also show women, children playing, community dances, people gathering fruit and honey, and women grinding and preparing food, which together suggest family life. Many shelters also have hand prints, fist prints and dots made with fingertips.

Period III: Chalcolithic comes next, followed by four later periods. NCERT's chapter covers only the first three in detail.

The artists used shades of white, yellow, orange, red ochre, purple, brown, green and black, but white and red were their favourites. Minerals were ground to powder and mixed with water and a sticky binder such as animal fat, gum or tree resin. Brushes were made of plant fibre. NCERT says the colours are believed to have survived because of a chemical reaction with oxides on the rock surface.

Paintings appear on the walls and ceilings of shelters. Some are in living spaces, but others are in places that may have had religious importance. Some of the finest are high up near the ceiling, perhaps so that they could be seen from a distance.

Why they matter: these paintings show how early humans lived, what they ate, what they did each day and how they thought. NCERT calls them the greatest wealth that the people of this period left behind. For later art, see Indian dance, music and painting and the Indus Valley and Vedic age.

Q1. Who discovered the Bhimbetka caves? easy

✅ Answer: (c) V.S. Wakankar

💡 Explanation: NCERT credits V.S. Wakankar with discovering the Bhimbetka caves in 1957-58. Carlleyle made the first Indian rock-art find in 1867-68.

Q2. In Bhimbetka paintings, the red colour came from: medium

✅ Answer: (a) Haematite (geru)

💡 Explanation: Red came from haematite, known as geru in India. Green came from chalcedony, and white may have come from limestone.

Q3. Which period has the largest number of paintings at Bhimbetka? medium

✅ Answer: (d) Mesolithic

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the largest number belong to Period II, the Mesolithic. These paintings are smaller, and hunting scenes predominate.

Q4. Lakhudiyar rock shelters are in which state? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Uttarakhand

💡 Explanation: Lakhudiyar is on the Suyal river in the Kumaon hills of Uttarakhand, about 20 km along the Almora-Barechina road.

Q5. Kupgallu, Piklihal and Tekkalkota are known for paintings on: medium

✅ Answer: (c) Granite rocks

💡 Explanation: NCERT says the granite rocks of Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh gave Neolithic painters their canvas; these three are the best-known sites.

Why did prehistoric people paint the same spot again and again?

NCERT offers possible reasons: the artist may not have liked the earlier work, the spot may have been sacred or special, or different generations used the same place. Some Bhimbetka shelters have up to 20 layers.

Why are some paintings so high up on the rock?

NCERT suggests that paintings near the ceiling or high on the rock were meant to be seen from a distance. Some shelters with paintings do not seem to have been living spaces and may have had religious importance.

How have the colours survived thousands of years?

The paints were ground from minerals such as haematite and mixed with water and a sticky binder. NCERT says they are believed to have lasted because of a chemical reaction with oxides on the rock surface.
