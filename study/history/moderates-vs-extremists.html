Moderates vs Extremists in the Congress: Leaders, Demands, Methods, the Partition of Bengal, Swadeshi and the 1907 Split

Congress founded 1885 • Moderate demands and petitions • Lal-Bal-Pal and 'politics of prayers' • Bengal partitioned 1905 • Swadeshi and boycott • Muslim League 1906 • Split 1907, reunion 1915

UPSC SSC State PSC 📅 Last updated: 2026-11-04

The Indian National Congress was founded when 72 delegates from all over the country met at Bombay in December 1885. NCERT notes that the need for an all-India body of educated Indians had been felt since 1880, and the Ilbert Bill controversy sharpened it. For its first twenty years the Congress was "moderate" in its aims and methods. From the 1890s a more radical group questioned that style, and the clash between the two came to a head after the partition of Bengal in 1905.

Exam papers call the two groups the Moderates and the Extremists. NCERT's Class VIII textbook calls the second group the Radicals. This page uses both names and follows the textbook's facts.

The early leaders were largely from Bombay and Calcutta: Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Badruddin Tyabji, W.C. Bonnerji, Surendranath Banerji, Romesh Chandra Dutt and S. Subramania Iyer, among others. Naoroji, a businessman and publicist settled in London, was for a time a member of the British Parliament. His book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India offered a scathing criticism of the economic impact of British rule.

Their political demands were:

  • more representative Legislative Councils with more power, and councils in provinces that had none;
  • Indians in high positions in government, with civil service examinations held in India as well as London. Senior posts were then monopolised by white officials, so this was also a stand against racism. Because British officers sent much of their large salaries home, Indianisation was expected to reduce the drain of wealth to England;
  • separation of the judiciary from the executive, repeal of the Arms Act, and freedom of speech and expression.

They also raised economic issues. They said British rule had caused poverty and famines: higher land revenue had impoverished peasants and zamindars, and grain exports to Europe had created food shortages. The Congress asked for lower revenue, a cut in military expenditure and more funds for irrigation, and passed resolutions on the salt tax, Indian labourers abroad and the sufferings of forest dwellers.

The Moderates wanted to build public awareness of the unjust nature of British rule. They published newspapers, wrote articles and showed how British rule was ruining the country economically. They criticised the government in speeches and sent representatives to different parts of India to mobilise opinion.

Behind this method lay a belief: the British respected the ideals of freedom and justice, so they would accept the just demands of Indians once they knew of them. The task, as the Moderates saw it, was to state those demands clearly and make the government aware of Indian feeling. The Radicals mocked it as the "politics of prayers".

By the 1890s many Indians had begun to question this style. In Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab, Bepin Chandra Pal, Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Lala Lajpat Rai explored more radical aims and methods. Exam guides group the three as Lal-Bal-Pal.

  • They criticised the Moderates for their "politics of prayers".
  • They stressed self-reliance and constructive work: people must rely on their own strength, not the "good" intentions of the government, and must fight for swaraj.
  • Tilak raised the slogan "Freedom is my birthright and I shall have it!" His Marathi newspaper Kesari became one of the strongest critics of British rule.
  • Lala Lajpat Rai, from Punjab, was a leading critic of the politics of petitions and an active member of the Arya Samaj.

In 1905, Viceroy Curzon partitioned Bengal. It was then the biggest province of British India and included Bihar and parts of Orissa. The British cited administrative convenience, but instead of removing the non-Bengali areas, the government separated East Bengal and merged it with Assam. NCERT suggests the main motives were to curtail the influence of Bengali politicians and to split the Bengali people.

The partition infuriated people across India, and both Moderates and Radicals opposed it. Large meetings and demonstrations were held and new methods of mass protest developed. The struggle became the Swadeshi movement, strongest in Bengal; in deltaic Andhra it was called the Vandemataram Movement.

Swadeshi aimed to oppose British rule and encourage self-help, swadeshi enterprise, national education and the use of Indian languages. The radicals pushed for mass mobilisation and boycott of British institutions and goods. Some individuals began to argue that "revolutionary violence" would be needed to overthrow British rule.

In 1906 a group of Muslim landlords and nawabs formed the All India Muslim League at Dacca. The League supported the partition and wanted separate electorates for Muslims, which the government conceded in 1909.

The Congress split in 1907. The Moderates opposed boycott because they felt it involved the use of force. After the split the Moderates dominated the Congress, while Tilak's followers worked from outside. The two groups reunited in December 1915, and the next year the Congress and the Muslim League signed the Lucknow Pact to work together for representative government.

Q1. Where and when was the Indian National Congress founded? easy

✅ Answer: (b) Bombay, December 1885

💡 Explanation: NCERT says 72 delegates from all over the country met at Bombay in December 1885 to establish the Congress.

Q2. Which of these was NOT a demand of the early (Moderate) Congress? medium

✅ Answer: (c) Boycott of British goods and institutions

💡 Explanation: Boycott was the Radicals' method. The Moderates opposed it, and that disagreement split the Congress in 1907.

Q3. Who raised the slogan 'Freedom is my birthright and I shall have it'? easy

✅ Answer: (d) Bal Gangadhar Tilak

💡 Explanation: NCERT credits the slogan to Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who also edited the Marathi newspaper Kesari.

Q4. In 1905, East Bengal was separated from Bengal and merged with which province? medium

✅ Answer: (a) Assam

💡 Explanation: NCERT notes that instead of removing the non-Bengali areas, the government separated East Bengal and merged it with Assam.

Q5. When did the two groups of the Congress reunite after the 1907 split? medium

✅ Answer: (b) December 1915

💡 Explanation: The Moderates and Tilak's followers reunited in December 1915; the Lucknow Pact with the Muslim League followed the next year.

Why does NCERT say 'Radicals' instead of 'Extremists'?

NCERT's Class VIII chapter calls the second group the Radicals. Exam papers and many guides call them the Extremists. Both names mean the same group: Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bepin Chandra Pal and their followers.

Why did the Congress split in 1907?

The Moderates opposed boycott because they felt it involved the use of force. After the split the Moderates controlled the Congress and Tilak's followers worked from outside until the two groups reunited in December 1915.

Did the Moderates only speak for the educated elite?

No. NCERT points out that the early Congress also passed resolutions on the salt tax, Indian labourers abroad, forest dwellers and peasant revenue, so it did not speak only for professionals, zamindars or industrialists.
